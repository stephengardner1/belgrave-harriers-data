Editorial · page 3

EDITORIAL

In February last we commented on the "News of the World's" decision to reprieve the National London - Brighton relay race and cancel the Southern Clubs "Brighton" instead. This decision was well received by the majority of people but two questions remained unanswered. Firstly,how will the ten southern clubs for the National event be selected ? Secondly,what will replace the premier autumnal relay ?

Those questions were uppermost in the minds of most relay enthusiasts - not least those of Belgrave Harriers' officers. This Club has always regarded its object as the encouragement of amateur athletics (see Rule 4) and there was unanimity amongst the Committee that we should make every effort to plug this gap in the athletics calendar.

After considerable lobbying it was found that the vast majority of Southern Clubs would welcome a subsitute event providing it was of sufficient stature. On the strength of this Belgrave Harriers have decided to promote an Invitation Road Relay on Saturday,October 9th,from Belgrave Hall,Wimbledon. It is a tribute to our reputation in the Athletics world for efficient organisation that,of the twenty-five clubs invited, twenty-four promptly accepted;and we are indeed honoured that they should endorse the view that our proposals were the best of many put up at the time.

Twenty-five clubs will compete,each fielding teams of 12. There will be two laps - a "red"circuit and a "green" circuit;the former will be 5½miles and the latter 3 miles. Each will be run alternately,giving a race distance of 51 miles. The race commences at 12 noon and all Belgravians (whatever their sectional interests) are asked to come along and support the inaugural race of an event certain to become a red-letter day in the athletics calendar.

Our Annual promotions are now as follows:- Open Field Events Meeting (March) Open 20 Miles Road Race (July) Invitation Road Relay (October) Open Youths Road Relay (October) Open 7 Miles Walk (November).

Hon Sec's Notes

A.A.H.

Our Headquarters at Wimbledon now falls within the new Greater London Council area of the London Borough of Merton; and details of our Club and activities have been passed to the new Council who will make them available at Public Libraries etc.

Our association with Wimbledon now goes back a very long way; the Club first using cross-country courses in the vicinity as far back as 1911-12.

* * *

We view with very great interest the road construction work which is imminent at Tibbett's Corner and thereabouts; and are hopeful that there will be no great interference with our running and walking road courses. Everywhere, particularly in urban and suburban areas the menace of increased traffic accompanied by the demands for more and more roads makes life difficult for the athlete using the roads and for Clubs responsible for promotion of road events.

Some of us recall when our 5, 7 and 10 miles Cross-Country Championships were held over courses which involved the crossing of the main road and entering Robin Hood Gate into Richmond Park to run round Spanker's Hill Copse and Pen Ponds - but wider roads and increased traffic ended all that.

Our Past-President, Syd Roberts and former holder of a number of offices in the Club extending over many years, has recently had an extremely severe illness. Fortunately he is now home again and progressing well and after a period of convalescence should be back with us again. We wish him a speedy return to normal health.

* * *

The change of August Bank Holiday to the end of the month has given rise to some fixture alterations and members should amend their Summer fixture list accordingly. The London Fire Brigade Meeting will take place on September 18th and this programme will include two A.A.A Relay Championships as usual; and a special Relays Programme with several newly-instituted A.A.A Championship relay events will be held on August 28th at Hurlingham Track.

* * *

We regret that our German friends from TURNVEREIN 1862, LANGEN were not able to confirm acceptance of our invitation to come to London this year.

* * *

The Track season is well on its way and we shall soon be back in force at Wimbledon once again retracing our courses on and around the Common. It is now the time to resolve to have a set of place medals - or even winning - in the E.C.C.U. Championship next March. We have the men - all we need is the will and determination and just a little bit of luck in the way of health and injuries.

* * *

We once again acknowledge the generosity of Vice President E.T. RYAN in providing a further Shield - the Belgrave Shield - for competition in the cross-country Championships promoted by the South London School Association. Thus all three groups into which schoolboys are now divided have a trophy thro' the Belgrave Harriers, at the expense of Eddie Ryan.

* * *

For many years past we have conducted a Football Competition Scheme which has gone a long way towards providing us with amenities well above average among athletic clubs; and enabled us to participate in practically every Championship and major events held, to a greater extent than any other club.

In recent years with the retirement of some of our older members and changing circumstances; interest has waned somewhat in our money-raising activities and we now seek to fan it into flame again. We ask that every senior member should take a personal interest himself, and in addition that those able to do so should enlist the aid of their friends to take part in what is generally accorded to be one of the most generous competitions of its kind. Our Hon. Treasurer will gladly give further information - lets have our Supporter's Scheme back where it was years ago.

* * *

Our Belgrave Road Relay over twelve stages to be held at Wimbledon on October, 9th promises to be most successful, and twenty five clubs - the maximum permissible - have accepted invitations comprising the best in the South. Whilst the glamour associated with the Brighton Road will be missing- nevertheless the racing will be as keen as ever.

* * *

To economise on postage,etc.,the Notice of Annual General Meeting is included in this issue of the Belgravian on page 5,and no other notice will be circulated.

It is hoped that all members will make an effort to attend.

A.A.H.

BELS 6th in NATIONAL C.C.

Parliament Hill-March 6th

Although 6th is certainly no disgrace in an event of such stature, Belgravians were generally disappointed with a team which had promised so much. The margin between glory and mediocrity in these championships becomes increasingly narrower as the years go by. The smallest slip and half a dozen places are lost. Unfortunatley Geoff North and Trevor Hart, who had been showing great form in the preceding weeks, both dropped out. To lose two such runners and still finish 6th was a tribute to the rest of the team.

Gerry North once again made the international team but this time had his supporters' hearts in their mouths. After one lap he was still in the twenties and although he began to make ground on the second circuit it was a slow and painful business against such opposition as Adcocks and Kilby (Coventry), Rose, Wheeler and Wyatt (New Zealand), Tulloh, Roberts and Barlow. With one lap to go it was fairly certain that the first 9 were decided; as these included two New Zealanders the next two Englishmen would make the "International". With two miles left Gerry was on his own with Tulloh and Roberts fighting it out 50yds ahead and Mike Wiggs moving up very fast through the field behind. The runners were next seen with half a mile to go. North had put in a tremendous effort over the back of the course to catch and pass Tulloh and Roberts and was now 30yds clear. However, the race for 10th place was by no means over and with 600yds to go Wiggs also caught the Pompey/SLH pair---"picking them up" in a long sprint for home. Gerry's 30yds had dwindled to five by the time the final straight was reached but after a desperate final effort he held off Wiggs by 1 second, Tulloh by 5 seconds and Roberts by 7seconds---quite a sprint!

Dabbs and Fairclough had good runs and were very consistent; Kerr and O'Hara made considerable ground in the closing stages and John Thresher showed great courage in fighting on, despite losing 40-odd places in the final lap.

If the experience of this occasion is put to good use and our team can keep free of illness and injury then Belgrave stand every chance of being amongst the medals at the next "National".

BELS lose title at ILFORD

ROAD RELAYS

Ilford Road Relay--March 13th

Unfortunately this race clashed with the Southampton/Bournemouth Relay which nowadays attracts a better class field. We therefore sent a strong team of our best six runners (too strong as it happened!) to Southampton and endeavoured to win the Ilford trophy for the third successive year with our next best 10 men. It almost worked. Many "ifs" come to mind, particularly those affecting Alan Black (unable to get away from Loughborough), John Bicourt(Junior Cross Country Race) and Pat Newell (shift working), but it is a lot to hope for--getting our top 16 men out in one day--and the question still remains "which fixture should have had the top priority?".

The New Zealand cross-country team competed as guests and "won" by 7 minutes, but can be discounted as far as race description is concerned.

Laurie O'Hara gave us a great start by winning the first stage from Dellar(Camb. H) by 1 second--the third man, King (Essex Beagles), being over half a minute behind. O'Hara thus became only the 4th Belgravian to break 18 mins round the course.

Mike Shingles, who had been below his best for several weeks, was unable to break through the spell and dropped us to 4th on the next stage- a position maintained by Jack Brown on the 3rd leg, and who regained much lost ground in closing up to within 13secs of the runner ahead. Mitcham were in the lead at this point, 41secs ahead of Cambridge H and 53secs on Romford.

Charlie Walker had not had a good winter season, and although he gave of his utmost on stage 4 it was nowhere near his old best, and he handed over to Jackie Hampshire a full minute behind 3rd place and 2 mins 11secs behind the new leaders--Cambridge H. Jackie must have picked up Charlie's trouble at the hand-over for he also was nowhere near his best and dropped us even further behind, in time (3:02), and position 8th.

The revival commenced on Stage 6 with Mel Thorpe. Displaying scintillating form he scorched round the lap to take nearly a minute off the Cambridge man and move us up to 5th place. At this point Baldwin took Blackheath into the lead--a position they held to the end. Bill Kerr tried to continue the good work but, for him, his run was not spectacular. John Dear passed Mitcham on stage 8 but had to allow Poly to pass. We were still 4th, 1½mins behind the second team and 28secs behind the third--Blackheath were certain winners and the race was on for second place.

The penultimate leg was taken by John Moore, another to have had a below-form winter, and although he recorded a mediocre time he took us past Polytechnic into 3rd place only 46seconds behind Cambridge Harriers.

Everything depended on Don Maclean over the last stage and no-one envied him his task--Maynard of Cambridge H. 46seconds ahead and Malcolm Browne, the Essex Beagle miler, just 20seconds behind. Don made a great fight of it, and although Browne went right through to 2nd place and Belgrave had to be content with 4th position, Mac was fast closing on the Cambridge man at the finish, ending up only 13secs behind.

POMPEY ARE THRASHED

Great Win in Southampton/Bournemouth Relay

Belgrave gained sweet revenge for their defeat last year by Portsmouth, by smashing the Hampshire club's course record by no less than 2½ minutes and beating them by 2 minutes 40seconds into the bargain.

Of our six runners, three broke stage records and none was outside the fastest two for a leg. This fixture is growing in popularity and it was a pity it clashed with the Ilford Relay, for this team with our best six at Ilford would have run the New Zealanders very close in that race.

John Thresher shadowed Gibson (Surrey AC) for most of the first stage but let him get away in the closing stages by a six second margin. John was still 54seconds inside the stage record though! Trevor Hart went off like a man possessed and put Belgrave in a commanding lead--a lead they opened up more and more over the next three legs. Gerry North was in grand form in stage 3, increasing our lead to 2 minutes and adding another record to his collection.

Charlie Dabbs was first off into the second half of the race and although he was 8 seconds slower than Lonnen(Bristol) he was only 10secs away from Martin Hyman's stage record. The Bels really had their tails up now and Geoff North collected a 3rd stage record for the North family (Gerry holds two), and thrashed Dave Cooke of Pompy (2nd fastest) by no less than 45seconds, giving Tony Fairclough an invincible lead of 3:22 for the final stage. John Cooke reversed the treatment his brother received on the previous stage but it had little effect.

This was undoubtedly the most decisive relay win we have had for years and augered well for the forthcoming "Brighton".

PIPPED AT BEXLEY

Cambridge H. Relay--March 20th

After our fine double outing of the previous week, at Ilford and Southampton, when we had 16 'A' team runners performing, there should have been little difficulty in getting 6 of our best dozen runners out at Bexley and thus clinch another convincing win. But it was not to be. Only 4 of our expected six appeared and Jack Brown and Don Maclean were moved up to fill the gap. Both ran very well, but we were obliged to concede 53 seconds to Blackheath at the end and had to be content with 3rd place. This was another case of key runners not notifying the team manager when unable to appear.

Laurie O'Hara gave the opposition another dose of his 'Ilford' treatment on the first stage and romped home with a 5 seconds lead over a surprised Roseman(SLH). John Thresher increased the lead to 17secs but Trevor Hart met Baldwin(B/Heath) in record breaking form and converted this to a deficit of 32secs. Jack Brown held second place but Blackheath doubled their lead and SLH were moving up close behind. Geoff. North put us bang into the running again by equalling Baldwin's new course record and regaining the lead by a 10seconds margin.

Poor Don Maclean. For the second successive week he had the unenviable task of being our last man with the race in a precarious state. Mac made a tremendous effort to stay with Herring of Blackheath but was dropped after 1½miles. Second place seemed assured, but a storming finish by Avila(SLH) took his club into second place a few yards before the line and a possible Belgrave win had become a 3rd place.

April 24th 1965

Two teams (less one last minute defaulter) journeyed by road to Blackpool on the day before the race and were accomodated in North Shore boarding establishments.

On the second stage Gerry North opened the gap to about fifty seconds and John Dear held on to 3rd place for the B team.

Trevor Hart ran the third stage for the A team and widened the gap over the Manchester club to about 80 seconds but Bolton United who had had a bad start had moved into 3rd place with a fast run by Freary who with 21:53 put up a new record for the course. The B team, with an unfit Peter Hilliar running, were falling back.

Tony Fairclough took the fourth leg and kept the lead but Brian Hall of Manchester closed the gap to about thirty seconds and the Lads Club were getting confident of their chances.

After the race the boys (and the team manager) set out to enjoy themselves and were able to show the North brothers a thing or two about Blackpool which even they didn't know! In all a successful weekend for all concerned.

Brighton Relay

OLYMPIAN

April 10th 1965

Our selected team for this race was probably the strongest that we had ever fielded and on this fact alone we were justified in hoping that we would finish higher than our fourth place in 1964. Unfortunately we found our main opposition at the top of their form with one team, North Staffs & Stone, running far ahead of their previous best. Our finishing position of fifth was by no means a disgrace, and was in fact, broadly speaking, a better performance than in 1964,for we were only 2½ minutes behind the winners instead of over 6 minutes. This means that whilst we are improving, teams like Coventry and Derby may well have passed their peak and we can look forward to 1966 with great anticipation.

Laurie O'Hara on stage 1 did all that we had expected of him--he gave us the lead we needed and sent off John Thresher on what we had hoped would have produced a good margin by the time Mitcham was reached. Stage 2 has, however, given John a complex--although he is probably our third best runner over five or six miles he wont believe it himself and his performances suffer in consequence. Instead of romping away from the opposition he was forced to drop back to fifth and we were 30 seconds in arrears when Pat Newell took over heading for Wallington. Pat also lost a little ground with a run somewhat below that which we had expected after his excellent effort in the club 5¾.

Gerry North running on stage 4, one of the hardest of the whole race, was in devastating form. From seventh to second against strong runners was a first class performance. To take 37 seconds off John Cooke of Portsmouth and to record the fastest time of the day, even with Ron Hill on the leg, was better than even his most ardent supporters would have expected.

Trevor Hart ran well on the Redhill stage and kept his place, although losing a little ground on Coventry, and when Jack Brown (40 this year!) took over on the short leg Coventry were only 37 seconds ahead. Jack reduced this lead to 22 seconds and sent off Mel Thorpe with every chance of going into the lead. Mel made no mistake, and producing almost the carbon copy of an earlier race when Laurie O'Hara took this stage, came home with fifteen seconds to spare.

Geoff North came next on the most gruelling leg of them all and fought every inch of the way, but Dominic Keily of Derby and Dick Taylor of Coventry were in great form and at Handcross we were back to third, but only ten seconds behind Derby and two behind Coventry.

Could Bill Kerr keep close enough to Kilby and Bullivant to give our last three runners a chance or would these top class runners open up a large gap? The latter ,not unexpectedly, proved to be the case and John Jackson, running for North Staffs, caught up forty seconds and also put a long distance between himself and Bill.

At the start of stage 10 we were fourth and nearly two minutes down on Coventry who were only a short distance ahead of Derby. Charlie Dabbs obviously thought there was still hope of a place for he gained sixteen seconds on North Staffs and 22 seconds on Coventry, although losing a few yards to Derby. The gaps behind were closing. Portsmouth who had been behind us for some time were getting ominously near, for Meadows on this leg put up the best time of the day,and were at this point a mere nineteen seconds behind us.

Tony Fairclough, who had run so well on the Dale Hill stage last October, was well below his usual form and instead of repeating his previous effort he lost much ground and a place to Tim Johnston of Portsmouth who proved to be his club's ace card.

And so to the last leg which Alan Black was taking. We were not really worried about being 30 seconds down on Portsmouth for we were sure that Alan would catch Gough. This he did and Alan was with the Portsmouth runner with a mile to go. Knowing Alan's fast finish we were still confident but Gough hung on and in a desperate effort, which some athletes seem to be able to produce even when they are on their knees, came home by inches from a most surprised Black. In front another battle had been enacted between Coventry and Derby--Coventry turning a small deficit into a winning lead of fourteen seconds to win for the third year in succession after a race which had proved to be one of the best contested for many years.

Feature · page 15

HOW I TRAINED

Jack Rutland

We continue our series of training articles from Belgrave Newsletters of the early 1950's

(3) JACK RUTLAND

I started walking at the age of 15 years by winning the Bels one mile Novice event. It was not until two years later,however,that I was encouraged to enter 7 mile events.

Whilst perhaps specialising in 7/10 mile events,at which reasonable success was acheived,first class standards have been gained at 20 miles and 50 kilometres, and I decided to make 1950 a long distance year.

I believe that walkers can only expect good times by attaining real physical fitness;it is no good"taking a bashing" if you are not up to it. Therefore whether stripped training or strolling,make it regular and recover from one outing before attempting another.

Improved times must be shown if physical fitness and style can be developed and from this should come stamina and speed respectively. As to the right amount of training,this will depend on the individual;I would say two or three times a week is sufficient,thus:-

SHORT DISTANCE. Two mid-week outings and of course Saturdays club or race turnout.The distances mid-week can be restricted to 5/7 miles with time checks if possible. Strolling 10 miles on Sunday provided Saturday's outing has not been a hard race.

LONG DISTANCE. Plenty of stripped spins 20/25 miles,with occasional"sharpeners" of 15/10 miles. Recovery is essential from each before attempting the next. Strolling once a week of 5 hours duration.

I have found that stripped spins can score over strolls mainly because of time factors;more mileage can be covered to so build up confidence and knowledge of pace which is essential.

One certainly needs to put in quite a lot of work for distance events, but providing a little is made,most walkers should be able to do some distance work and may find an increased strength the following season.

Jack Rutland first made his presence felt by winning a club novice race at the age of 15 in the remarkably good time of 8:35. With increasing years he improved and was a member of our winning Junior team in 1936, finishing 7th. Following this he scored in many of our winning scratch teams in pre-war days. He won the first Army Road Walk Championship and in the season 1945/46 won a succession of open races in exemplary fashion. Thereafter he was Club Champion at 10 miles;Southern 7 miles Champion; second in the initial R.W.A. 10 mile Championship and placed in County Championships. Jack was among the six "possibles" for the Olympic Games 10,000 metres event in 1948 but did not strike his best form in that year.

Feature · page 20

A DIRTY WORD !

Clive Shippen

.......this is the reaction "professionalism" invariably gets. Are there too many heads firmly embedded in the sand? Are our interpretations of"amateur" and"professional" outmoded? Have we got our priorities wrong? ......Clive Shippen asks these questions and reviews the present illogical situation.

The word AMATEUR is of french origin and stems from the latin words amator (lover) and amore (love). The professional sportsman,then,can be an amateur in spirit just as easily as the amateur can be a professional in approach.

When some of the rowing clubs were first formed on the Thames, membership was restricted to "gentlemen" — to the exclusion of artisans. However one may view this sociologically, it was a fact that these "gentlemen" desired exercise with friends of similar physique and of similar occupation. A true amateur was one who gained no physical advantage from his daily occupation.

If we leap across the years to the present day we find there is no restriction on an athlete who has a job,say,which necessitates a perfect shot-putting action being carried out fifty times a day;nor an athlete who has all day and every day to train while he is kept by his parents. Few people would argue that there should be any discrimination against these athletes - indeed,against any athlete - for the majority prefer to see the most able succeed,irrespective of how they obtained their prowess. It seems quite illogical therefore,that an outside and irrelevant factor should be introduced - "money",or in its broadest sense,"material gain".

Not irrelevant? Then of what possible benefit could Arthur Rowe's rugby league football be to his shot-putting? Or Berwyn Jones's to his sprinting? Their participation in this tough game was a positive disadvantage to their athletics,with a fair risk of injury thrown in. As for the money they earned,well even if they used this to match the advantage of the "kept" amateur it would be to no avail,for they had already blackened their name with that dirty word "professional". So it is no longer a case of fair play,but a question of principle — just another rule,but with no evidence to support its existence.

Let us suppose that monetary gain was not frowned upon and that sportsmen were judged purely on physical ability. Would Snell,Halberg,Clark and Co. start doing an extra 100 miles a week and go twice round the world each year instead of once? Would Eastern European athletes show vast improvements if they were paid broken time for all of the year? And would those swimmers who train six hours a day while their parents earn their keep suddenly acquire improved training schedules? Of course not. The attitude of leading athletes to material gain is purely incidental. If there is something to be had they'll have it - if not they'll train just as hard for the next race. Athletics is too tough a sport to enter just for the pickings - if any.

If professionalism was given official blessing the most noticeable effect would be in the advertising field,as there is no money in athletic promotions. Our most famous athletes would be seen eating,drinking,wearing or using all manner of things. A few Olympic Gold Medallists might even be able to live on the revenue - as long as they stayed good enough. Athletic "purists" and opponents of material gain would be horrified to see such goings-on,but would it really do our sport any harm? Have the cycling and table-tennis worlds suffered? Surely the publicity would be beneficial , possibly resulting in higher attendances at the White City. Opponents of such licence should remember that for every athlete who thinks he can hold promoters,selectors or officials to ransom there are a hundred who would willingly pay to take his place. There are a handful of world famous names in any era whose crowd-pulling ability makes their owners potentially well off,but many a blind eye has already been turned towards the activities of some of these athletes in this so-called amateur age.

Athletics administrators in all non-communist countries are,of course in a difficult position. To acknowledge even semi-professionalism (as cyclists have) would cut their country off from world competition until the others followed suit. No one,therefore,has the courage to take the lead in suggesting a revision to the rules. No one can be blamed, for such a suggestion would meet with violent opposition from the communist bloc,who do not recognise professionalism in any form,in theory; besides many other Western countries.

Nationalism is already proving a far greater evil than professionalism Athletics enthusiasts form only a minute percentage of the multitude who eagerly await the gold medal tally at an Olympic Games. World interest is mainly concerned with National prestige and the resultant political propaganda and it is Nation versus Nation rather than athlete versus athlete. France is embarking on a mammoth programme to improve sports facilities, not because her sportsmen need them any more than before but because de Gaulle wasn't satisfied with the few medals gleaned at the last Olympics. China,in turn,will probably enter the Olympics when they consider their athletes (like their table-tennis players) are strong enough to beat the world. So the shades that appeared at the 1936 Olympics are continuing to darken.

Rather than witch-hunt for technical "pro's" our administrators would do better to campaign for the abolishment of victory ceremonies and their nauseating crowd-chanting, flag-raising, anthem-singing embellishments; and make any other moves which will help to reduce the Nationalistic atmosphere of the Olympic Games.

However, long after the Olympic Games has heaved its final death-throes and the amateur/professional wrangle is long forgotten,club athletics will still be pottering along very much in its present style. Scrubbers will still be wanting to run against scrubbers and stars against stars. Championships will still be held and life will continue to revolve round the domestic calendar of fixtures - and the only persons to have fallen by the wayside will be those who have forgotten that athletics is purely and simply contests of athletic ability between sportsmen who have not the slightest concern about the colour,class,creed or material gain of their opponents.

CLUB CHAMPIONSHIPS

Chris Steer

May 22nd - Hurlingham

The Club Championships were held on a cloudy but warm day which contrasts with the arctic conditions usually experienced at this meeting. Like the weather the standard of performances was not particularly high,often downright poor,although there were some occasional'bright periods' .

Not surprisingly for Belgrave (how often must this have been said?) most of the better performances came in the Mile and Half,the only events where,it would seem,it would seem,there was any true depth. In the sprints and field events,although there were some excellent winning efforts,the overall standard was in many cases abysmally poor. An all time low must have been reached in the Senior 220 yards where only the winner could be said to have any real claims to being a sprinter. Time and again the achievments of the second and third places would have been a disgrace to a schoolboy meeting.

However there were some performances worthy of note. One of the revelations was the casual ease with which Nick Head broke the Javelin record. From a run-up of a few yards he threw 207'7¾" - almost 20 feet further than Colin Clair's 1963 record. He also won the Shot and Discus with more modest marks. Another who demonstrated remarkable versatility was Clive Brooks who won the High Jump,Triple Jump,100 yards and 220 yards (another Gerry Leroy?). Completing the field events picture,Bob Jones jumped within 6" of the Long Jump record with 22'6½" .

On the track the best race of the day was undoubtedly the 880 yards. It was a somewhat unusual line-up for a half - it would seem that certain middle distance runners can't bear to be separated from each other.Nevertheless, a fine race ensued with John Thresher making most of the running only to be outsprinted down the straight by(suprise! suprise!) Geoff North. The time of 1:56.3 broke Dave Cocks' record by one tenth of a second. John was second in the same time and Alan Morrison,showing a startling and welcome return to form,came third with 1:58.0 . The Mile provided some good performances but no-one could match Laurie O'hara's devastating finish. His time was an excellent 4:16.2 . Mel Thorpe (in great form these days) was second in 4:19.8 and Bill Kerr third in 4:20.2. The 3000m Steeplechase (a new championship event which should become more popular) proved an easy victory for John Bicourt whose experience at this type of race was too much for John Dear who had led in the early stages . The other senior track event,the 440 yards, was won by Alan Mead in 53.2 from a very sparse field.

In the Junior events,Adrian Major found the 880/mile double too much for him - he won the mile in 4:47.0 but was beaten in the half by Richard Moody who recorded 2:07.3 . The junior sprints,unlike the senior were well supported and performances were good. Ben Lulham's 'stable' was well to the forefront in the absence of Jim Vivian (appendicitis) both 100y and 220y were won by Chris Martin with Denis Gleeson and C.Oliver second and third in both races. Other junior winners were R.Nelson (440y) and J.Martin (High Jump). In the Boys events winners were A.Bent (100y) and J.Lewis (880y).

Generally then,a disappointing meeting especially for the handfull of spectators who did take the trouble to come. I may be biased,but it is time Belgrave stopped relying on the middle distance runners to provide all the excitement. However well they may run on the Brighton Road,they cannot win trophy meetings and the like. Perhaps with a solid nucleus of good junior sprinters and the few good field events men we do have,these Championships will be more worthy of the name of Belgrave in future years.Here's hoping.

Chris Steer

FIREWORKS! ROCKETS! MEXICO!

FIREWORKS! ROCKETS! MEXICO!-----"What is the connection"? you might ask. Well, on November 5th those North rockets, brothers Gerry and Geoff, will have their blue touch-papers lit at London Airport, will rise to about 20,000 feet, head W.S.W., and fall only a little over half that height to the land of the 1968 Olympic Games. Gerry and Geoff form part of a six man team of runners (others so far are M. Hyman, J. Cooke and D. Keily) who will be under the surveillance of Drs. Pugh and Owen and Chief Coach John Le Masurier during a one month's training programme in the rarefied atmosphere of Mexico. The trip has been orgainised by the British Olympic Association in order to ascertain the effects of altitude on the performances of distance runners and what sort of acclimatisation period is neccessary.
