Editorial · page 3

EDITORIAL

It was sad news to all athletics enthusiasts to hear that the era of London to Brighton road relay races has ended. The financial burden of sponsorship has obliged the NEWS OF THE WORLD to withdraw their support from all road-relay races only 12 months after discontinuing the 'Southern' event.

As everyone knows, the April event was widely regarded as the "Cup-Final" of inter-club road racing in Britain, and we are grateful that the NEWS OF THE WORLD should have supported it so well over the past 40 years. The support by this Sunday newspaper has been, and still is, extensive in the field of athletics. In return, it is probably fair to say that most athletes have "supported" the paper itself.

But is it a proper state of affairs that a sport which is the principal one at the Olympic Games should have to rely on such support? Is it generally realised how many White City meetings or cross-country championships would disappear or barely survive but for the help of sponsors? Is it also appreciated just how many clubs are unable to participate in the National Championships when they are held in the extreme North or South of the country? Perhaps it would be an eye-opener for the Minister of Sport (who has probably never heard of the London to Brighton Road Relay) if all sponsorships were suddenly withdrawn and the White City National and Inter-Counties meetings abandoned---but it is doubtful, for few people outside the athletics world would seem to appreciate what happens in between Olympic Games and four-minute miles. Yet is the case of Athletics really any different from that of the Arts? Government sponsorship of the National Arts Theatre is accepted, why not also support for the National championships of road, track and cross-country?

These are some of the thoughts that come to mind when the efforts of hard-working amateur officials receive a set-back of this sort. Every endeavour will be made to find someone willing to sponsor a National London to Brighton Relay on a more modest scale than usual and with the clubs contributing perhaps a third of the cost, but there is virtually no hope of salvaging the 1966 event.

For those members who consider these problems rather out of their province, we would remind them that this Club would cease to exist if we were obliged to rely solely on subscriptions---even if everyone did pay up regularly each September---and we would ask athletes everywhere the heart-searching question "DO WE PAY ENOUGH FOR OUR SPORT?"

Obituary · page 4

Edwin Gordon

A.A.H.

It is with sorrow that we have to advise the death of Ted Gordon, in his 89th year; a Past President and Trustee of the Club and a vital personality in the growth of the Belgrave Harriers.

Teddy joined the Club as far back as September 1908 and old minute books indicate that he quickly played his part on the Club Committee and worked solidly for the developement and welfare of the Club in those early days. After the first World War he was one of the workers who assisted in the re-birth of the Club and its gradual growth to National honours and renown; and in this he received every assitance from Mrs Gordon.

He became President to W.H.Hare and his lively personality permeated on Club life; and whilst he supported all our Club activities, he claimed and earned for himself a very special place as Chairman of our Building Fund Committee. His energies, assisted by the efforts of many others, were applied to our rather small but cherished Headquarters Fund, and the hundreds of pounds grew into thousands and ultimately gave us the Headquarters we have today.

Many of us well remember Teddy turning out regularly in sprints and veteran events. We remember him as link-man in his car on London-Brighton Relay races, ready to dash forward with our runner should traffic so dictate, for in those days we hired a double-decker bus which was our combined dressing room and grandstand. We remember Mr and Mrs Gordon setting out at 4 am on Brighton Stroll nights to overtake the strollers and prepare breakfast ahead, to be partaken at the roadside. We recall Teddy as Chairman of a Smoking Concert for the Building Fund and at other Social affairs, and a worker in so many ways for Belgrave; and we shall treasure his memory as one of thr truly great Belgravians to have gone before.

Teddy had a great interest in the Camping movement and was President of one of the Area organisations; and later became a Councillor and Deputy-Mayor of Malden and Coombe. During his year in this latter office and as he was about to become Mayor, Mrs Gordon, who had rendered sterling support throughout to her husband, died suddenly, and Teddy decided in his distress to retire from Civic duties and to live in the West Country. For a while he served as a Trustee to the Gloucestershire AAA; and then moved on to North Devon where he kept himself remarkably well informed on Athletic and particularly Belgravian matters.

We are indeed grateful for Teddy's near-sixty years of membership, and all he did for us - the evidence of which still abounds and endures.

A.A.H.

HON SEC'S NOTES

We are Surrey cross-country champions once again - the forerunner of other championship wins, we hope - and it seems incredible that our last win should have been in 1952. Granted that Surrey has more than its rightful share of strong cross-country clubs it still seems remarkable that we should wait so long in recent years for a title that once came to us with some regularity.

* * *

Our first win came in 1925, and great was the joy at "The Kings Head" Epsom when the cup circulated, primed by Harry Hare who had managed to find two winning horses on the same afternoon! Our team was Ken O'Kell(3), Mick Pyer(4), Bert Footer(8), Jack Andrews (17), Bert Smith(21), Harry Bruce(26). Our 79 points gave us a two point win over Epsom H, with Surrey AC third.

* * *

For the first time Belgrave has a Vice-President- or rather two - on the Southern Counties AAA. They are LES COHEN and ALF HARLEY and we wish them well in these new appointments which reflect honour on the Club as well as the individuals.

* * *

His many friends will be glad to know that Joe Scamell is making a good recovery after serious respiratory trouble. Joe joined us in 1920 and has continued to show his walking paces until as recently as a few months ago. Our best wishes go to Joe for a complete and speedy recovery.

25 YEARS AGO.... and 40 YEARS AGO

25 YEARS AGO....

From the Belgravian 1941: "For the purposes of inter-club matches etc. we have arranged to combine with the Polytechnic Harriers. There has always been a very friendly feeling in existence between our two clubs and in difficult times it is natural that we should turn to one another for mutual help."

Because of the war the usual racing programme was severely restricted. The Southern Counties C.C.A. staged three races in the November 1940/ February 1941 period and Tom Carter won every one. Three other fixtures were held during this period and Tom won each of these also - including a convincing win over the great Sydney Wooderson.

....and 40 YEARS AGO

BOYS: Our team this year has been going from strength to strength. Raymond Young has emerged as the leading runner in this age group but only just. BICOURT LEADS JUNIORS TO SURPRISE 2nd PLACE IN SURREY C.C.

January 1st 1966 - Wimbledon Common

With the retirement from athletics of Colin Church (Walton AC) at the ripe old age of 18, all the local papers tipped Steve Badgery (Mitcham) for an easy win, although our own John Bicourt had beaten both these fine runners in the previous North Surrey League race.

From the start the Mitcham boy and John were well to the fore and after a mile were 30 yards clear of the field. As they climbed the hill from Queensmere for the first time John, his Alan Simpson type chest held high, looked far the stronger and showed just ahead of Badgery who seemed to be feeling the pace. Along the horseride and turning out again for the second lap, John kept piling on the pressure and it was soon obvious that nobody would beat him on this day. 'Badge' kept fighting gamely but a gap was opening up and he eventually had to give best by 12 seconds.

Meanwhile, Adrian Major was having a battle royal with the rest of Mitcham's scoring team and after running one of his best races to date,he finished a very distinguished 5th. Great backing by Derek Hurd (who seems to be finding his old form) and quarter-miler Philip Smith (he could never have dreamed of winning his first County medal in a cross country race) ensured us of a suprise 2nd place in the team event.

Last year we predicted that John Bicourt's forward running could well earn him the title at his next attempt, so let's stick our necks out once more and forecast a win for Adrian Major in 1967.

RESULT:

Teams:- 1.Mitcham AC 18 pts 2.BELGRAVE H 56 pts 3.HHH 59 pts.

Due to New Year's Eve festivities the night before, we were unable to close in a Youth team. The Race was won by J.Rix (SLH) in 18:36. Belgrave positions were R.Moody 47th (22:10), A.Brooks 56th (22:57) and P.Gee 57th (22:57).

SOUTHERN C.C.CHAMPS.

Olympian

Parliament Hill Fields - February 12th

Youths: This was the first race of the afternoon and we were not expecting a great deal from our team this year as they are young and inexperienced in races of this kind. This was borne out by the result for we finished last of the teams that closed in with the large score of 751 points. RESULT: 1.Mitcham AC 72pts 2.Windsor & Eton 105pts 3.TVH 125pts 31.BELGRAVE H 751pts.

Juniors: As usual in the major championships the Juniors have the worst of the conditions after hundreds of Youths and Seniors have churned up the course. This year it was worse than usual and only the fittest could hope to run to form.

Our Junior team had been at Parliament Hill almost three hours before their race was due to start but nevertheless the majority of them were only just in the starting pens before the maroon was fired, despite a very adequate five minutes warning. Adrian Major, of whom we expected a good performance was left behind and had the invidious task of chasing the field whereas he would normaly have been contesting the lead. It is to be hoped that this experience will have taught him a hard earned lesson.

John Bicourt was soon prominent and at the end of the first lap was lying 9th although a fair distance behind the leaders. Adrian Major,despite his belated start, had moved through to 22nd but was showing signs of his extra exertions. James Rimmer and a new member,Michael Baker,were our other scorers in 100th and 133rd places respectively.

Little change took place on the second lap. John Bicourt moved up to take a very respectable 7th place again beating his local rival Steve Badgery from Mitcham. Adrian made no further progress and finished 24th lacking the necessary finishing fire to take him past the six runners all within striking distance. Without doubt he would have been much higher if his start had been normal. Rimmer and Baker came in 105th and 131st to make a team score of 267 points which gave us 7th place.

MEXICAN TRACK DANCE

Geoff and Gerry North

Geoff and Gerry North write of their trip to Mexico as part of the team investigating the effects of high altitude competition; with the 1968 Olympics in mind.

(Photos: Monitor Press Features Ltd.) GERRY GEOFF

We arrived in Mexico City on November 7th 1965 accompanied by Martin Hyman and John Cooke (Portsmouth), Dominic Keily (Derby) and Mike Turner (Liverpool) together with John Le Masurier, the team coach, and Doctors Pugh and Owen. Prior to embarking on the trip we had taken part in a programme of physical fitness tests and racing that enabled direct comparison of results to be made.

Mexico struck us immediately as a beautiful country, with captivating scenery and exciting little market towns, whilst Mexico City itself was very modern with buildings of striking architectural merit. Whilst there we paid a visit to a bull-fight at the 80,000 capacity stadium - the largest bull ring in the world. The Mexicans have a very cheerful outlook on life and we found them easy to get on with. We stuck to English food throughout and took regular doses of "Streptoriad" tablets - these safeguards kept all members of the team free from stomach ailments. Our hotel was well away from the main traffic and was in a pleasant and relaxing situation. We made several trips to outlying places during the days and in the evenings we found many other places of interest ! The beer was very good and the people always very friendly - particularly the senoritas !

The first few days of training were most uncomfortable. The rarified air made us feel as though we were just unfit, with laboured breathing, a tightness in the chest and heaviness in the legs. A race was held after 3½ days, Gerry finishing 4th and Geoff 6th, and our performances were about 1¼ minutes down on our current sea-level times. As one would expect, our pace judgement was bad and in the early races there was a noticeable slowing-up after about a mile. Recovery afterwards also took longer than usual to begin with but, as with everything else, it felt normal once we had become acclimatised.

Four races were held during our stay in Mexico and these resulted in average percentages down on sea-level times by 8%, 6.9%, 6.6% and 5.7% respectively - a clear indication of the benefits of an acclimatisation period of 4 weeks. The results of each weekly race were as follows, (the figures in brackets give seconds down on mean sea-level times)

Throughout our stay we continued the laboratory tests that were held during our final few weeks in England. Doctors Pugh and Owen collected data on heart rate, respiration rate, blood pressure, oxygen consumption rate, oxygen debt and other functions.

It was proved conclusively on this trip that no harm would come to the fit distance athlete training or racing at this altitude, and that an acclimatisation period of four weeks (minimum) is essential - we were both continuing to feel fitter and faster right up to the moment of our departure.

We are agreed on one vital point. No thought could have been given to the middle and long distance athletes by those delegates who voted Mexico for the 1968 'Games'. It is evident from all tests so far carried out that the highland athlete will always retain an advantage over his sea-level counterpart when competin at high altitude. With the latter also having to spend a month or so beforehand to only partly close the gap, it seems distinctly unfair. Another factor of course is professionalism. With many countries setting up training camps at high altitudes and most sportsmen requiring even more time off from work when 1968 arrives, the Mexican venue will mean yet another step in the direction of non-amateur sport.

HOW I TRAINED

Schwab jnr.

We continue our extracts from Belgrave Newsletters of the early 1950's

Here is the schedule of Schwab jnr. the 1950 European 10,000 metres Champion and silver medallist in the 1948 Olympic Games.

TRAINING FOR THE 1948 OLYMPIC 10,000 METRES.

Commencing with the winter season 47/48 a training stroll of 1½ to 2 hours was taken every Sunday. During the stroll special care and attention was taken to exercise and strengthen the inner organs, and deep breathing was always practised with a view to improving the lymph and blood circulation and to fully exercise the lungs, the digestive organs and the heart.

This strolling was done in all weathers thus getting the body used to any climatic changes, or inclement weather. HAS HE RETURNED......?

Charlie Manning

Of course, if you are reading this sitting in a deck-chair whilst the wife is mowing the lawn, or sun-bathing on a beach to the delightful squeals of children dancing through the waves,or just being jerked along in a bus that allows 54 sitting, 5 standing but has, in fact, 618 on board, then this article will lose its point. You see, in the warmth of human contact (that includes the wife), little fears can be laughed at, but for just a few minutes try to discard your modern cynicism, cast off the mantle of sophistication and look again through the eyes of youth.

Turn the leaves with frantic haste, with excitement and finally gaze with awe at the two pages in the middle of "The Wizard". "The Truth About Wilson". Who'd ever have thought that in our sphere we would seriously concern ourselves with such ficticious nonsense...with so much data recorded.. performances observed....sagacious timekeepers..impossible.. and yet.. he HAS been observed.

A few whispers to close friends have spread gentle ripples across the black bottomless minds of the Titans that run around Richmond Park. "Is it him...?". "Can it really be...?" . "I wonder...". This phantom, if that is what it is, does not materialise when there is a large pack or the green-sward is littered with picnickers, or more restricted parties are agitating the ferns. No. It manifests itself, except in one rather singular case reported below, to lone runners. It is most likely to occur when the "Park" has one of her moods; she can make the conditions right, fatigue prepares the susceptible athlete's mind and inclement weather catalyses the whole action.

"The world is a stage and we but actors..." etc., so wrote the bast.., sorry bard from Stratford, but, upon what apron in this global theatre are there charged such forces that would a Hercules quake as now forgather within the gates of Richmond Park. (If you read from the word "but" in a sort of iambic pentameter it sounds rather good, don't you think). However, we digress - let us return to our backcloth loaded with atmosphere. One of our actors is a well known Belgravian - accountant - holder of the Blackstaff Shield - we are with-holding his name in case he is charged with heresy. A noble, astute and uncompromising reason lies in this voter's head - which is why we believe his story more than the others.

The fleet footed figure has been observed definately on three occasions. The first reports were early spring in 1965; secondly, in December of that year and again in January of this year.

The facts are simply that the man is lean and drawn with lank hair. He moves at great speed with all the effort registered in his countenance. He appears to be naked from the waist up and his trousers are tight, black and stop just below the knees. Sifting through all the information is an arduous task. There are those who make ridiculously extravagant claims, some who just don't want to be left out and others who are pure romancers. Now we have irrevocable proof .... that there is a mysterious athlete.. but, is it HIM. The evidence will be exhibited at our next social in April. At the moment it is being covetously guarded in the vaults of the Westminster Bank, Hampstead.

It happened this way.- The sky was a dull monotone grey, torn into great jagged pieces and moving rapidly across the heavens. The trees cracked their leafless icy fingers in the biting wind. Two figures struggled up the north face of Spankers. One was the intrepid Belgravian already mentioned, the other was the equally talented winner of the Inter-Counties last year. Their speed and strength confirmed their rankings until quite suddenly they looked relative scrubbers to the runner that flashed past....it was him...it must be...no ordinary man could dwarf such giants ..His athletic prowess was immediately evident...as quickly as had been his devastating appearance, so was he out of sight amongst the forbidding tangle of black bushes. This story would have remained untold but for what followed... About thirty cold minutes later, having circumscribed the park without meeting a soul abroad, they once again approached the forbidding copse. Having traversed the ridge with the "White House" behind them, they began the descent when they saw him again, swinging through the trees with surprising agility. He was naked; it all happened so fast. They shouted a friendly "Hallo". he dropped to the frozen ground, glared malevolently, grunted and disappeared into the wood.

For every fact there are a dozen rumours, perhaps you can decide. Myself, I must now have my wild honey, selected herbs and spring water, to sleep for 10 hours under a roof of stars for tomorrow's exploration to the limit of my endeavours.

Charlie Manning

CARNIVAL NIGHT

Amanuensis

For many years the activities of the Belgrave Harriers in the athletic world have been front-page news. It is possible that this developement was not envisaged by those far-seeing Belgravians when they founded the Club in 1887. For a certainty the Belgrave New Year Social would not have been imagined.

A long time ago, someone said "The hand that rocks the cradle, rules the world", this would seem to indicate that females play an important part in the affairs of men. It is a fact, no doubt, that the female supporters of the Belgrave Harriers play an important part in the well-being of the Club. Mothers, wives and girl friends have become a feature of any Belgrave meeting; encouraging the athlete and committee-man to greater endeavours.

For some years the Belgrave Harriers have recognised the value of Social gatherings and some splendid functions have been organised for the enjoyment of the members such as 'Jazz Festival', Jumble Sales (rather hectic) Week-End at Butlins (Indoor Sports), with the Saturday Socials and the Belgrave Annual Dinner.

One of the finest of these functions was held on 1st January, 1966, when the members of the Bels. in the company of athletes from a large number of other clubs met in Belgrave Hall to celebrate the commencement of the New Year and literally competed in a Carnival Social.

The Fancy dress and period costumes seen on the ballroom floor as the wearers swirled and twisted was a most colourful sight. It appeared that the history of the nations was being paraded for the purpose of comparison; the togas of the Romans, the wisdom of the Greeks, the gaiety of the French, the piracy of the seas, the joys of Pantomime, the misery of doom (or was it departed Pussyfoot), Grimms fairy tales, the grossness of the Middle Ages, the hope of a newly born baby. To so much colour, to the carefree laughter of this 'Get With It' congregation should be added the music that seemed to capture the mood of the moment; Jazz, Old-Time, The Blues and of course the Twist. Truly an evening of relaxation and enjoyment with the thoughts of training and performances on the road forgotten.

During the interval some clever miming to records was performed by various groups and the efforts of B.Merry & Co. is worthy of repetition. The costume parade by the ladies provided the selection committee (or Judges) with a most difficult task, it was only after five circuits of the Hall that the judges agreed to give the award to the 1966 Baby (Pat Balaam) for being so apposite. The Principal Boy (Jean Manning) and Aladdin (Mrs Lucas) were noted. The costume award for the gentleman was a little easier, but it is said that the winner did not know that he was King Henry VIII, he thought he was a pirate. Mention was made of 'David Crockett' (Hacking Col.), the Victorian Barman (Ted Stimpson). The award for the best lady and gentleman pair was gained by an Indian and his squaw, their costumes were perfect (two of the judges thought they were 'Sioux'), It was later established that these 'Indians' were John Bicourt and Val Harper.

Ted Stimpson and his assistants on the Social Committee should be well satisfied with their efforts in providing a wonderful social gathering for the enjoyment of the Belgrave Harriers and their Ladies.

BELS. 2nd again in 'SOUTHERN'

Olympian

Although the weather was not unduly cold and we were spared the promised snow the conditions under foot were worse than had ever been experienced,even at Parliament Hill. The whole area was sodden with water and it needed very little to turn the ground into a sea of mud.

On paper our team looked very strong indeed and we had every hope of reversing the 1965 result when Portsmouth beat us by 42 points. However, the Hampshire club were at the top of their form and the result turned out to be almost identical to that at Brighton the preceding year, with the same margin of points.

It was not our team captain's day for he was up-ended before even getting out of the starting pens and deposited ignominously on his bottom. This gave him the extra task of catching up with the leaders, a difficult job in normal conditions but infinitely worse in the sloppy mud of today's race.Nevertheless after 2½ miles Gerry was our first man in 7th place followed by Thresher 19th Geoff North (fully confident that he was to beat his brother at last in a cross-country race) 31st, Fairclough 35th, Thorpe 46th and Black 54th well backed up by O'Hara 55th and Dabbs 56th. The score at this stage was 192 and Portsmouth, despite having four men in the first eleven places, were approximately 180.

One mile further on all our lads had improved considerably except Black who had fallen back to 87, his 6th scoring place having been taken by Dabbs who had come up to 47. Score now 169 as against Portsmouth's 155, for whom Martin Hyman was moving through the field from 80th place at a great speed.

Towards the end of the 2nd lap Gerry had moved up to 3rd with Thresher 17th, Geoff North 22nd, Fairclough 26th, Thorpe 39th and Dabbs 45th,totalling 152. Hyman for Portsmouth was now up to 33rd and their score was down to 130.

As the field went up the hill at the beginning of the third lap we had made up a few more places to score 147 but Portsmouth had drawn away a little and were now totalling only 115.

Over the last lap our fortunes wavered for Mel Thorpe,who had been running very well, dropped back twelve places,but Laurie O'Hara who had been late arriving and started without a warm-up, was coming through fast and gained fourteen positions to pass both Thorpe and Dabbs. Gerry was finding the going tough and had to give way to John Roberts (SLH) and Peter Yates (Windsor & Eton) and brother Geoff also lost some ground.

The final reckoning gave us a total score of 148 as against Portsmouth's 106 so we must wait another year before we displace them from the leading position in Southern cross-country circles which they have held for six years Perhaps we could do it in this year's National at Sheffield.

THE 1966 NATIONAL

National Championships, Graves Park, Sheffield---March 5th

What a race! What a finish! After Hill (Bolton) and Turner (Liverpool) had been locked in combat for the whole race, alternately pushing the pace and out on their own, Hill made a frantic effort to shake off his rival 1½ miles from the finish but 2 yards was all he could get. With just 600 yds left Turner made his 'final' effort and when a gap of 4 yards appeared it seemed all over, but the Bolton man showed a new lease of life 100 yards from the line and produced a second sprint that momentarily carried him sailing past Turner; but with the finish drawing nearer at increasing speed the Liverpool Harrier responded yet again and as they crossed the line in a blanket sprint finish it seemed there could be no justice in a result other than a tie. However, inches do count in the end and on this score the verdict went to Ron Hill who, surprisingly, is National Champion for the first time.

Our own Gerry North had an excellent run, proving the forecasters wrong again---none of them considering Gerry for the International team yet alone the first four. After the usual fast start, up a steep hill, the field had to negotiate 2 laps of a miniature ½ mile circuit---hardly fair to the outside positions in a field sometimes 15 abreast. Gerry and Geoff got a good start though and were both in the first six after 600 yards. With only 1¼ miles gone the steep climb from the start had been covered three times and the field well and truly sorted out. Geoff began to drop back the second time up and Gerry was dangerously near losing contact on the third ascent. Briault was pushing the pace at this stage, but the pace slackened for the only time in the race on the flat, zig-zagged portion of the course farthest away from the finish. For a short distance the runners were 5 abreast and the leading group had consolidated itself to 10 strong. It was at this point that Turner decided to break up the 'pally' gathering and with Hill the only one to hang on the leaders were soon strung out in Indian file. Gerry's effort in regaining contact with the leaders left him little with which to respond but he still managed to carve himself a niche in 5th position, a place he slowly but surely improved upon--reaching 3rd at 6 miles and for a spell pulling back the leading pair. Unfortunately the steep slope down to the start caused him serious discomfort each of the four times it was covered, due to a lingering stitch, and on each occasion he lost ground. Hogan, who was running in his first English "National" and who had been trailing Gerry throughout, probably noticed this for on the last descent just prior to commencing the final large lap he made his effort disposing of a 10 yards deficit and opening up a 20 yards lead. For a further mile Hogan kept the pace going and it seemed for a spell that he might well catch the leaders but after getting 50 yards clear of Gerry he could make no further impression Gerry held his form well and finished strongly, though his pursuers were making ground over the final circuit.

Geoff also suffered on those first 3 steep climbs. he was not quite strong enough to maintain his excellent start but he deserves full credit for the way he battled over the whole distance to hold a slipping position From 9th place at ½ mile to 17th at 1¼ miles, he was 28th at the half distance. His tenacity over the final 4½ miles enabled him to finish 38th and be our second scorer.

Of the remainder, none was inside the first 150 after a ½ mile and, except for Fairclough, none inside the first 120 after 3 miles. Tony worked very hard to make ground over the first large lap and at 3 miles had reached 68th position, but the final stages of the race were a bit too much for him, most of his slip to 81st occurring in the final mile or so. Charlie Dabbs showed his strength after being outpaced at the start. He worked his way through to 145th at 3 miles and then forged through the field at the rate of 10 places a mile; just failing to catch Tony at the finish. Laurie O'Hara also progressed steadily and gained more valuable places with his usual final burst. Our sixth scorer was 20 years old Joe Reilly, who had only come in as reserve but rose to the occasion in great style--taking 30 places in the final lap and finishing a creditable 134th.

Mel Thorpe had great difficulty in getting to the start on time. A fast car drive from Blackburn and a sprint from the dressing-room enabled him to just make it, but this, together with the effects of his present move to Coventry must have left him with precious little nervous energy for an event of this stature. As it was Mel felt unable to continue when well down the field and retired from the race. Whether or not it would have made any difference no one will ever know but Trevor Hart, who had been told to stand by, didn't even report to the team manager who, when teams were declared entered Mel's name more in hope than design. In the normal course of events Hart would have been declared as a runner. So much for our growing band of runners who consider reserve status beneath their dignity.

The team race was very interesting. With five men home Portsmouth led Coventry and North Staffs, but the position was reversed on the close-in with N. Staffs' 6th man 90th, Coventry's 132nd and Pompey's 173rd.

Our 4th place was our best effort since 1948 and with Thresher and Barrow still to come our chances of further improvement next year are very good.

Have you seen who were 5th? Is it likely that a renewal of the great Birchfield/Belgrave battles of the past is imminent?
