EDITORIAL

"He who pays the piper, calls the tune".

Have you ever heard athletes talk of anything but athletics?. Are there any wives who doubt what the topic of conversation will be at social gatherings?. And when it comes to the dedicated athlete, is it not true that those outside the understanding atmosphere of the athletic club would regard him as selfish, inward looking?. Athletes may talk politics sometimes but other athletes never hear them do so---it is a non-political world. All the sadder then that international politics should be encroaching into it.

Several GOVERNMENTS have recently announced their withdrawal from the Mexico Olympics. No one knows what their own Olympic Committees thought, least of all the athlete's views. In the political arena it doesn't matter what they think. One does not question the reasons for such actions, simply the procedures and their ramifications. Where will it all end? Will the United Nations soon be legislating on where international teams may compete?.

Athletic Associations are bound by the laws of their country---those that are state sponsored are bound even tighter. We in Britain may have to hand the cap round at each Olympics, rely on commercial sponsorship and make do by our own efforts; but at least we run our own affairs, and the Government would get short shrift if it tried to speak for the AAA or BAAB. As tempting as it is to have lavish government support for athletics, is it really worth having the political strings that go with it?.

On Thursday, March 14th, Mr Denis Howell, Minister of Sport turned down a back-bench suggestion that the Government should not continue to subsidise the British Olympic team for the 1968 Games in Mexico in view of the racially segregated team from South Africa. Mr. Howell told M.P.s, quite rightly, "British participation in the Olympic Games is a matter for the British Olympic Committee----". The significance of such questions being raised in the House of Commons should not be overlooked.

State aided sport will soon be widespread. Those who think this will solve all our problems would do well to ponder the events of recent weeks.

Denis Howell

HON SEC'S NOTES

A.A.H.

The Track Season will be with us very shortly and we would once again ask all members to support our fixtures to the full as active athletes, or as helpers and officials, or as supporters. Particularly we would ask you to note our Championships date - Saturday May 25th at Hurlingham Park Track. Other dates booked are :- Sat. 23rd March - Tooting Track (Field Events Meeting) Sat. 20th April - Battersea Park. Wed. 1st Ma y - " " Wed. 12th June - " " Wed. 26th June - " " Wed. 3rd July - " " (Inter-Club Walk) Wed. 17th July - " " Wed. 31st July - " " Wed. 7th August - " "

Please also note Sat.July 20th when our Open 20 miles Run will be held from Belgrave Hall, Wimbledon, and Sat.Sept.7th in the evening, when the Borough of Wandsworth are sponsoring a 7 miles Road Run which we have agreed to promote on their behalf. The prize list looks like dazzling everybody, and refreshments for all competitors are a probability which should guarantee three dozen Belgrave entries at a minimum.

There is likely to be a special prize for the first Wandsworth resident not gaining any other award; and now that the Borough of Wandsworth embraces additionally most of the area previously under Battersea Borough Council, we may expect keen competition to take this extra award.

In years gone by our Club drew tremendous strength from the district now falling within the London Borough of Wandsworth, and every street name carried recollections of Belgrave members. If, as is possible, their Seven miles run becomes an annual event it may well be the means of bringing a regular supply of new members to the Club. Many will remember that at least three "local lads" BERT FOOTER, ARTHUR PENNY and HARRY CHURCHER became International athletes many times over, in the intervals of assisting Belgrave to win Championship team titles.

For many years our Committee met at the "Feathers", Broadway, S.W.1. where we enjoyed adequate facilities in a very central position at no cost to the Club. Our December meeting at this venue was our last, however, for we understand a block of offices is to rise on the present site in the near future. If any member does know of a suitable monthly meeting place with this advantage of centrality, your Hon Secretary would be glad to hear from him.

We are always interested to follow the careers of those who have served the Club's teams well in the past; and the recent press announcement that ALBERT FROST, after eight years as Treasurer, has been appointed Financial Director of I.C.I. Ltd., (probably Britains largest company) calls for our congratulations and good wishes. Probably Albert's best run for the Club was in the 1947 "National" at Apsley over a course covered with two feet, and more, of snow, when he finished 17th - not all that far out from being nominated as an International. This however did fall once again to LEN HERBERT (6th); and other members of our team on this most difficult day were :- DICK TOBIN 31, VIC BLOWFIELD 37, TED STIMPSON (this years President - none other) 51, STAN EALES 65. Sutton Harriers, from the North, took our title from us, scoring 160 pts. We were second with 184 pts, and Reading A.C. took third medals.

In our last issue we printed a photograph of Club members outside Tom Sullivan's pub at Barnes, supplied by Albert Macher. For the record it should be noted that this photograph was taken in 1913 (not 1912 as stated) and in fact appeared in"LOTINGA'S Weekly" on 26th April 1913. Incidentally we were very glad indeed to see Albert Macher and his daughter at our 10 miles C.C.Championship; and to see Albert present the "MACHER" Shield in the Hall after the race.

Charlie Speechley who has been a member of the Club since 1919, has been made Life Honorary Member of the Club to mark his many years support to Belgrave.

During the coming Track season it has been decided to promote two "pilot" Leagues of equal precedence, and as one of the major clubs in the country we have received, and accepted, an invitation to compete. This will involve two full scale meetings in the Midlands and one in London, and will demand 100% enthusiasm and support from our track and field athletes if we are to do ourselves justice. Based on the experience and knowledge gained in this years "pilot" Leagues the new official National Leagues, with promotion and relegation, will commence in the 1969 season. Obviously any scheme of this sort will not achieve immediate perfection or be without its critics; and suggestions for possible improvements have been invited. One such that we have made(which was turned down in the pilot League arrangements) is the addition of a 6 miles event. Races at this distance are one of the great attractions of the major White City meetings and lead to tremendous interest. Can the Leagues afford to neglect this event? Any suggestion that the time taken (30 minutes or so) rules this event out is surely invalidated by say the inclusion of the Pole Vault which with twelve competitors or varying standards may take hours and hours. The writer feels, too, that the Leagues should consist of twelve clubs each fielding one man per event, rather than six clubs with two athletes in each event. Twelve clubs would ensure a more widespread national appeal, bring more "star" athletes within the scope of one meeting, create less difficulty for the clubs in that the teams would be half the size. The difficulty here would be in deciding the minor placings in the short distance events; but with the goodwill of the clubs this should not prove insuperable - a system of cross heats should take care of it. This is not the time or place to go into further detail on this matter of Athletic Leagues, but we may be sure there will be plenty of discussion on this subject in the months to come.

In 1969 the A.A.A. Championships will move from the time honoured July date to Aug. 1st & 2nd, and all championships are likely to be at metric distances. The equivalent of the mile will be 1600 metres, four laps, and not the usual 1500 metres we have seen hitherto in international events. Four laps of the amended track seems entirely logical, and the translation to metric distances inevitable following the pressures being exerted in governmental and industrial circles. All A.A.A. championships will change, including Youths & Juniors; and Relay championships also and this is calculated to give the Clerk of the Course and groundsmen some worries on old established tracks. Presumably Area and County championships will follow suit when practicable and in the fulness of time our Club Championships may also undergo changes. We shall miss the magic of "All out for the Mile" - but no doubt there will be compensations.

Another change which will have much earlier application, (effective April 1st in fact) is that relating to team eligibility. New rules approved give athletes at School, College, Universities etc., opportunities, if desired, to represent their educational establishment in open competition (subject to conditions laid down); but to be eligible for their "open" club if the "closed" club does not compete. The "qualifying" period (which applied to athletes changing their clubs if they had represented their first club in open team competition) of 14 mths is reduced to 9 mths. The County residential qualifying period is also reduced to 9 months, and some help given to athletes born abroad. To come in line with the above an athlete joining a club over twenty miles from his place of residence will now wait only 9 months instead of the 14 months previously required. It will be interesting to see whether this general move to ease restrictive rules (generally speaking, introduced many years ago to avoid various abuses) will be found to function fairly and satisfacorily both for individual athletes and for clubs.

It is pleasing to be able to report that PAST - PRESIDENT TOM MORRELL is now making great progress following a heart attack just before Christmas, and should soon be with us again. Tom is one of those quiet workers for the Club who accomplishes so much without ever creating any sense of fuss, or calling attention to himself. His services in connection with prizes, issuing of numbers and collection of fees at our track meetings, recording and keeping account of the points earned by the competing clubs have been most valuable; as are the other duties he carries through unobtrusively but reliably. We shall welcome his return to Club activities with real pleasure.

BOXING DAY MEETING - 1967

HARRY SHIELDS

HARRY SHIELDS WRITES:

After the fug of Christmas activities, to those with energy enough,what compensations there were in this delightful gathering, on a lovely fresh sunny morning with its mixture of young in heart and young in body. The thought of some thirty years ago, Arthur Penny fresh from his reputed Christmas diet of fish, regularly winning the 2 mile handicap, now resplendent in his red starters cap escorted by that epitomé of courage, his wife Winn. Dave Shepherd, Norman Dewberry and many others not looking a day older. Tom Carter acting with the august majesty of a Cohen on the top of the Judges stand. Ted Stimpson, Our President, busily looking after the tape. We all missed the solid presence of Tom Morrell and his unassuming "general factorum" in obtaining the prizes, being competitors steward, results recorder etc., and voiced our hopes to see him speedily restored to health. With the exception of the walk, the events were well supported and very obviously enjoyed. The rich mixture of experience and the product of that academy of young athletes presided over by Reg Hopkins, produced keen and exciting competition. After all the pomposity one hears in connection with the "Byers Report", international athletes and so on, how refreshing it is to be in this atmosphere of true amateur athletics. The whole meeting was suitably rounded off by a typical gesture of our "A.A.A's" timekeeper E. Ryan who by sleight of hand produced two aircraft construction kits to be added to the prize list for the younger athletes. Thank you Ted they were much appreciated. (Cont'd overleaf)

HOGS BACK

HOGS BACK 9 MILE---DECEMBER 16th 1967

The old cliché, 'we was robbed' would have been a reasonable comment following our failure to win this annual outing over the Hogs Back. For the second year, near ideal conditions prevailed, only this time our entry was stronger and our hopes higher. However, fate was quick to deal its blow when just after the start Gerry North, whilst leading the field, was accidently tripped from behind and received a badly gashed arm and thigh. After this serious shaking he was faced with the unenviable task of trying to re-catch the leaders in this class field. At around 5 miles there were only about six in front including brother Geoff. and a very strong Alan Rushmer (Tipton H.) leading the field. At seven miles, before the fast run down to the finish, only Rushmer remained in front, but the effort had taken its toll and ,as the results show, by the time those last two miles had been covered Gerry had been forced to concede some of the places so gamely fought for. Geoff. fading a little over the final stages ran a great race,as did Peter Whewell to complete our scoring three. Bill Dance ran well considering he started with hardly any warm up, arriving as did Pat O'Connor just in time to get their numbers. In fact their last minute timing (not for the first time!) prompted the question as to whether Don McLean's 'title' was in jeopardy!!.It was good to see steeple-chasers John Bicourt and Dick Piotrowski out on the road, at what is a slightly longer than usual distance for them,as well as being a tough race. John recorded a time only a few seconds slower than last year (47m26s), when he was our first man home in 36th Position!. 'Old man' Mike Stearman on the come-back trail showed he meant business by giving Pat O'Connor a few uncomfortable moments and some of these watching food for thought.

YOUTHS & BOYS

The boys & youths would like to thank those seniors who help our section through the winter by helping to mark and marshal our cross-country races - not forgetting the ladies who serve us with tea and cakes afterwards.

An appeal to all boys & youths to pay their subs. It is a costly business running a club,and our postage bill, carding all of you almost every week, comes to quite a large amount. It is our duty as good club members to help pay our way.

We have had some grand turn-outs at the track this winter with, on one occasion, 25 boys in attendance. This is most encouraging and augurs well for the coming track season. Training has been quite varied and during the snowy weather included a three hour snowball fight! Ideal for the throwers perhaps and, in Nigel Bacon's case, for his running also!

Reg Hopkins and Tom Carter have been taking movie films from time to time, and they hope to put on a show in the near future. Included will be a repeat of that film classic(!) "Belgrave Win the London-Brighton Relay".

Bill Curtin once again won the South London Schools Cross-Country Championships held at Wimbledon just before Christmas and so will now represent his area in the All London Champs, this unfortunately for us is on the same day as the final match in the North Surrey League when we would have liked his services in trying to hold our 11 point lead. Never mind, we wish him luck and know he will be out to win his way into the all-England race. Another one of our Boys will be absent for the same reason. Richard Langley was the winner of the East London Schools Cross-Country Championships in 18.35 (180 Runners) helping his school to win the team event. This was a 3 mile cross-country race held at Hanaiult Forest. Bill and Richard will not be meeting in the London Championships however as Bill is in a higher division. We can however hope to have two winners and, perhaps, two winners in the All England?

LATER! TWO BELS AT STOKE Bill Curtin and Richard Langley won themselves a trip to the English Schools cross country Championships to be held at Stoke on March 23rd. Bill gained his place by coming third in the intermediate division of the 'London' over four miles at Hainault on Saturday February 23rd. Richard also gained Third place, but in the junior division.

YOUNG PROFILE No:1 CLIFF CALDERWOOD

Clifford Owen Calderwood was born at Isleworth, Middlesex on 17.12.49. But for his brother Dick (also a Belgravian) he would not be carrying a claret & gold vest in our Youth teams today. Cliff, who is a slight 8½ stone and 5'5½" tall, runs daily throughout the year and, apart from pre-race days, each session lasts a good hour. His ambition, in the short term, is to re-write the Belgrave Junior 3 miles and 6 miles records and, in the long term, to win a marathon race. One would expect a youth showing such singleness of purpose to admire most of all the Jim Ryun's, Kip Keino's or Ron Clarke's of the athletics world, but no, its still elder brother Dick - "It took a lot for him to get from 40 cigarettes a day to a 1:56.9 half mile in two years". Cliff has no coach but works to a well thoughtout schedule which includes a Lydiard type jog of 15-20 miles every Sunday. His best marks to date from 880 yds to 6 miles are 2:11, 4:52, 10:48, 16:30 and 33:16 - times he confidently expects to slash this year. The performance which has given him most pleasure so far was his winning of the Belgrave Junior Cross Country Championship this season, whilst still a youth. It seems fairly certain that he has far greater pleasures to come.

REMOVE THE SHACKLES!

In writing the following article your editor speaks only for himself and, in doing so, hopes to provoke sufficient feeling on your part to send in your views, for or against, on whether we have reached the time to

REMOVE THE SHACKLES!

Perhaps the greatest difficulty besetting mankind in this so-called space age is the inability to recognise and move with the times. Progress, however cynically one may view it, proceeds at an ever increasing rate and improvements that once took 2,000 years to achieve will soon be gained in a single generation. Civilization is in a state of flux and the codes of one year are often discarded by the next. Those that drag their feet for more than an instant will soon be left trailing in the wake. Already there are signs in the athletics world of this failure to move with the times. Those Victorian citadels of power that worship the divine right of amateurism, the I.A.A.F. and the I.O.C., each year bog themselves down a little more as they fight their losing battle to preserve amateur laws and definitions that were framed to suit conditions at the turn of the century. Slowly but surely they are working their way into a tortuous situation that could eventually lead to a major split in world athletics. With Avery Brundage floating gently in the clouds, reminiscing over his boyhood days, his Olympic committee have issued their latest amateur proclamation which, if enforced, would eliminate all athletes who have accepted prizes worth more that $50, have received athletic scholarships or have spent more than 4 weeks a year in a training camp - that covers virtually all the top athletes in the world! Imagine the I.O.C. trying to invoke this rule against the national heroes of some lands. We could well be on the way to two Olympic Games - the official one and the unofficial one. Meanwhile, the I.A.A.F. has not been standing still. Don Pain, their part-time secretary of this world-wide federation, is very cross at the practice of bringing into the arenas hold-all bags carrying trade names. "....we intend to stamp out this growing practice" he is reported as saying and, sure enough, an additional rule is hatched out. Not, of course, one that is in the interests of the athlete - only the rules first-ever devized did that. Part of the new I.A.A.F. rule says "when and where it is proved that the name or the picture of the athlete was used for the purpose of this paragraph without his knowledge the indemnity to be collected from the person or persons responsible for the injury, privately agreed or stipulated by the courts, will revert to the Federation to which the athlete belongs". How's that for a gem! Who takes who to court? Supposing the athlete is innocent but doesn't wish to take proceedings against the Advertiser? Is he guilty unless proved innocent? A few test cases would prove most interesting but would only serve to show up the pathetic state of today's outlook. Since the last world war the percentage of college-sponsored or state-sponsored athletes in the world's best performances lists has increased greatly. This trend will continue for as long as the Arthur Rowes and the Berwyn Jones's of this world are discriminated against by rules that only sort out the "private" offender. Despite the barriers, one day we shall all be called just "athletes" with no strings - similar to table-tennis players, cricketers and soon no doubt, tennis players - and the little man at the White City who spends his time sticking blank paper on hold-all bags will be out of a job. Unfortunately there are signs that the rule-book formulators intend to go on burying themselves in more paper. Their only hope of survival is to join the Hockey Federation whose President is against league and knock-out competitions and dislikes "highly organised competition in an amateur game" - so much for hockey! When are we going to realise that the best athletes are only interested in meeting the best opponents and beating them on equal physical terms. We are not interested in our opponent's colour, religion, country's policy, class or how he amasses his "wealth", but simply in crushing him competitively. This is part and parcel of dedication, whatever the ethics, and those that don't like it can stick to the inter-club scene which will go on as always. Although there have been political withdrawals from time to time I have yet to meet the athlete who would refuse to compete against a "professional" on the grounds of unfair advantage, potential corruption, or his wealth. Unwittingly, these international bodies are protecting the very elements they consider to be undesirable and are discriminating against the athlete who is unable to obtain "approved" sponsorship. It will not be long before ALL Olympic finalists will, by necessity, need to be students, civil servants or members of the armed forces. An indication of this trend is the world's No. 1 shot putter Randy Matson. For some undisclosed reason he did not take his examinations in his final year at college and it has now been announced that he will have a year's special extension to take them. He will not be eligible for any N.C.A.A. competition but, coincidentally, his extra time occupies Olympic year 1968. If Matson was an Eastern European he would also be able to rely on being looked after and would certainly undergo no hardship when he attended the lengthy training camps held by such nations. He would have no fear of being branded a professional,because communist countries simply do not recognise professionalism - what an admirable solution to the problem! It has already enabled the U.S.S.R. and other countries to field their World Cup squads in the soccer qualifying matches for the Olympic Games. (Cont'd on page 30)

REMOVE THE SHACKLES (Cont'd from page 15)

The professional-amateur distinction was drawn in most sports because of the unfair advantage of full-time training over part-time training. This still applies to a few sports but in athletics no such distinction exists. There are many many international athletes, swimmers and tennis players who would not spend any more time training than they do now, even if they did have all day, every day. My plea is not to stop the widespread breaches of these unnatural laws butto remove the shackles and discourage no one from taking part in sport. Give them all a chance by calling them just "athletes" and if they can earn pin-money in advertising or pro football good luck to them, because it won't be for long and when they lose their form they will bite the dust quicker than ever - athletically and financially. In conclusion, I quote Leonard Cawley, whose comments on the croquet-putter dispute in the golf world could be applied to many of our sports. "And if the Rules of Golf Committees start telling us how we should even grip our clubs they will soon become like governments who wake up one morning and find themselves with no one to govern". Can you hear us up there in the clouds, Avery?

'I WAS THERE' No. 5: by Alf Harley

Alf Harley

During over forty years of membership I have been privileged to be present at many of the Club's individual and team successes, all of which have given great pleasure and satisfaction - but the outstanding occasion, because it was the first time ever, was the winning of the English Cross-country team title in 1935. Even now I can recall the thrill,when acting as Disc-recorder, of accepting the numbered discs from our men, of rapidly totalling our score, of checking with the officials of other leading clubs, and the deep satisfaction of knowing that we really had won! Success was really ours. Not that there was anything fortuitous about our success. Our Cross-country captain BERT FOOTER had directed all his efforts towards this eventual success for many years before. Other victories on the way were welcome, but no substitute for the Championship title that was to be ours. Two years earlier, 1933, we had been placed second in the "Southern" at Chingford; and a fortnight later in the "National" at Alderley Edge, Cheshire we were placed for the first time. FOOTER and ARTHUR PENNY gave us a glorious start in finishing 4th and 5th and our team position was third with 233 points against Birchfield's 80, and the 101 of Salford H. The following season we scored our first Southern win at Taplow; and at the E.C.C.U. Championship held at Himley Park, with Penny 4th and Footer 9th, we were again placed third, but this time our score was 113 points, one point only behind the Northern Champions, SALFORD HARRIERS. Birchfield were again the winners, with 88 points. So came March 9th 1935 when the Championship was staged at Beaconsfield. In the "Southern" a fortnight earlier at HASSOCKS we had displeased some of our supporters by finishing only second, beaten a point - but nevertheless we were quietly confident. 295 runners thundered from the pens and within the first few yards two of our men, BERT SHAW and HARRY SHIELDS were prostrate on the ground. However, they picked themselves up and chased the field. At four miles JIM GINTY was leading our hopes in fourth position and we seemed well-placed, though accurate team scoring was impossible. We could see however that Surrey A.C. (who had beaten us in the "Southern") had their sixth man behind our eighth, that Salford had three men well up but the others were not prominent, that Birchfield Harriers, Champions on so many occasions, were far from happy with two men only in the earlier placings and CURRIE, Midland Champion, retired from the race. At seven miles our confidence knew no bounds! Excepts for accidents the Title was ours! JIM GINTY, BERT FOOTER and DAN PATIENCE were more or less together in the first twenty places or so. A few seconds behind them, a most encouraging sight - ALF TAYLOR, HARRY SHIELDS and TOM CARTER running together in good heart. Not so far away were BERT SHAW, ARTHUR ALLUM and JACK FLOWER; and we rejoiced that our nine men were all in front of Birchfield's sixth! On the last three mile lap many places were gained, the TAYLOR-SHIELDS-CARTER trio pegging back many positions. In the last mile our runners gave all they had and we finished with the remarkable scoring sequence of 11,14, 15,18,19,20 - six men in a range of ten placings. Our next three soon followed in and we had our nine finishers in 57 scoring positions. Truly a great day! I can assure you it is no joke acting as disc-recorder when the Club runners follow one another in so rapidly - but it gives great satisfaction! The congratulations poured in - the foundation had been laid on which many further victories in Championship events could be built, and on which we can still build. A really great day, a day of critical importance; for thereafter we competed as Champions. The minor successes of yester year no longer held importance - to take second place in the Championship was failure. Such was the significance of March 11th 1935 - things could never be the same again! For the record I give the relevant detail below and you will be struck by the handsome nature of our win. A word of sympathy, even at this late stage, for ARTHUR PENNY, who was nursing a broken bone in the foot at that time, and so missed running and scoring on this eventful day. HERE & THERE

Congratulations to BARRY SAWYER on the birth of his Australian daughter. The joy of the occasion helped to temper the disappointment he suffered in December when gastro-enteritis broke his remarkable training sequence of 708 consecutive days. He didn't give up without a struggle though and timed his final run beautifully - just reaching the front door in time ! Congratulations also to RANGIT BHATIA on the birth of his Indian daughter. He hopes she will be India's answer to Mary Rand! Rangit recently finished 5th in the 5,000 m event at the National Inter-State Championships in Madras. It is good to see FRANK SIMMONS out and about again. He came out of hospital on November 29th for what he intends to be the last time and, apart from some pain in his legs, feels fighting fit! Is this to be Chris Steer's year? But of course it is. He has been biding his time waiting for the announcement to be made. He even took a job at the nearby B.B.C. in anticipation of it. And at last it came. "The Wormwood Scrubs running track will open shortly". Now he can swing into those lunch-time training schedules. It's especially convenient too when one's cell is on the ground floor anyway! Members will be relieved to hear that TOM MORRELL is making good progress after his heart attack in early December. Tom, our President of 13 years ago, has never ceased to serve the Club in a multitude of ways. His readiness to take on the unpopular backroom jobs has always been appreciated and this dates back to those early days when the tin baths had to be filled and the pennies collected. During the war years, as helpers came and went, he continued to keep our HQ in a good state of repair and, for a good 16 years or so, stocked and sold Club clothing et. This latter job led to that unenviable task of obtaining medals, arranging engraving and buying handicap prizes for what must have amounted to several hundred events - and it only needs a couple of these arrangements to go wrong to incur the wrath of the side-lines! As with most long-serving officials he carries this burden resolutely and still has enough enthusiasm left over to assist our Hon Treasurer and act as competitors steward and/or recorder at all our home fixtures, summer and winter. These are just a few of the thoughts that come to mind on hearing of our Past-President's misfortune. Get well soon TOM, we look forward to your return.

Column · page 32

A COUNTRYMAN'S JOTTINGS

How wrong can you get! In the December issue I wrote "Portsmouth-----they are a spent force. Nth Staffs, now reputed to have broken up-------". Yet both teams pushed us out of National medals on the last lap of that fateful Sutton Coldfield course! Not because they were especially good but because our runners failed to click yet again. Coventry were deserved winners on the day and proved that 6 in 60 is now necessary to win the 'National'----the 'retired' Basil Heatley closing-in in 63rd place. What went wrong? Well, Whewell and Fairclough at their best are both capable of finishing in the thirties or forties, and that alone would have given us a win. Poor Bill Dance, after 2½ laps of his best form to date, suffered calf muscle cramp and our supporters witnessed the unusual sight of Bill losing no less than 15 places on the run in. It was ironical that the team who beat us in the Southern, Reading AC, should finish only seventh and that Portsmouth (only 4th at Parliament Hill) should finish 2nd! Pompey deserve all the praise they can get. For the second successive year every runner rose to the occasion and fooled the forecasters; they would certainly have repeated their win of last year if Tim Johnston could have got home from Mexico. And so we have to wait for another year. Can we go on improving or is time running out? Are we doomed to hover on the fringe eternally or can runners like Major, Bicourt, Piotrowski, Williams etc give us a lift up into the medals next year?. Like all clubs we have our ups and downs. The ups can be frustratingly "almost up" and the downs depressingly "very down". The following graph shows our 'National' placings since World War II----you may interpret them how you will.

[Graph: NATIONAL PLACINGS SINCE 1946. POSITIONS 1-25 against years 1946-1968]

We have always enjoyed very friendly relations with our neighbouring athletic clubs, particularly Mitcham AC whose many successes in recent years have been largely due to the Mitchell brothers, who are former Belgravians. On more than one occasion recently though, I have heard the comments "of course, all our boys are local lads" and "no imports in our team" and "all Surrey runners at Mitcham". All said, we hope, in good humour, but one wonders whether the inference is that Belgrave rely on athletes "drafted in" from the rest of the country, whilst Mitcham raise their own. I wonder what Thresher, Fairclough, Major, Bicourt, O'Hara, Hart, Dabbs, Steer etc. think of that. Agreed that the North brothers, Dance, Whewell, Williams, Kerr and others have played parts of varying degrees in our success but they joined us on moving to London because they had to change their club. I wonder how necessary it was for Dunphy and Murray (Hercules), Kortenray(Wimbledon) Smallbone (Epsom and Ewell) and--almost--Collins (Surrey) to change their's. I would have thought that a "local lad" is one who LIVES within comfortable distance of his club's facilities---his place of birth or life history is quite irrelevant.

Many view the recently introduced 9 months qualifying period (instead of 14 months) for athletes changing clubs within a 20 miles radius of their home, as a go-ahead for widespread movement. A runner can now resign immediately after the 'National', qualify for his new club by Christmas and wear his new colours thro' all the championships from the County onwards. For the cross-country enthusiast this is no penalty at all.

JOHN BICOURT scored a fine win in the final race of the London Colleges Cross-Country league at Twickenham on March 8th. He beat Grice, Mouat, Stock and Buchanan in good time. JOHN is all set for an even better track season than 1967, when he recorded 8:57.6 for 3,000 metres steeplechase. He recently ran a time trial in 9:17--pretty sharp for this time of the year--and is now all set for the National Road Relay at Leicester and could well be one of our leading performers in that event.

We say farewell to Peter Whewell who returned to South Africa the day after the 'National'. During his stay here he worked for a firm of auditors, and it was the frequent spells of late working and consequent lack of training that led to his loss of form between arrival and departure. Nevertheless, he always threw himself enthusiastically into all of our activities and we valued his membership highly. Thank you Peter for your support, and don't forget to keep in touch.

Our team was unable to go to Hannut, Belgium, this year to defend "Le Soir" inter-club title they won in 1967 because the sponsors were unable to contribute towards the cost. If we had been informed early enough perhaps something could have been done but, as it was, we were unable to go. The cup we won last year, to keep, has been entitled the "Le Soir" Cup and will be awarded to the winning team each year in our "Southern" Invitation Road Relay.

On April 27th we tackle the National road relay championship at Leicester. If ever a race should suit Belgrave it is this one. With twelve stages to run our depth is of great advantage---or should be. Once again we can expect to see our runners fighting desperately for inclusion in our team whilst many other clubs are having trouble finding that last man. But finding a strong twelve is no good if they don't hit form on the day and the test of our fortunes will almost certainly lie in the on-day form of the three runners who will join the North brothers and Thresher on the six long stages. On the short stages we match anyone; witness last year's one fastest and three second fastest stage runs. Of the opposition we face, Coventry (the holders) must be favourites. With Heatley and Kilby both in form again and Bartlett and Lancaster to join their winning 'National' team (9 in 117) they should be a stronger force than last year's winning dozen. Second last year were Derby and, although they only managed ninth at Sutton Coldfield (7 in 150) they have three outstanding Juniors to come in. Bronze medallists were Portsmouth---but I really will eat my hat if they match that this time! Manchester D.L.C. pretty well matched us man for man in the 'National'; they have no difficulty with depth either and should beat Nth. Staffs. My forecast is 1. Coventry. 2. Belgrave. 3. Derby. 4. Manchester. 5. Reading. 6. Nth. Staffs. 7. Portsmouth, but I am hoping I have one and two round the wrong way!

4th AGAIN ! MEDALS ELUDE 'NATIONAL' TEAM

SUTTON COLDFIELD ---March 8th

This year's race proved without a shadow of doubt that nine miles is too far for most of the Belgrave team---the valuable points lost on the last lap when we should have been moving up were the difference between winning and 4th place. With one lap to go we were definitely second and still with a chance to overtake Coventry but at the end we were beaten back and missed the championship by 98 points and second place by only 23 points. This was a repetition of the Southern where Reading beat us in a similar manner. After half a mile our runners were most prominent---Geoff North was 3rd and Gerry North, Thresher, Dance, Hart and Fairclough were in close attendance. What a change at 3 miles when only Gerry North was anywhere near the leaders and was running with his usual flair for the big occasion. From then on it became a dreary procession-only Gerry stayed in touch, the others looking tired and beaten. Tony Fairclough was unfortunate to suffer two falls one of which involved an argument with an irremovable post and in consequence he finished well below his usual placings. Bill Dance also suffered a muscle sprain which impaired his performance. Adrian Major in his first year of Senior competition ran well up to expectations to take 78th place and to show some of his more experienced club mates how it should be done. There are no real excuses-the course was by no means difficult, being dry with fast going and this should have suited us admirably. Gerry was unfortunate in the end not to get his colours again, being selected only as a reserve for the International in Tunisia later in March. Many of our members will consider that 4th place (out of 84 teams to close in) for our Senior team is a good performance but to those of us who know the potential of our runners this was a most disappointing result. This is the third year in succession that we have occupied this position and although we move nearer to the leaders year by year there is no doubt whatsoever that our team this year could with the right preparation and right approach have won handsomely. Low winning scores are now a thing of the past--the days when a club could finish (as we did in 1946 and 1948) its six scorers in the first twenty places are gone. It would need almost a full international team to achieve this now that the standard generally has greatly improved and the size of the fields has gone up from 300 to almost a 1000 in twenty years. It is therefore only necessary to place the scoring six in the first sixty positions with the leading three in the first twenty five--by no means an impossible task for Belgrave with their present runners. Unfortunately the whole approach to a National Cross Country win is indifferent-no one likes to be left out of the team but with a few exceptions the will and spirit to become the top cross country club in Britain is missing. Our teams are made up mainly of individualists (they are proud to call themselves this who do not achieve the fame they envisage in other spheres of athletics and consequently fall between two stools. If they trained wholeheartedly over the country perhaps the best of both worlds could be gained. Think of the prestige to the Club and its members and the resultant invitations to the Continent which are lost by a half hearted approach. We fielded teams in the Youth and Junior races but as we are particularly weak in these classes at the present time there is little to be said except that Peter Blair-Fish who finished 108th in the Junior race ran reasonably well.

SUPERMAC!

The winter of disappointments was well underway. Weren't Belgrave going to win anything? Seven days between fixtures is an eternity and my nerves were already badly strained. I mean, you can beat your wife after a couple of disappointments but you musn't let it show too much. I confess that my behaviour for 3 days after defeat is despicable, but for three days prior to defeat it is less despicable, I have my friends back and we speculate about next Saturday. Wednesday is a hiatus---I just brood. I had been having quite an enjoyable brood this particular hiatus and was almost tempted to unlock my wife from the cellar a day early. Weren't Belgrave ever going to hit the headlines this season, I thought, and idly twiddled the radio knobs. The rasping cacophony of sound was suddenly broken by the 10o'clock "News and Comment" man. Perhaps, I thought, I could devise some means of drugging the opposition next Saturday or-------"Mr. Maclean who is in the studio with us". I cocked one ear. "Mr. Maclean"--I was right, that was the name he'd said---"what are the reasons for this spate of buying in the silver market?". "Well-----"; the dulcet tones of Mac got no further. "It's him" I cried. My saviour had arrived. He had carried our colours over track, country and road; he had carried our written word to the local papers; he had put an inch on Belgrave chests when he dived into the freezing Thames for a rescue and now he was on the air. What a genius he is, why hadn't I thought of it before? But how could he plug Belgrave in a programme discussing the silver market? I sobered down, and Mac's closing remarks gradually became discernible "-----but of course there will soon be greater speculation in gold, particularly claret and gold, Goodnight,and UP THE BELS!". My hero! He'd done it! I'm sure that's what he said and no one will talk me out of it--- least of all my wife, who is eternally grateful.

Mr. Maclean
