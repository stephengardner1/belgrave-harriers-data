EDITORIAL

Dear Fellow Members, May we wish you all a happy, prosperous and successful New Year.

1960 produced a year of tremendous activity in the world of athletics. At all levels the sport was boosted by an Olympic Games which raised standards on to yet another 4 year plateau.

The established International star, if such a person exists, may well find the coming season something of an anti-climax, but the club athlete who aspires to any representative honours will face his contemporaries in a year which will reflect the effect of new Olympic marks in no uncertain manner. The fixture-congested "summer" season now spans from April to Sept., and whether or not this is a good thing, the athlete must be fit early on to make any headway on the athletic scene. He should make his mark long before the arrival of the A.A.A.'s Championships, and then hold his form for most of the season.

To do this, it should be realised that a more determined and scientific approach must be made towards training. No longer is it possibl to ignore hard training in the Winter and Spring months if one wishes to score in the Summer.

It is not necessary, and indeed inadvisable, for the athlete to become the slave of a schedule, but it is important that he undertakes a training programme and adheres to it's general pattern. Any intelligent programme, thoroughly followed will bring forth a high degree of success, provided it's essence is hard work at regular intervals.

We hope the Belgravian of 1961 will show a greater sense of purpose in his training, more determination in his racing, and the desire to bring many individual and team awards back to Belgrave.

HON. SEC'S NOTES

A.A.H.

What a great thrill it was to see our team once again in the lead in a London-Brighton Relay race! True, we could'nt sustain it to the end but we were less than a minute behind the winners when Brighton was reached, and proved to ourselves that all we needed was "a little bit of luck" to give us the premier awards, as of yore.

How much extra effort is needed before history repeats itself with a Belgrave win?

In truth, not very much! A second gained in every mile would have given us a win! Still further reduced; one extra stride in each minute's running by all our men would have carried us to victory. By such small margins are races won or lost--and knowing this secret we can assuredly win, win, win!

All we need is that one extra stride per minute from a fit, determined team!

THE PRESIDENT ~ L.J. Cohen

A.A.H.

Between the wars a great feature of our Winter programme was the series of three-sided cross country fixtures between Ashcombe AC, Thames Valley Harriers and ourselves which took place from the quarters of all three clubs in turn. These were "mob" matches, and everyone who had two legs turned out, and great was the sport and pleasure thereof. In the very early days after World War 1, Thames Valley had prevailed, but gradually Belgrave became stronger and stronger and eventually dominated the competitions. In 1928 we travelled(over thirty of us) to the Valley's quarters at Feltham, and the race was won by Percy Bicknell of TVH, with the long-legged hairpin-thin subject of this article, running in Ashcombe AC colour in second place. Then came the Bels- Pyer, Penny, Duffett, Bill Webb and so on, and we won with 77pts, to the 275 of Ashcombe and 276 of TVH.

A fortnight later the event moved to Wimbledon where there were over 100 starters, 60 of them Belgravians. The winner on this occasion-Les Cohen, followed by Footer, Jack Flower, Duffett, Penny, Allum, Rice, Pyer, with, needless to say, a Belgrave team win of convincing proportions. In the same month Les won the South of the Thames junior race at Epsom, and incidentally, Belgrave had a very satisfying team win!

So we continued to meet and admire "Leslie's" running ability and his sporting qualities for many years, as he did his utmost to keep Ashcombe's colours prominent. When it was apparent that the club could no longer effectively carry on, what was more natural than that Putney-born Leslie Cohen should pull a Belgrave vest over his head?

He served us well on track and over the country and figured in our scoring teams in both the cross country races held just outside Paris, to which our club was invited. He gained Club Championship medals, and was to be found playing his part in the numerous team successes that came our way at that time. He also took an interest in the financial side of the Club's affairs, and that interest has continued throughout the years and still continues. It is largely through his efforts that we own Belgrave Hall and have been able to add the various amenities that we possess today.

After the war, Les decided to play his part on the administrative side of Amateur Athletics, and to serve the sport which had given him so much pleasure. He did this so well that he soon became one of the best-known and certainly best-liked officials in the game. Most years he heads the poll in the election of the Southern Committee of the A.A.A., and the number of honorary positions he holds is legion. He is at the moment President of the English Cross Country Union and of the Southern Counties Cross Country Assoch. He is Championships Hon Secretary of the A.A.A. and Counties Athletic Union, Hon Secretary to the London Business Houses S.A, Championships Hon Secretary to the London County Council and with many another job in addition! Recently he visited Lagos, in Nigeria, to advise on track layout and technical matters generally; and with his usual mixture of good humour and hard work, order was resolved out of chaos!

And now we honour Les as President of the Belgrave Harriers. There has never been a Club member with the same status and influence that Leslie wields within the Athletic circle- and the Club can take a lot of credit from this. At the same time and concurrently with all these activities there have been very few within the Club who have done so much for Belgrave.

It is a great privelege to have Leslie in the Presidential chair. We have done ourselves great honour; and we wish him many years ahead of continuing health and strength to carry on doing those things he does so well, and with so much personal charm.

A.A.H.

THE CLUB DINNER ~ 1960

In an admirable speech proposing the toast of Belgrave Harriers, Mr. J.C. Crump, Honorary Secretary of the B.A.A.B., paid tribute to the Club's struggle in earlier years, and It's present wide representation on the governing bodies of athletics in Britain.

This year's dinner was held at the Ardington Rooms, Clapham Junction, on Nov.5th. Perhaps this would have been an appropriate occasion for the fireworks to fly, but the only controversial issue touched upon was a reference to the International Athletes Club. Mr. Crump referred to "---a Sunday Newspaper journalist, known to us all" who had criticised the A.A.A. for not accepting an Athletes Registration scheme put to them by the I.A.C. "I sometimes wonder", Mr. Crump, "whether we are organising athletics for the thousands of Club athletes throughout the country, or for a handful of stars". Our own Secretary had supported the proposal's rejection.

President L.J. Cohen and Secretary A.A. Harley were spoken of in glowing terms by Mr. Crump, who was given a great ovation.

In his response, Dave Shepherd mentioned the impressive lists of past performances and trophies shown in the Club Handbook, and thanked the many Club officials who carried out their duties"--at their wives' sufferance and employers' expense!!! Our newest vice-President, Ranjit Bhatia, was now back home in India, but our thoughts were with him on this occasion for he would certainly have joined fellow Olympians Stan Vickers and Eric Hall as a Club guest.

In his conclusion, Mr. Shepherd thanked the Social Committee for their year's work and related several amusing incidents that occurred during the Belgrave party's stay in Rome.

In proposing a toast to "Our Guests", our Secretary paid tribute to our Olympic representatives who had performed so well in Rome, and Mr. R. Savage who now had 50 years of membership behind him. We were indeed honoured that our guests should also include such distinguished men as J.C.G. Crump (Hon.Sec.B.A.A.B.), E.H. Clynes (Hon.Sec.A.A.A.), W.Harris (E.C.C.U.), L.W. Woodcock (Hon. Sec.Assocn.London Clubs), P.S. Gale(Treasurer A.A.A.) organiser of the now famous yellow draw tickets!, R. Bacchus of the "News of the World", who had been responsible for so many athletic promotions, and H. Hathway (SLH) whose timekeeping had been of such great assistance.

In his response, Mr. Clynes said he hoped that our new President would emulate a certain well known public figure by ringing home the Bels on the Brighton front to a London-Brighton Relay success, during his term of office.

Our retiring President, T.C. Jones, then gave an account of Les Cohen's successes in active athletics and his rise to the top of the administrative ladder. This year he would also be President of the English Cross Country Union--a great honour for the Club.

After thanking everyone in his typically modest manner, Les Cohen said "I got a great deal out of athletics, and I get a lot of fun putting something back into it. I hope a lot of you will one day do likewise". (These were words that could well have been spoken to the International Athletes Club).

Mr. Cohen presented the "Tommy Green" Cup to S.F. Vickers for the year's most meritorious performance by a Club athlete, and called on Mr. Crump to present the same athlete with the A.A.A.'s "Harvey Memorial" Gold Cup; awarded to the year's best A.A.A. Champion.

Stan Vickers, after remarking how dangerous it was to give gold to a member of the Stock Exchange, promptly "dropped the gold standard" by allowing the Cup's base to fall to the floor! But in his usual pleasant and unruffled manner he was soon paying tribute to his "many helpers"; Ray Hall, Harry Shields and "Old Man" Goswell. "You runners", said Stan, "don't know how lucky you are not to have a man like Goswell around!" But his tongue was stuck firmly into his cheek whilst he spoke.

Dancing was underway by 9pm, and the tinkling of glasses and merriment of the company continued in full swing until midnight.

Thus ended one of the most successful Club dinners ever----thanks to A.T. Jordan and his social committee.

FRANK JARVIS REPORTS:

The building is Belgrave Hall, time 6.30 p.m., Friday, 23rd September 1960. The occasion was not an Open Run nor an Open Walk, but an open night for all Belgravians- the Annual General Meeting for 1960.

Were you there? If you were, these few words will refresh your memory If you were not able to be there, this report of the proceedings will attempt to show what you missed.

The number present was small compared with the actual members, there is no doubt that a way will have to be devised to encourage attendance at the A.G.M., maybe a 'whipper-in' for each section.

The President T.C. Jones, opened the meeting, and the Secretary in presenting his report, spoke of the respect with which the 'Belgrave' are viewed by other clubs and officials in the athletic world. We in Belgrave may criticise the Club, but all outside admire the Club and its activities and know that within a short period the 'Bels' will win the events that seem to count.

Then Syd Roberts, as the Treasurer, presented the Balance Sheet. What accounts! What figures! What an athletic club! This surely, was a balance sheet suitable for a company in the City. It made visible the amount of work that is necessary to organise the Belgrave Harriers, and, maybe, we accept this effort on the part of the officials too easily.

After this financial wizardy, it was pleasant to hear Dave Shepherd's proposal that R.T. Taylor be elected a Life member of the club, and its adoption.

The election of Officers commenced with the retiring President nominating as his successor, Les Cohen, a man, who, perhaps above all others, has contributed to keep Belgrave Harriers financially strong with his football scheme; and he still finds time to do this invaluable work. It is significant that he has also been elected President of the E.C.C.U.

Cont'd. on P.26

Cont'd. from P.5.

The election of other officers followed the normal course, some sections had their plans made with members willing to serve, others had difficulty in finding members to act as officials. The election of the Committee was notable in the fact that only nine names were nominated to fill the ten positions on the committee. Is this the first time that this has occurred?

In many ways this seems to be 'missing the opportunity'. Criticism is aimed at the Committee (and maybe rightly so) for lack of understanding, but, on election night, there are no volunteers to assist in this matter. Members can learn so much from Committee work-Club adminstration, it's finance, arranging meetings and so forth. A good club committee man is the equal of a champion runner or walker, within the Club.

A man who became a good Belgravian in a short time, Ranjit Bhatia, was proposed by Clive Shippen to be made a Vice-President of the Club, and this was adopted amidst acclamation from the members.

Then came that item on the agenda, when anything can happen-"Other business". And it happened. A member asked what action the Officials were going to take about Police competitors running for the Police at open meetings; the status of their 'first claim', including the fact that Police members were not running for the Club. The opinions expressed seemed to indicate that the Police athlete was running for the Police Club instead of his private club at his own request. That there was a definite misunderstanding of the Policeman's position, as an athlete actually competing on behalf of the Police Authority, was apparent.

Each man selected to represent the Police Authority in a sporting event of whatever nature is entitled to four hours time off for the event once a week. If for any reason he is unable to attend the event he cannot claim the four hours privilege time off under any circumstances.

It can be seen that an athlete selected to represent the Police Authority in an athletic event, for which his duty has been facilitated by the grant of the four hours time-off privilege, could be at an event at which his private club have entered a team. The athlete must represent the Police Authority, he must not use the four hours for his private purposes.

As a general rule, however, a policeman athlete who is willing to help himself will obtain assistance from his senior officers to attend events, bearing in mind, though, that Police Duty must come first.

The Chairman then informed the meeting that the matter of Police athletes at Open meetings was being investigated, and there being no other business, closed a successful A.G.M.

In retrospect, it is to be regretted that more members were not there to give their views, and we, all of us, should endeavour to be at the Belgrave Harriers Annual General Meeting next year.

THE POLY. ROAD RELAY

Record upon record was the outstanding feature of the Poly Road Relay. A new Club record of 13:30 set by Jack Brown on the first leg did not survive the afternoon; 30 seconds was lopped off it by Pat Newell on the 5th leg.

Pat's 13:00 mins beat the relay record by 2 secs; but this was not enough to erase Bob Roath's new mark of 12:55 set on the first stage. To complete the records our agregate time was our fastest ever.

First off was Jack Brown. This was his first race since leaving Cyprus and running him with the 'fast-boys' was a gamble--a calulated one. But come off it did, for Jack handed over in third place--behind Roath (Walton) and Day (Ponders End). Eleventh was Eric Gebbett for the 'B' team. Chas Walker with a personal best, replaced Ponders End for second place, whilst Dick Shackleford, although dropping to fifteenth, turned in a surprising 14:19 for the 'B'.

On the third leg Jim Radford had to give best to Poly's Ralph Dunkley (13:21) and Rooke of Essex Beagles (13:36) and handed over to Derek Crookes fourth. Derek on his first run for the 'A' team maintained his position well. Poly and Essex had taken over the second and third places, with Walton still in the lead.

By the time Clive Shippen handed over to Pat Newell the leading positions had altered drastically. Brian Turner (13:17) had taken the Poly into a 22sec lead over Essex Beagles, with the deposed leaders, Walton, a further 16 secs in arrears. Ellis (TVH), had moved ahead of us; but the 'Hill' were our nearest rivals, 45 secs behind.

The splendid run by Pat Newell gained us one place--that of TVH, but there were still 20 secs separating us from the Beagles; whilst Proffitt (Poly) and Llewellyn (Walton) were beyond capture. Jumbo Masters, with a time better than two of our 'A' team had gained the 'B' team two places.

Don Maclean ran well, despite the agonised look on his face and the jack-tar sway of his body near the finish, but Terry Stacey had taken the Valley back into fourth place. The leaders were unchanged.

Mal Robinson on the last lap was clearly not at his best; but with Card (TVH) chasing George Knight (Essex) ahead it was a tough proposition, and we finished 4th. Our 'B' team ran well to finish 10th and collected a set of medals for the first 'B' team to close in; the third time they have gained this award in five years.

BROWN WINS 5¾ ROAD RACE

W.E.L.

A field of about 37 faced the starter for this, the last but one, trial before the London to Brighton Relay on October 8th. Amongst the runners were Ranjit Bhatia, on his last outing from Wimbledon before returning home, Jack Brown, recently returned from Cyprus and already re-establishing himself as a danger man and Cliff Roffey, home on furlough from Kenya.

John Thresher set the pace with Charlie Walker for 2 miles but decided that two laps of the common was far enough and left Charlie to hold the baby! Jack Brown by this time had moved up and Ranjit who had taken a steady start was also prominent. Hallowell, Gebbett, Roffey and Shippen were in close attendance and Peter Shipley, our 2nd claim member from Farnham, was about 20yds further back with Jim Masters.

Down Copse Hill the situation was much the same, but when Cambridge Road was reached Jack Brown went ahead followed by Ranjit; Charlie Walker dropped back to 3rd. This was the order when the Common was again in sight but Cliff Roffey had moved through to 4th. Gebbett, Hallowell and Shippen were still in the hunt, close behind.

Jack came away well to win a good race from Ranjit, who seemed to have suffered from his heavy summer season culminating in two races in the Rome Olympics. He was certainly lacking the fire which produced a club record over this course last April. Charlie Walker had held on to take 3rd place, only 30yds ahead of Roffey who had 60yds to spare on Gebbett.

Generally speaking the race had run to the form book, although it is difficult to reconcile Don Maclean's 35:14 in 30th place with some of his better performances. Brian Sawyer a greatly improving runner, more at home over longer distances, took the first handicap prize with Brian Johnston, John Collier and Charlie Henn sharing second place- how's that for handicapping?!!

SOUTHERN LONDON-BRIGHTON RELAY: VICTORY ELUDES BEST BELGRAVE TEAM FOR YRS.

'Olympian'

The sight of claret and gold vests leading the field on the Brighton road once again gave Belgrave supporters a deserved "shot in the arm". It may not have lasted for long, (about 8 miles) but it provided us with the brightest spot of the day. The Bels eventually came 3rd, putting up one of their best performances on this course for many years, and finishing a mere 51secs behind the winners (TVH) and 41 secs down on the runners up and holders (Portsmouth).

It all began at 10am in Old Palace Yard, Westminster where twenty runners including Eric Gebbett lined up to await the first stroke of Big Ben. We have become used to seeing Eric taking our first stage and as in April he performed well. Nothing detracts Eric from his steady running and this time he managed to arrive at Clapham Common without mishap in 5th place, 27 secs behind Portsmouth, who had taken a good lead which they were destined to keep most of the way to Brighton (Gebbett 21:25; 5th fastest time).

Charlie Walker set off on the second leg in great style with Eddie Ellis of TVH breathing down his neck. These two battled throughout and soon disposed of Walton, Blackheath and Highgate but Ealing represented by Blagrove, Brighton with Briault, and Woodford with Chapman were making faster progress from behind and eventually passed our runner. Charlie eventually gave over to Pat Newell in sixth place with a deficit of 42 secs on Portsmouth (Walker, 25:58; 10th fastest time).

Pat Newell, who seems to like this third leg, set about closing the gaps and soon took us up to 2nd place in grand style. R.C.H.

In what must have been the most exciting team race for years (only 10 points covered the first 5 teams) Belgrave were edged to an uncharacteristic 4th.

Why has this race glamour? Why has this race such popularity? The early pace of Don Thompson was amazing to watch---especially from behind! and he soon moved well away and out of sight. Poor George Beecham had one of those very rare races (for him) where the distance must have seemed like 100 miles. Between Crawley and Brighton he was forced to drop 11 places. It was ironical that his misfortune should contribute towards the excitement of the team contest.

The "Belgrave" Cup was retained by Ray Hall, rather unfit after his Roman holiday, with John Morris gaining his first runners-up medal in a Club Championship. George held on to gain the 3rd medal, and John Keown made up the scoring four.

Has anybody a spare pair of legs for Harry Shields still suffering from a large slice of Roman history?!

SPOTLIGHT: RANJIT BHATIA

L.E.R.

As the hot Roman sun sank to the west, casting the Stadia Olympia into shadow, Ranjit Bhatia stepped on to the brick-red track for an Olympic 5,000 metres heat. At 24 years of age Ranjit had gained the highest award attainable by an athlete-that of representing his country at an Olympic Games.

Ranjit Bhatia was born in the Middlesex hospital on the 27th May 1936. A year later he had moved to Eastern India where he lived until 1948, when that part of the country became Pakistan. Those troubled times forced him to depart to Delhi in great haste, where, four years later, his athletics career commenced.

When in 1957, he left India for a two-year mathematics scholarship at Oxford he had already won the Indian Universities' 1500 metres (4:10.6) and placed second in the 800 metres (1:58.4); but these times were no real guide to his future.

During his first year he did not find the change of climate to his liking-and in any event studies came first-but even so he reduced his best mile time to 4:16.0 when running for O.U.A.C. v Middx and had won his first Club medal when finishing third in Belgrave's one mile championship.

Contrary to popular belief about Asian athletes, Ranjit seemed to prefer the longer middle-distance events, and after a winter season of rather mixed fortunes he was a member of our second placed team in the Ilford Road Relay, April 1959. Most of his winter training had been done on the track and by May he had reached his peak with 52.3, 1:57.1, 4:15.5 and 9:16.0.

It was fortunate for Belgrave, and India too for that matter, that the next 2 months were spent at intensive study; for Ranjit did so well in his finals that he was awarded a year's continuation for research at Oxford. For the remainder of the summer he left the track alone, "I was bored with racing, so I left it and went to Sweden for a holiday".

In the autumn of 1959 he changed his training methods and in the company of such notable athletes as Stephen James, Paddy Montague and Alistair Wood, trained exclusively on the country. Each day he lapped round a circuit which included several steep hills, and by the New Year he was considerably strengthened.

In the early months of 1960 he established himself as one of our leading country runners, being our second man home in both the South of Thames Senior (39th) and Southern (61st) and winning the Oxfordshire County Championship. He steadily improved and came into his own when the road relay season arrived.

In the Ilford Road Relay he was the fastest man of our winning team and became the second ever Belgravian to break 18 mins in that race. The following week he reduced Pat Newell's record by the fantastic margin of 25secs in the Club 5¾ mile Road Race Championship, recording 27:56.6--a time that should stand for many years.

Then came the "National" London Brighton Relay. Commenting on the previous autumn's Southern race "Olympian" had written"---a moderate performance with a few bright spots in the form of Bhatia, Black and Dabbs." Ranjit had raced in third on the first leg. Six months later he was promoted to the 9th stage of the "National", and Olympian's report read"---with a time of 28:56 (third fastest of the day) sliced off half a minute from the Club record, besides being faster than such seasoned stars as Gordon Pirie and Bruce Tulloh". Most will remember this as his finest performance; it was a lesson in relaxed, even-paced running.

And so to the Olympic year's track season. Initially he had no thoughts towards Olympic selection, but a 4:08.7 and 13:57 on successive days in the Oxford University Championships in April had many thinking otherwise. In the space of the next four months he had run 3 miles thirteen consecutive times under 14:12--a time only one other Belgravian had ever beaten!! During this period he set a new record in the U.A.U. Championships with 13:56, recorded 13:57 in the inter-Counties, was runner-up in the "Kinnaird" to Alistair Wood with 14:02, and on several occasions raced two "threes" in one week---consistency of running which many faster three-milers would envy.

When told he was Asia's fastest miler Ranjit realised that Olympic selection was more than just a possibility; but on learning that the team had already been nominated he gave up the idea. Bob Taylor thought otherwise and advised him to contact Les Cohen whose communication to the Indian Olympic Committee resulted in Ranjit being selected for the 5,000 metres. A vacancy existed in the 10,000 metres event as well, so Ranjit decided to use the Club '6' as a trial, instead of competing in Scotland over the shorter distance. He readily agreed to run alternate laps with Dabbs and Robinson, but although winning the race in a new Championship best performance of 29:50.6 he apologised to one of the competitors for not running his laps fast enough!---such is his modesty.

Three weeks later he ran in Rome. It would be nice to record that he won through his heat, but he didn't. His peformance dropped in the same sort of proportion as did those of Britain's 5,000 metres runners; although his only comment was "I didn't feel up to it" and the matter closed!

Thus we say goodbye to an athlete who endeared himself to us all. It was to honour not only his running prowess but also his modesty and love of Belgrave Harriers that at the A.G.M. 1960 he was elected a Vice-President.

L.E.R.

'IT'S ALL A PROBLEM'.....OR.. When in Rome do as the Belgravians do!

M.E.R.

As our train gathered speed and Dennis Logan gave a last cry of "arevederci" to the fast disappearing figure of his daughter on the platform, we settled into our seats to see what the newspaper pundits had to say of the XVIII Olympiad. Our trip to Rome had begun.

According to our schedule it should take 36 hours to reach the "City of the seven hills"; and exactly to the minute we arrived. Having got there trouble-free we näiveley assumed there would be no difficulties, but it was in Rome that trouble reared it's ugly head. "Your accommodation" said the most genial, but rascally, Italian ever to be an assistant agent, "has been moved to a lovely villa, not far away". The Villa Gracia was indeed lovely, but over two miles from the City centre and quite out of the way for the stadia.

After two nights of Tom Carter and friend Reggie initiating us into the art of late night habits, and giddy climbing over 10 feet high barred gates at 2 a.m., we moved to the Pensione Adriana. This was just outside the station(a magnificent example of modern architecture) and was indeed very comfortable.

"Black Wednesday" was our first day at the Stadia Olympica. Hewson out---Rowe out---Salvat and Ranjit Bhatia out; what a start! and then Bruce Tulloh, after a tremendous fight, was also eliminated. But saddest of all we saw the slender, loping figure of Gordon Pirie----the only man to stop the incessant "RA!RA!RA! GERMANIA!" chant of the Germans----finish outside the first six.

As we trooped out of the Stadium, a dejected band, the confusion over transport did little to improve our feelings. However, we couldn't remain glum for long, there was so much to see and do. This, Harry Shields emphasised each morning when he mapped out the day's schedule for his family. St. Peter's by 11am, then the Vatican, the Castel S.Angelo and lunch, and then on to the Stadium. "Can we get in the Parthean? we'll see; it's all a problem"!! A problem it was.

A night visit to the Colloseum was a must. One's imagination could easily cast off the ruined appearance and visualise a vast marble stadium housing thousands of Romans clad in white togas, gesticulating at the figures in the arena to the accompaniment of lions roaring in the dungeons below; whilst Nero (fiddle and all!) turned down his thumb.

Rome is not all ruins, or rather, out of the ruins there often rises beauty. The open air performance of Verdi's opera "Aide" at the Baths of Caracala was indeed spell-binding. Spell-binding too was the view seen from the flat roof of St. Peters, where, looking over the stone balustrade of small sculptured figures, could be seen the Tiber meandering past Castel and hill. The inside of St. Peter's was in itself a wonder. Every stone, mural or font was studded with history. "Christ leant against that colonnade whilst preaching; this mural Michaelangelo painted for his life; this stone------; that window-------," so much for a lay-man to absorb.

But all roads led to the Stadia when the noon-day heat was down. "Gee! burn those cinders Morton!", "Come on Radford!", "Viva Berrutti!" could be distinguished from the cheers of the many Nationalities all fervently pleading for their favourites; and each athlete strained even harder to do justice to his support, eager to gain a coveted Olympic medal and all aware that only one man can be the winner. As the evening drew on the hot sun slipped behind the Stadium walls and the ensuing darkness was relieved only by the electric scoreboard and the Olympic flame which fluttered continuously through rain and shine.

In a two weeks athletics programme there was so much to see that it is difficult to bring forward all the memorable moments. Though, Thompson striding to a great win in the 50 Kilos walk; Radford flashing to a bronze medal in the 100 metres; Elliott toying with the world's best over 1500 metres; "wild-man" Cerutty running on to the track; Avril Williams throwing the javelin beyond the Olympic record and into second place, only in her excitement, to step over the line and be disqualified; Halberg jumping the 5,000 metres field for a 3:11:0 last 3 laps (60,65,66); Kryzkowiak winning the steeplechase exactly how he wished; the three Americans, after taking all three medals in the "shot", parading around the arena in complete disregard of the officials; the tall lithe negro Otis Davis jumping with abandon when hearing that his winning time in the 400 metres was a world record, and Robbie Brightwell with a new British record failing by the narrowest of margins to qualify for the 400 metres final, come instantly to mind.

Most memorable, of course, was the sight of our own Stan Vickers striding gamely into the Stadium to take third place in the 20 Kilos walk. Our 6'X 3' Union Jack which had not been seen very frequently during the Games was waved till the shaft broke! We cheered and cheered, and before there was any quietning Eric Hall appeared in 10th place, his Legionare cap hiding his distorted face as he finished his final effort. It was difficult to know who caused the most attraction, Stan and Eric, or ourselves. Our neighbours were not quite certain for whom we cheered "Up the Bels., B-E-L-G-R-A-V-E, Belgrave!" but their cameras clicked and their movies whirred just the same.

And so to those evergreen moments on the social side. Dave Shepherd and Win with Harry Shields and family in the land of spectacle, romance, music and delicious wines, crowding round a small parafin stove on the marble floor for a "brew-up"!; a certain gentleman choosing this particular fortnight in this particular country to have 4 stitches inserted in his head, when no free health-service existed; the look of utter surprise on the face of the East German 1500 metres star S.Valentin, as we galloped past during a hard training session on the sands of the Lido, and the sight of Valentin getting up from his reclining position to train in the opposite direction----mad dogs and Englishmen etc! These, and many more memories come crowding in.

As always our last day soon arrived, and as we filed past the beaming Pensione woman, the rascally agent Angelo (what a name!) and on to the Continental Express, our Roman holiday was brought to an end.

THE CLUB SEVEN

R.L.

A field of 28 took part in this event held on one of the most miserable afternoons of the winter. The start was fairly fast with first Mike Stearman and then Eric Gebbett enjoying a brief few seconds of glory. By 600 yards however Charlie Dabbs was in command with a fair sized bunch of runners all lying handy. Alan Black and Mal Robinson were soon level with Charlie and for the first two miles these three fought it out with never more than a few yards in it. At two miles Alan gradually crept in front with Mal and Charlie only a few yards down.

At the end of the first lap Alan (20.53) led Mal (20.55) with Charlie a further 4 secs down. Jack Brown (21.10) was also fairly well up. After these four the field became rather strung out with Hallowell, Johnson and Gebbett filling the next three positions. Stearman, the initial leader, was back in 14th place, and our old friend Macdonald although well down the field was in fine form, vocally that is, and let fly a sizzling stream of abuse at Bill Lucas who will in future know better than to offer "Mac" any advice in the middle of a race! Another to distinguish himself in an unorthodox way was Bob Glover who retired simply because he wanted to watch the leaders finish!

Over the final lap the positions remained practically the same except that the gaps increased but Eric Gebbett, now getting fit again after a bout of gastro-enteritis, made up two places.

At the finish both Alan and Malcolm appeared to be quiet fit and strong and one can only hope that this result will give Alan a little bit of confidence; which he seems to have lacked in the past. On the debit side mention must be made of the gap between the first four and the remainder of the field; four good men unsupported will get us nowhere in the major races

CROSS-COUNTRY CAPTAIN'S REVIEW

C.S.

Since the last issue of the Belgravian we have both welcomed and bade farewell to Gordon McKenzie of the United States. Belgrave Harriers were not exactly unknown to Gordon. How could they be when his wife is none other than the former Chris Slemon of Selsonia Ladies A.C., and close friend of Betty Jones, our immediate past-president's wife.

Although their post-Olympic stay with Belgrave lasted but 4 weeks, most active Belgravians have had the pleasure of the McKenzies' acquaintance We hope Gordon's memories of the London-Brighton Relay, two races in Belgrave colours, a Saturday evening social, a Sunday ramble, and the Club dinner will be sufficiently pleasant to persuade him to visit us again soon.

Ranjit Bhatia, who ran against Gordon in the Olympic marathon is now teaching at St. Stephen's College, Delhi; and Cliff Roffey, who ran so well in the "Brighton" is now back in Kenya for another 3 year tour of duty.

Talking of the "Brighton", what a heartening sight it was to see Jack Brown handing over to Ron Linstead! The former has got back into his running most impressively after his 3 year absence whilst Ron has burst into the limelight in most sensational fashion. Many of us were aware of his potential, but his reluctance to compete unless he considered himself 100% fit and at the height of form led one to believe his intentions were not really serious----until the Belgrave "Open 20" that is. His 6th place in 1:51:49 was a definite warning to the Country's distance runners-----a warning which he underlined, in no uncertain fashion, with 3rd places in the "S.LH. 30" and "London/Brighton" runs. The shorter distance athletes were not exempt from this treatment either, for he chalked up fastest time by far on the Redhill stage of the "Brighton" relay, and was only 3 secs slower than Alan Black in our own Yacht '3'. Unfortunately, Ron is rather injury-prone and will miss the chance of gaining his county colours this January. However, the main part of the season is yet to come and he should find some tough cross-country races a valuable background to his road-running, as have Jack Holden, Jim Peters and Fred Norris before him!

We hope to see Charlie Walker out again in the New Year. The tragic loss of his 4 year old daughter in October has naturally curtailed his activities. Several of the lads contributed towards a floral tribute and sent their sincerest condolences to Chas. and Betty, whose young son Stephen helped them magnificently in their time of sorrow.

Pat Newell had his first outing of the season in the Match against Mitcham and in his first race for 11 weeks completely pulverised a strong field. His excellent form during the track and road-relay seasons suggests he could well occupy forward positions in the Southern and National Championships. Pat has helped a great deal in "carrying" the Bels through their lean spell these last few years. It rather looks as though he'll be getting the support he deserves---perhaps this will raise him to new heights.

Turn-outs for our fixtures have so far not been very pleasing. If 35 can regularly appear in our own closed races, surely we should average 25 at our inter-club fixtures; the present figure is more like 15. Those who rarely miss a fixture include George Piddington, Chris Fairfield Dennis Jones, Eric Penny and Mike Stearman, with another half-dozen not far behind. It is to be hoped that this nucleus will shortly be supplemented by most of our leading runners, who should now be tuning up for the Southern and National events.

Charlie Dabbs is another to have joined our long list of "temporarily inactive" runners. Suspected pleurisy has restricted Chas's training for at least 8 weeks, but he should be back in form for the Southern. Most improved runners of the season are at present Ken Miller, Alan Black and, Junior Cyril Forey; but perhaps there are some dark horses waiting for the final run-in before they show their hand---Mike Heck, Jim Radford, David Jones and John Thresher for example.

By the time you read this the County Championships will have been decided. We could well have Jack Brown (Kent), Pat Newell (Middx) and one of our Surrey team competing in the Inter-Counties at Bingley.

May 1961 bring to you all, the greatest of success-----this, of course will call for a little harder work all round!

BLACK & ROBINSON MAKE SURREY TEAM

Running on home ground, over a fast three lap course, Alan Black and Malcolm Robinson ran themselves into the Surrey County team at Bingley with 8th and 10th places respectively, at Wimbledon on January 7th.

After one mile Ken Miller, Mal and Alan were together around 10th position; and at the end of the first lap these three were still with the leaders, Mal in 2nd place, Alan 10th with Ken just behind; but for fourth scoring man we had to go back to Clive Shippen in 52nd place - seemingly ruling us out of any team award.

With one lap to go the leading group had consolidated, but dropping Ken; but Clive had made up nineteen places and Eddie Short and Eric Gebbett were pegging back places also.

On the final lap, R.Langridge (S.L.H.) got away from the field to win by ten seconds; but the next nine places were sorted out only after a desperate fighting finish - 10 seconds covering all nine. Ken held on for 16th place and the Bels took third team award, after a 5 year spell of being "also-rans".
