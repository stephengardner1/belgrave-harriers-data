Editorial · page 3

COMMENT

It is with great sorrow that we have to record the death of Alf. Harley, a man who worked tremendously hard for his Club and athletics in general and who in return was held by all who knew him in high regard. Whatever the event, Alf. Harley always seemed to be there and it is almost impossible to visualise Belgrave's future functions without his dignified presence. The tributes to him have been many and included in this gazette are personal memories of those who have worked alongside him for many years and those who knew him only comparatively recently, some in length and some extracts from letters. All make plain the fact that in the history of Belgrave Harriers there has never been a greater man and there probably never will be.

Alf. Harley

Column · page 4

GRAPEVINE

When ALF HARLEY departed from us in January it was requested that no flowers should be sent to his funeral. Donations instead were to go to the Mount Vernon Hospital where Alf spent his last days. Spontaneous contributions were received from all quarters of the Club and we were able to send a cheque for £38.00 to Mrs. Harley for forwarding to the hospital. Alf's funeral was held on Monday 15th January at Brakespeare Crematorium, Ruislip - the same day as the funeral of our other Past President, SYD ROBERTS, which was held at St. Annes on Sea, Lancs. Although the majority of mourners were at Ruislip - 90 attended - the thoughts of all Belgravians were also with Syd on that cold and wet January day; a black day indeed for Belgravians.

Are the County championship events losing their appeal? This years Surrey Junior and Youth races at Morden Park certainly seem to indicate that things are not all they ought to be. In the Youth race only three teams managed to close in but the Junior race was even worse - not one complete team made it to the finishing funnel.

The South of the Thames Senior cross country race was held on Wimbledon Common towards the end of January and it was good to see so many Bels out to help mark the course and act as pointsmen. Which ever way one turned,it seemed, familiar figures were there armed with flags to guide the 300 odd runners around the course.

News from Delhi, where RANJIT BHATIA has been nominated a member of the A.A.F.I. Committee to prepare plans for the reorganisation of athletics in India. He has also managed to persuade the A.A.F.I. to send two men to compete in the 1973 Maxol Marathon and is in fact going to concentrate on the marathon himself this year.

Even further afield, in Australia, MICK HARVEY (21, Mount Street, Coogee, N.S.W. 2034) and MARTIN ELLIOTT (13, Forest Way, French's Forest, N.S.W. 2086) both seem to be settling down to Sydney life. They live a mere half hours drive apart and who knows, this note may well encourage them to get together for a training session. Could we yet see an antipodean section of Belgrave being formed.

" A shake up for Surrey athletics? " cried a headline in the "Surrey Comet" of February 3rd, and rumours of club amalgamations are in the news again, this time between Surrey A.C., Walton A.C. and Epsom & Ewell H. The article went on to say that this could result in Surrey's first (?) 'super-club' and that both Surrey and Walton are suffering from declining membership and saw amalgamation as a possible solution. Latest gossip, however, hints that Epsom & Ewell H., already 400 strong and doing well, are not really interested. We await developements with great interest.

At the last count, taken at the end of 1972, it was found that Belgrave Harriers had 453 members on the books, comprised of 327 seniors, 29 juniors, 15 youths, 48 boys and 34 honorary members. Let us hope that many of our swelling band of boys will stay with us through to senior status, strengthening our Junior and Youth Sections on the way.

Although meeting on the sad occasion of Alf Harley's funeral, Jack Goswell was very pleased to see FRED RICKARDS looking so well and hearty and defying his 73½ years. They last met in the mid-50's when Jack looked after him and his pupil George Checkley in his last walk in Club colours (was it the Birmingham Outer Circle 25 miles or the Leicester Mercury 20 miles race) and Jack well remembers his belief in 'rice pudding'. Fred joined the club in January 1924 and during the years 1924/39 he won the Police Barking to Southend Walk no fewer than eight times. His very distinguished career also included - 1930 Norwich to Ipswich 42 miles,1st in 7:11:48; 1931 and 1933 Guildford Cup 50 kilos, 1st; member of winning team in the National 50 kilos 1934/5/6/8. Fred moved from the Slough area some years ago and now lives at 19, Davids Avenue, Palm Bay, Cliftonville, Kent, and how well the sea air must suit him. He would be pleased to hear from his old friends and walkers, particularly from TOMMY GREEN a great friend of his. How about it Tommy? He did travel to Southampton some time ago to look you up but had the old address and was unsuccessful. Tommy's address is believed to be 19, Consort Road, Eastleigh, Hants. So Fred, pleased to see you again and trust you keep looking so fit and well, and hear again from your old friends.

Another who wishes to be remembered to his old friends is ERNIE DUFFETT. He and his wife are now living at 42, Springfield Court, Church Village, Pontypridd, S.Wales and are thoroughly enjoying themselves down in West Wales. Ernie is the Secretary of the local Senior Citizens Club and hopes to hold that office for as long as he was Running Hon. Sec. for the Bels - 25 years! He passes on his best wishes and says that he is with us in spirit if not in person.

The end of an athletic era draws to a close as ex-Belgravian SANDY LAW closes up his shoe making business to move to Eastbourne. For many years athletes have gone to Sandy's shop in Revelstoke Road, Wimbledon for a pair of hand made to measure running shoes, possibly his most famous customer being Roger Bannister, the first man to run a mile in under four minutes. Unfortunately in today's world individual manufacturers like Sandy find it increasingly difficult to compete with the mass production of shoes by the giants of the sports shoe business.

Former Belgrave quarter miler JOHN COPE, who helped us to many inter-club wins in the days of Harry Shields' track captaincy, was recently appointed Deputy Chief Industrial Relations Officer by the London Transport Executive.

The ubiquitous WILF WESCH is now Manager of the Sport Hotel Adidas at 8522 Herzogenaurach, Beethovenstrasse 6, West Germany. The luxurious hotel is equipped with facilities for a real sportsman's holiday. Wilf and Anne hope to be visiting England again shortly but in the meantime extend a warm invitation to Belgravians who are promised "an extremely comfortable stay".

Congratulations to CARL LAWTON and best wishes to BARBARA INKPEN on the occasion of their marriage on March 10th. Let us hope that being married to a Belgravian, and a real 'case'at that, will not affect Barbara's international career as a high jumper.

More congratulations are due, this time to PETER WRENCH and his wife ANN. Ann has recently given birth to a daughter, their second child. Rumours that Peter is only doing so well at work because he named his son Robin after his boss Lord Rubens are quite untrue.

ALFRED A. HARLEY

Harry Shields

Harry Shields writes:-

When the Vicar who was to officiate at the cremation telephoned for some details and to ask what I considered Alf's greatest characteristics, my mind went to my schooldays in which we learnt extracts from the Bible, more particularly that piece which includes, " and the greatest of these is Charity". In the new English Bible, in the First letter of Paul to the Corinthians, Chapter 13, I note this now reads:- "Love is patient; love is kind and envies no one. Love is never boastful, nor conceited, nor rude; never selfish or quick to take offence; does not gloat over other mens sins, but delights in the truth." The other quotation which came to mind was:- " Life is made up, not of great sacrifices or duties, but of little things, in which smiles and kindness and small obligations given habitually, are what win the heart and secure comfort ". Those of us who were able to attend the funeral of this great "Belgravian" could not fail to be impressed with the splendid and thoughtful "Oration" given by the vicar outlining Alfred's career and many qualities. His devotion to his Club, Belgrave Harriers. Administrative qualities so thoroughly and competently carried out in the whole spectrum of athletics. His outstanding career during the War years with the R.A.F. and the human understanding he displayed. The high regard in which he was held by his firm and business colleagues and the esteem of other organisations to which he belonged. Above all the love he had as a husband and father. In the September 1970 edition of the "Belgravian", Clive Shippen wrote a splendid "Spotlight" to mark the end of Alfred Harley's second term as President of the Club in recognition of his 26 years as Secretary and 44 years of service to the Club. Those members who read this "Spotlight" cannot fail to have been inspired by the unique qualities it portrayed and that the "Epitaph" of his passing must surely be "It is well finished". Looking round at the large number who paid homage at his funeral, there were many from Athletic Bodies, from his firm and other organisations in which he held high office, but it was clear that first and foremost he was 100% Belgravian, in its full sense with no sectional bias. There were many whose presence I missed, but knew they had written wonderful letters to Alf's wife and family. The regard with which the Club is held by his family is only second to that held by Alf himself, and I have been asked to express their deep thanks for the support and many kindnesses they received during his illness. All age groups of the Club were represented but I especially noticed those from Alf's earlier days, in the 1930's, Ken O'Kell, Bill and Frank Webb, Tom Carter, Tom Morrell, Albert Shaw, Tom Cotton, Tommy Scrimshaw and many others. I wondered at the thoughts going through their minds and how Alf would have loved to be there, shake their hands and have thoughts similar to my own. Tom Cotton, who, in the pouring rain at Shepperton camp, gave an exposition lasting many hours warning us younger members of the wiles and wickedness of women. Ken O'Kell for his enormous enthusiasm in introducing new members. Frank and Bill Webb for their comradeship on those wonderful all day strolls over Ranmore and Leith Hill, with Frank no matter what the weather, emerging debonair as ever. Tommy Scrimshaw for his outstanding athletic career and tussles with the great Tommy Hampson at Stamford Bridge. To each and everyone, Alf's memories would be of the great times we had and the pleasure from each others company, the arguments we had and above all the enormous team spirit for the Club to succeed. For myself the outstanding memories of Alf are peace. Sitting on the banks of the river Fal, listening to the lap of the waves, watching the sunset, waiting for the Ferry to take us to the other side. Resting on Rinsey Head, near an old Cornish Tin Mine, in beautiful autumnal warmth and sunshine, watching the sea-gulls and shag-greens on the rocks, enjoying the colour of the heather and bracken and, except for the noise of the sea and birds, complete quietness and solitude. I am sure these reflect the qualities deep inside Alf, the love of comradeship, beauty and serenity. His passing will leave a large gap in many of our lives and his memory will always be with us. An influence, impossible to evaluate, is how he helped to develop many of our lives. He was educated at the Upper Latymer School, Hammersmith when many of us had to leave school at 14 years to find our way through life. His inspiration was the first experience many of us had to carry out administrative duties and service to our fellows. Much of any success we may have later achieved emanates from his example. As well as I knew Alf it was not until his funeral that I learnt he was a "Freeman of the City of London". Such was his humility. It all brings to mind that lovely song:- "If I can help somebody as I pass along then my living will not be in vain". Our deepest condolences go to his wife and family and we thank the Supreme Being for the opportunity of enjoying his comradeship and the benefit of his talents.

A.A. Harley

Gordon S. Doubleday

When I turned up at our old mid-week training headquarters, the "Rising Sun", Ebury Bridge Road, in the thirties I was greeted by a tall red-headed fellow and welcomed to the Club. Thus began a friendship which was sadly ended by the death of Alf. Harley. Alf believed that amateur sport played a part in our lives, his chosen sport was athletics, and the club of his choice - Belgrave. Not only was he an active athlete, first as a runner then as a walker but he was also Walking Hon. Sec., Hon. Editor of the "Belgravian" and on the Committee. He was obviously a man of great administrative and organising talents and these were unstintingly given both to Belgrave and other athletic organisations. He had a deep admiration for what people like Bob Ricketts, Jimmy Belchamber, Oscar Horwood and many others had done to make Belgrave Harriers one of the strongest clubs in the country. Not only were our teams bringing championship honours to the Club but our administration and organisation were on a sound basis. He threw himself wholeheartedly into anything associated with the Club - a Social or Dance would find him there, if a few banded together to go to a "show" in town there he was. He was a great believer in the all-day Sunday stroll and was one of the regulars of a group of Belgravians who would tramp many miles over tough country in the Home Counties. These strolls sometimes extended into week-ends at youth hostels, indeed many of his holidays were spent with a Belgrave party. Expressing his ideas and thoughts came easily to him whether written or spoken, one of his favourite topics being the future well being of the Club. We know that his views were sincerely held, and he in turn always respected other peoples views. The Second World War saw the majority of members doing some form of National Service and Alf joined the R.A.F. where his administrative gifts were put to good use. He rose to the rank of Squadron Leader. He maintained close touch with the Club by corresponding with those at home and when the war was over he was soon back in his job of General Hon. Secretary. In due course he was honoured by being elected an Hon. Life Member, a Vice-President then President. His election as President for a second term of office is unique in Club history. It must have been one of his proudest moments when as President for the second time Alf and his wife Nancy greeted members and guests at the 1970 Annual Dinner. He was on many committees right up to the top levels of athletic administration and thus he had an unrivalled knowledge of both athletes and clubs, and his opinions on Committee were always closely listened to. When he retired from active participation he would invariably be found judging or doing some other important job. In fact we can say that for nany years no Belgrave event or committee meeting would be complete without him. He was known to many as Mr. Belgrave. On a personal plane he was universally liked, a word for everybody, a laugh, a joke, or, if needed, a word of sympathy when the occasion arose. He was a representative of a large firm of printers and travelled a good deal and picked up a wide knowledge of all sorts of subjects and could hold his own in conversation whatever the topic. By encouraging others he played a large part in the Happy Club we are proud to be members of. Let us hope that the work and enthusiasm of Alf Harley will be as much an inspiration to others as was the inspiration of those whose example Alf so faithfully admired and followed. There are many who feel that they have not only lost a fellow Belgravian but also a warm and loyal friend who radiated sincerity and good fellowship.

Gordon S. Doubleday

A.A. Harley

Frank Simmons

Many words will have been spoken and perhaps many written more eloquently than my written respects towards Alf Harley. I first met Alf in the winter of 1935 and was, as a young man, deeply impressed by his desires and sound reasoning on behalf of Belgrave Harriers. Having been on many holidays with Alf, walking over many parts of England, I feel that I got to know him well. To listen to his reasoning and wisdom made me realise what a great Club personality we had. A person with a gentle but strong approach to various subjects, his wonderful ability to calm slightly heated discussions and to guide committees upon a good path to decisions, his democratic insistance that even the newest or the youngest should be allowed to air their views, made him into one of the Great Belgravians. I am proud to be able to say with many more, "I knew Alf Harley".

Frank Simmons

Obituary · page 9

A.A. Harley

Peter Hilliar

It is particularly difficult to realise that Alf Harley is no longer with us in Belgrave. He was a veritable embodiment of all that the Club represented and stood for and his untimely death has left us with a gaping wound which will be difficult to plug. My first experience of Alf in action came when I was elected to the Belgrave Committee in September 1966. I had written to him a few months previously on a matter of club finance and although I was probably unknown to him at that time he had replied with his customary courtesy and in great detail. It seemed to me that no matter was too trivial to engage his interest. At that time, he suggested that I should stand for election to the Committee at the following A.G.M. I was immediately impressed by his confident handling of Committee Meetings, not to mention General Meetings. What is more, he had a tremendous memory and a passion for detail that none could surpass. I never remember seeing him flustered at any time and the greater the odds against him the greater his confidence. You may recall that I was bold enough to volunteer to succeed Alf. as General Hon. Secretary in September 1970. After all, it seemed to be all so easy with Alf in the hot seat. I was soon to find out that it was by no means as easy as I had imagined but my transitional year was greatly assisted by Alf's second spell of presidential duty. He was always willing to give a helping hand and I can well remember his "little notes", which served to remind me of club business I might otherwise have missed. Needless to say, the notes sometimes amounted to four or five pages. Given Alf's immense breadth of knowledge on all facets of amateur athletics, it might be supposed that he was unapproachable but nothing could be further from the truth. He was always only too pleased to sort out a point of legal or administrative detail and to assist a less experienced colleague who might otherwise have given way to panic. Even after he had retired from the Committee "top table", which he had occupied for some thirty-odd years, he was to be seen at Committee and Club athletic meetings through thick and thin and I cannot remember having seen him absent from two in succession. I consider it a great privilege to have served the Club at the same time as a man of Alf's calibre. I feel we shall not see his like again but Belgrave will be able to take advantage of the tremendous volume of work he put in for the Club.

Peter Hilliar

A.A. Harley

Reg Hopkins

If you can walk with Kings and yet keep the common touch Rudyard Kipling.

As we go through life we meet many varied types of people. Some come into our orbit and pass without any apparent effect upon us. Others with some special quality have a much greater influence. It is not always possible to analyse what these qualities are but we all recognise them when we are in the presence of someone who has them, and we listen carefully to what they may have to say, knowing it will be of value. Such a person was Alf Harley. Watching Alf at work on committee or indeed at any other time we learnt a tremendous amount about how to conduct our affairs and at the same time glean much about human relationships. Alf was a living symbol of the democratic way of life. In my work with young people it is my earnest hope that what I have learnt from him, I can in some way pass on to them and like the proverbial stone in the pond, the reverberations will go on and on, perhaps the best memorial of all.

Reg Hopkins

Report · page 11

WALKING

Carl Lawton

An 80% success was the result of the Open '7's with wins in the Luton, Belgrave, Cambridge and Met. Police races. Our only unfortunate error was in the Enfield but we still managed third. All this was achieved without any individual win and purely on team effort. Our highest placing came from Carl Lawton with third in the'Cambridge'. Len Duquemin, John Moullin and Ray Middleton together with Carl were our ever consistant team despite an attempt by Allan Callow to split things in the 'Cambridge'.

Other consistant performances during the winter came from John Hall and Tom Cook. John for his consistant appearance and Tom for his consistant non-appearance. John progressed erratically and it was not until January that he began to smell 56 minutes. Since then he has meandered rather but if more time could be spent in races concentrating, he would improve beyond recognition.

Martin Bennett was another to star midfield with some excellent performances being thrown in. His best efforts were generally produced when he arrived late for race. Changing behind the fence at Imber Court and running down Enfield High Street at 2.26pm to mention just two.

John Dunsford had his days as did Dave McMullen, in between his complaints about his performances, despite his gold G.L.C. medal. Ray Hall began to regain his fitness but a stomach virus put paid to that. Len Evans, now prominent in Belgrave colours, showed that he could be reliable as one of our reserves in the summer season.

Bernard Eglington made the long trip down from North Wales for the Belgrave Open '7'. Bob Picton showed just a little of what he could so easily be capable of in the Met. Police '7' and Bob Carter and Doug. Fotheringham were generally oblivious but present.

John Bromley went into hospital for an operation in January and is now on the road to recovery and full fitness one hopes. There is still life in the old dog yet, especially with those veteran's prizes looming.

Johnny Morris and John Keown could not decide as to which of them was the most unfit.

The appearance of Eric Stroud could bring a bit of depth to our long distance side as could Paul Wiltshire to our sprint team. Pat Duncan did manage to put on a set of gear and compete in club races although questions were asked as to who the friend was under his shirt.

Welcome appearances were made by Percy Wilson and John Johnson, both of whom have suffered serious problems during 1972.

As to what the summer will bring in the way of results, we will wait and see, but with the Bels team still looking strong and with some promise in depth, we should be able to carry on the normal pattern. Since October we have had nobody disqualified which is especially promising with the new walking definition to be brought in to being at the end of August or there abouts.

Carl Lawton

Narrow win in Cambridge 'Open'

DECEMBER 9th - CAMBRIDGE HARRIERS OPEN 7 MILES - BEXLEY.

A cool but dry day, which made a change from recent weather, was prevalent for the Cambridge Open. The competitors were already lining up to start as Ray Middleton casually made his appearance in his car, horn blowing furiously. Fortunately, due to the Bels efficiency, he was ready to start with the others in under two minutes.

Harry Churcher's Medals

Jack Goswell

The Club have been pleased to accept the kind offer of Mrs. Churcher of the late Harry Churcher's medals and have agreed to hold them in trust during her life time, after which they will become the property of the Club.

After collecting the medals one Saturday afternoon, I spent the rest of that evening and the following day collating and putting them together into their various club categories. Harry's great career was spotlighted in a previous 'Belgravian' but one realises more the length and greatness of this career when dealing with these medals.

Space does not permit a full length copy of the list but they start with a 1931 Belgrave H. Junior Walk Championship 1st gold medal and move through the years to a 1933 A.A.A. 2 miles on to a total of ELEVEN A.A.A. championship medals up to 1951, 31 Surrey County A.A. medals 1933/52, 12 Counties Athletic Union medals 1935/1951, 14 Southern C.A.A.A. medals 1933/1952, 8 L.B.H.S.A. medals 1937/1952 - all over 2, 7 or 10 miles, also 20 Belgrave H."Junior", 2 and 10 miles championship medals 1931/1952 11 Polytechnic H "Kinnaird" team and individual medals 1938/1951, even 1 Surrey Walking Club medal.

This gives some idea of the scope of his career, the total being 13 gold plus 4 almost certain gold, 52 silver and the rest various metals bringing a total of 133 medals.

There was one puzzle I could not solve, One medal, not engraved, almost certainly gold and with a coat of arms and the motto "SINE LABEDECUS". I believe it is a medal from another country. Any ideas?

As one realises this collection is rather valuable and the Committee are now deciding how best to use them. They must be taken care of and preserved as a memento of a great athlete and Belgravian.

Jack Goswell

Column · page 22

ROAD AND COUNTRY

Gordon Biscoe, CROSS COUNTRY & ROAD RUNNING HON. SEC.

At the time of writing, with the 'National' the only cross country race left to go, one cannot actually look back and say that our record this season has been in any way impressive, having just lost the North Surrey League for the first time in seven years. True it was lost by only 13 points and it's very easy to explain it away by theorising as to what we could have done if other people had turned out, etc. But lost it was, through our inability to concentrate our efforts in the races that matter. This is surely the reason for many of our failures. Take the pathetic turn out in the Surrey Championship, only six runners from a club of over 450 members; or how about only nine runners instead of the permissable twelve in the Southern as a further example.

Is it too unreasonable to assume that anyone joining an athletic club and who has taken the trouble to train 50, 60, 70 or more miles per week would also wish to compete ? Is it also too unreasonable to assume that those who are good enough to have been entered, and then subsequently picked to represent their club in the major events should take some pride in turning out ? Apparently not, from some of the excuses and 'reasons' one is expected to believe. Now it's fully appreciated that everyone goes down with colds, 'flu and injuries from time to time but some of the recoveries from indispositions that strike after a training session on Thursday or Friday and then are cleared up by Sunday morning are truly remarkable.

Support for the teams is always welcome but to have members who have been picked to run, and are quite capable of competing, tearing round the course training and cheering on a depleted team is not going to endear them to anyone.

We are often told that the average age of our leading teams is high and although much improved of late, this is still basically true. However, it would be nice to think that in future some of these years of experience and wisdom could be put to good effect and that members will realise that no one is asked or expected to race every week but that we do expect a maximum team effort on the occasions that matter and that those concerned should realise which are the occasions that matter without having to be told, and gear their training accordingly.

It should not be assumed by the way, that any or all of the above remarks are being directed solely at our 'A' team runners, as a check on our record of percentage turnout (and wasted team fees) in races such as the South of the Thames Junior, etc., will show that our efforts have been far to slack at all levels.

Our past achievements and tradition has made Belgrave great but we cannot live on in the past and as soon as everyone realises that our future successes and trophies will only come by winning them and that we are not going to be given them by some Divine right, then the better for all.

Let's hope the coming road and relay season proves the point.

Gordon Biscoe

NATIONAL VET'S CHAMPIONS

FEBRUARY 18th 1973 - PERRY BAR PARK, BIRMINGHAM .

By winning the third National Championship for veteran runners on this cold and windy Sunday afternoon, Belgrave annexed the team trophy for the first time and furnished the individual champion also in the sprightly form of Laurie O'Hara.

Laurie didn't just win - he slaughtered the opposing runners. Belgrave didn't just win - they annihilated the opposing teams. It was that sort of day, Claret and Gold all the way. And it really was like the old days to hear the whistles of surprise at the presentation ceremony when our winning score of 29 points was announced.

The Group 2 (50-59) and Group 3 (Over 60) fields were combined and their race began at midday over three laps of a fast grassland course. We had no entries in these categories.

The premier event (Group 1, 40-49) was held at 1 p.m. over the same 10,000 metres course and 200 runners toed the line. A vicious start saw Laurie back in the twenties at the first turn, but as the field swept up a long slope into a bitter wind he moved through to the front group where Meadows (Portsmouth) was forcing the pace from Harland (Cambridge) - both newcomers to the ranks. Not far behind at this point, in the twenties, were Clive Shippen and Pat Newell running together, while in the ruck of the field Fred Paget and Arthur Bruce were well placed to provide team support if required.

On the second circuit, Harland opened up 20 yards in an effort to break clear, but Laurie was supremely confident and with marked coolness closed the gap and put in his own burst, which stretched and stretched and stretched to a winning margin of 34 seconds - a brilliant effort. Meanwhile, Clive was having his best race for a long while, making ground steadily to finish 10th, and closer to Laurie than he has been for many years; and Pat settled in to 18th which, with no racing of late, was an excellent performance. With three to score, that was that; but just in case something went wrong Fred and Arthur were fighting it out with the opposition in the sixties. It says much for their fine efforts that if our third scorer had failed to finish we still would have won with either of these two scoring. Further back down the field was the man who had more confidence in us than anyone - John Baker. He ran down that finishing straight like a man inspired. With over 20 runners behind him it was his day too!

But for Reg Hopkins giving the team a day of his leisure to ferry the team up and back, and Gordon Biscoe rising at 6 a.m. to ensure that everyone (including Reg!) met and departed on time, it is questionable whether a team would have appeared at all. Well, you know what it is like when you reach second childhood!

MARCH, 1933..................... COUNTRYMAN'S JOTTINGS

Belgravians have not had a great deal to shout about over the country this winter. No medals in the Surrey County Championship for the first time in 8 years; a lowly 11th in the 'Southern' at Parliament Hill; and the loss of our Surrey League title, also for the first time in 8 years, hardly merits self-satisfaction. But base statistics can often cloud the picture, and there were several moments worth dwelling upon. There was the excitement of both South of Thames races - 4th in the 'Junior' and 3rd in the 'Senior'; pruned opposition perhaps, but still healthy for morale to see a goodly splash of claret and gold up front. There was the sight of a much weakened team in the final league race at Coulsdon, fighting to the death against a full strength Aldershot F.D. squad before going down by the smallest margin yet in the history of the league. And last, but not least, there was the sight of Peter Carton finishing inside the first eight whenever he ran, whether it was the Southern Junior (2nd), the Surrey County Junior (1st), the Inter-Counties Junior (8th) or the South of Thames Senior (3rd). Peter has been regularly fighting it out at the front and is surely on the brink of a successful career as a Senior. I have been most impressed with his mature approach to running. He covers merely half the mileage of his close rivals yet is able to channel his efforts so effectively. Unlike the others, he also has more in hand for building up his training in the years ahead. I feel sure that we have only seen the beginnings of the real Peter Carton and the coming track season is likely to underline this observation.

Talk of Peter Carton inevitably reminds us of the other members of that quartet which regularly put fear into the hearts of opposing boy and youth teams a few years back. "Come back Tony Binda!" is the first thought, for Tony had already shown that he was about to break through and provide Peter with close support before his move to the West Indies wrecked the possibility. But the lad who showed the best natural flair, on the track at any rate, was John Stow. John has kept in shape regularly enough to find that a solid year's work could also get him up to his rightful place in the forward position. What Peter has done, the others can still do. Is Bill Curtin listening?

From the young to the (relatively) old. What about this man Laurie O'Hara? Can he really go on achieving life-time bests every year now that he is in his forties? Against veteran athletes he is truly incincible, and many of his conquests have been against former internationals. Students of veteran running have had to re-write many of their theories since Laurie came on to the scene. For instance, how does he fit on to a graph of deteriorating form? How does one compare his best ever performance with his veteran bests where the latter are superior? As someone remarked when Laurie cantered the Veterans 'National' at Birmingham a few weeks ago "That lad looks to have a good future"!

Talking of Vets., it was good to see Belgrave win the National Veterans title at Perry Barr, Birmingham. This championship is not the push-over that some may think, and the ferocity of the running had to be seen to be believed. There were 200 starters in the 40-49 age group alone, and those with memories of Nationals of 20 years ago saw much to make them reminisce. There was George Rhodes (7th) fighting it out with Eddie Kirkup (8th) just as they did for England vests in 1955. And the diminutive Johnny Wild was there, just 5 places down on his 8th spot of 1956. The man they all feared in the late fifties was Gerry North, and Gerry has been making sounds that suggests he is out to hammer them again in 3 years time when he "comes of age".

It is also possible to gain a decathlon star award by competing in ten different events.

Obituary · page 51

S. C. (SYD) ROBERTS

Jack Goswell

It is with great sorrow that we have to advise the death on January 10th of Syd Roberts at the age of 66 years, after a distinguished career as a walker then administrator for the Club and the R.W.A. Our sincere condolences go to his widow in her sad loss and we are grateful for having had the assistance and comradeship over nearly 36 years of a true 'Belgravian' and gentleman.

Syd joined the Club on 4th March 1937 and was soon making his mark in a very strong walking fraternity at that time. In May 1938 he finished 5th in the Barking to Southend Police Championship in 5:38:02 and led the winning team , B Div., home. This was the year when Belgrave had F.Bentley 1st in 5:1:11.4, F.Rickards 2nd, A.Readman 3rd, S.Roberts 5th, R.Martin 6th and five others amongst the finishers including F.Jarvis 44th.

At Manchester in 1939 he was a member of our winning Club team in the R.W.A. National Junior 10 miles. Like many others, the war took care of 1939/45 for him but afterwards Syd took over as Walking Secretary and soon helped to once again bring Belgrave to the fore by his wise administration and help for all.

I still have the letter he sent me, pointing out all the snags when I was taking over from him as Walking Secretary in 1952. Syd then moved to the office of Club Treasurer which he held from 1952 to 1962 and during these years he did a 1st class job in reorganising the membership book and finances - no mean job. He was elected to the highest office as Club President in 1957/58.

During these years Syd was also an active member of the S.R.W.A. Committee and held various offices. On the parent body of the R.W.A. he was really active, acting as Chief Recorder at many national championships, Judge Sub-Committee Secretary, then the first National Coaching Secretary for R.W.A. in 1963/64 an office he held with distinction.

In the late sixties he underwent a serious operation. After a period of convalescence he moved to St. Annes on Sea, Lancs., and it was from there that he and his wife travelled the 30 miles to Hulton, Manchester on the occasion of the R.W.A. 20 miles championship in 1972. It was a great pleasure to greet him once again and speak with them both. Quite contented and although a lot slower moving, he maintained that he was improving all the time.

Little did I realise that it was to be the last time I would see him. The sport of Race Walking and Athletics in general is the poorer for his passing.

The R.W.A. President and other members of the N.R.W.A. represented the Club at his funeral on the same day as Alf Harley's, Monday 15th January 1973 - a black day for the Club with the passing on of two fine English gentlemen.

Jack Goswell

Feature · page 52

CENTURIONS ALL

Gordon S. Doubleday

A Centurion is one who, as an Amateur, has walked in competition, in Great Britain, 100 miles within 24 hours.

To become a Centurion is the ambition of every long-distance walker. Most Walking Clubs in Great Britain can claim a number in their membership, and Belgrave, with its great traditions and record is no exception. We recently mourned the death of Ernest Neville, Centurion Number 7, who was associated with a number of Clubs and was one of our Vice-Presidents.

I have been unable to check which of the early Centurions were Belgravians, for example when I was competing in the North some years ago a chap wearing a Centurion badge said that he was a member of the Bels in his racing days. Coming to 1921 we find that Jimmy Belchamber finished second in the Brighton and Back race in 20:53:35 to become No. 64. Jimmy was a Past President of the Club and served in many offices, for instance for many years he was Hon. Auditor. He loved walking and covered many miles in his native Sussex. Our own 50 Km Championship carries a cup named after him.

Five years later Jim Chalk did a fine 19:03:30 for 100 miles in the "B and B". Jim was a staunch Belgravian and figured in many winning teams. After he had retired from competition he walked miles to come and support our teams on the road, invariably accompanied by his dog.

After World War II the first event at the distance was a 24 hours walk at the White City in 1946. It rained for many hours and Colin Sutton clocked 20:44:11 to become No. 125. Colin was a great performer at all distances, an R.A.F. pilot during the war he re-enlisted after a short spell as a civilian and was killed by a sniper in Malaya. In the same race was Bob Martin, a policeman who also figured in many of our teams, and who clocked 22:16:57 to become No. 127. He was killed in an accident whilst on a motor-cycle patrol a few years later. The above four, now departed, showed the way for other Belgravians to follow.

The first post-war "B and B" was eagerly awaited by the writer who fulfilled an ambition when he heared Ernest Neville call, "23:26:41" at the 100 miles point to become No. 142. When he is not timekeeping he still enjoys a walk (Wimbledon Common and Richmond Park are close at hand) followed by a warm bath!

1948 saw a 24 hours race at Motspur Park which resulted in a Belgrave win for Jack Stirling-Wakeley, whose death was recently reported. He covered 100 miles in 18:56:13 to become No. 143 and went on to cover 122 miles 1411 yards. Three years later he won the "B and B" in 18:46:0. In the same race Joe Wilson walked his usual sound race to become No. 164 in 21:04:46. Joe needs no introduction and is often at the roadside judging or helping with the feeding, or, indeed, giving his Club vest an airing. Joe always wears a Belgrave vest when competing - do you ? Further down the field came Harry Stallard, a Dover based enthusiast who worked hard for the sport in his area, in 23:20:04 to become No. 173.

In 1952 another classic route was walked, Bath to London (Chiswick) and Chelsea policeman Syd Spencer recorded a very fine 20:09:45 for badge No. 181. A solid Clubman who was a regular in "Belgrave" and Police teams for a good many years. Now retired, we often see him judging. In the same race Fred Herring, an R.A.F. man gained badge No. 191 in 21:50:00. We haven't seen Fred for a number of years but he did a lot to encourage Race Walking in the R.A.F. before retiring.

In the 1955 "B and B" there were 31 new Centurions, amongst them three Belgravians. Harry Shields followed up a great career as a runner (twice a member of winning E.C.C.U. teams) and an equally great one as a walker (member of winning team in R.W.A. 50 Kilos Championship) to become No. 235 in 20:02:22. Harry is still a well known official at Running and Walking events, he is also handy with the measuring tape and at raking the long jump pit! With brother Joe qualifying in the 1951 race Percy Wilson took his turn to become No. 246 in 21:53:55. Like Joe you cannot keep him away from the sporting scene, he recently had a spell in hospital due to a stroke but is now fighting his way back to fitness. Completing the Club trio was Frank Jarvis in 23:25:22, No. 258. Frank was another "regular" in Belgrave and Police teams. Nowadays he is kept busy as General Hon. Secretary of the R.W.A. Another to qualify in this race was Les Fairman in the colours of Poly. H. Now a Belgravian and well known figure at Belgrave Hall he walked a fine race to become No. 232 in 19:31:33.

The next "B and B" (1963) saw two Belgravians doing the journey stride by stride, Cyril Yescombe (another Police stalwart) who died tragically a few years ago and Bernard Eglington, former runner from Derby and R.A.F. man. They came through the 100 miles point in torrential rain in 21:28:57 earning themselves badges 344 and 345. Bernard now lives in N. Wales and occasionally makes a welcome appearance.

In 1965 the M.P.A.A. promoted a race on a lap course at Chigwell and amongst those to qualify was Doug. Fotheringham in 21:31:09 for badge No. 373. Doug. is still going strong - if not stronger! The older he gets the faster he goes! He is the Hon. Sec. of the M.P.A.A. Walking Section.

The Surrey W.C. 100 miles at Ewhurst (1971) is of recent memory, when our own John Moullin won in a phenomènal world-class performance of 16:55:44 to become No. 462. Lower down the field, stride for stride, came John Keown (473) and John Morris (474) finishing together in 20:49:27. They took second team prize and are still active, further anecdotes unnecessary!

We have many potential Centurions in Belgrave who, when the time comes, will add to the list. Good luck to them! They will need it.

Gordon S. Doubleday
