EDITORIAL

The announcement that the "News of the World" had decided, after all, to reprieve the National London - Brighton Relay has come as a welcome relief to all Belgravians.

For 40 years this race has been rated the Mecca of road running. Many of our runners, past and present, have geared their training programmes to this occasion - indeed, many have made it their sole ambition. It came as a profound shock, therefore, when it was first made known that the sponsors were discontinuing the race due to ever-increasing costs. Human nature being what it is, the athletics world needed a decision of this kind before it showed its appreciation of such a promotion. "Athletics Weekly" was inundated with letters, as indeed was the "News of the World." This response impressed the newspaper's organising committee to such an extent that they rescinded their decision and the National Relay is now ON. However, the "Southern" relay has been cancelled but at least ten clubs from the South will participate in the National event.

These events of the last few weeks have shown how close to the wind sponsor-ships sail.. Fortunately, they have also shown how much the athletic clubs of this country appreciate these relays and, in turn, have indicated to the club runner just how much is involved in staging them. Let us hope that all parties have something to gain.

HON SEC'S NOTES

A. A. H.

We have to report the death of one of our oldest members, TOMMY ASKEW, who died recently just short of 90 years of age. Tommy was a useful team walker with the Club many years ago without any pretensions to championship form, and was a regular attendant at our Battersea Park meetings when he was resident in Westminster. Our sympathies are extended to Mr and Mrs. Joe Coleman - son-in-law and daughter.

* * *

Our good wishes go with Joe Coleman on his entry into hospital for treatment on the leg which has handicapped him in recent years; and we hope he will soon be around again as lively as when he was one of the fastest walkers in the country.

* * *

Track meetings have been arranged as follows:- Saturday April 24th Battersea Park Monday May, 3rd Battersea Park Wednesday May, 12th Battersea Park, SATURDAY MAY 22nd CLUB TRACK AND FIELD CHAMPIONSHIPS - HURLINGHAM Wednesday June 9th Battersea Park Wednesday June 23rd Battersea Park Wednesday June 30th Battersea Park (Inter-club Walks) Wednesday July 7th Battersea Park Wednesday July 21st Battersea Park Wednesday August 11th Battersea Park

These fixtures will be supplemented by many many more in which we shall participate as arranged by Sectional Hon. Secretaries.

Attention is particularly drawn to the Club Championship Day on May 22nd- at Hurlingham Park Track and it is hoped that every member, will make a special effort to be there - as competitor, official, or spectator.

It is hoped to arrange a Social evening to follow: and that May 22nd will prove a day of Reunion of members of all ages.

* * *

We expect to welcome our friends from Langen, West Germany, during August and it is hoped to arrange a track meeting whilst they are in London, before they proceed to Blackpool.

*

We shall be supporting the A.A.A Supporters Association Derby Draw this year which should yield a handsome contribution to our own fund, and help our Hon. Treasurer to balance our Club Accounts. We ask all members for their help and that of their friends for we cannot maintain our facilities and unrivalled opportunities for competition out of subscription income alone. Frank Webb does a great job for us in organising our money-raising schemes and deserves the whole-hearted support of every one of us in his efforts on behalf of the Club.

BELS 2nd at BLACKHEATH

Hayes - September 19th

This was our first attack at the Blackheath Relay and Portsmouth, the holders, were very hard pressed for five of the six laps. However, they managed to repeat their win of last year and we had to be content as runners-up.

Tony Fairclough got us off to a good start finishing third on stage one, 15 seconds behind the leader, Harland of Cambridge H. John Bicourt, now a Junior and eligible to compete in Senior relays, took the second leg and only failed to hold his place by 1 second. Walton, Portsmouth and Hercules were now ahead of us with SLH breathing down our necks.

John Thresher had a great run on the third leg to bring us within 1 second of the new leaders, Portsmouth. From now on it was a 2 team race. Mel Thorpe had the difficult job of staying with D. Cooke and was unequal to the task, by 31 seconds. This created a situation which in recent years has become a familiar pattern to Gerry North - chasing the agile form of Martin Hyman! The Portsmouth runner's lead was slowly whittled away but, for all his effort, Gerry could only peg back 18 seconds of the lost half minute. This presented Bill Kerr with the unenviable task of giving John Cooke a 13 second lead and a beating - a feat even North couldn't have managed, for Cooke's time was second fastest of the day, only one second down on Gerry.

WALTON RELAY: POMPEY BEAT BELS AGAIN

WALTON RELAY

POMPEY BEAT BELS AGAIN

After their victory over us two weeks earlier at Blackheath, Portsmouth went to the "Hewitt and Jones" relay at Walton full of confidence. Their selected twelve for the following week's London to Brighton relay appeared and stages 1 to 6 comprised the 'A' team and 7 to 12 the 'B'! The latter, of course, was the stronger.

Belgrave had their strongest six running for the 'A' but only three of their "Brighton" team in the 'B' - so it was one set of gold hoops versus two sets of blue hoops! Apart from the first 2 stages, it was the stronger Pompey team out in front being chased by Belgrave with none of the others in it.

Charlie Dabbs ran well on Stage 1 to finish 4th, 18 seconds down on the leader (Collins of Surrey AC), but on the next leg Hyman beat Geoff North in the run-in and, for a moment, both Portsmouth teams were ahead of us. However John Thresher soon put the weaker one out of it by clocking 6th fastest time of the day. Despite this John Cooke had increased his team's lead to 23 seconds and in so doing set a new lap record. Tony Fairclough, not at his best, watched Tulloh disappear into the distance and Kerr lost further ground to Salvat, so Gerry North was faced with a deficit of 69 seconds on the final leg. With a tremendous effort Gerry took all but 10 seconds off the Pompey runner (Wale) and, although we failed to win, we were able to furnish our last leg runner in the "Brighton" (Jack Brown) with some interesting facts about his probable opponent!

BELS FAIL BY 14secs / TITLE LOST IN "SOUTHERN" RELAY

Olympian

BELS FAIL BY 14secs

TITLE LOST IN "SOUTHERN" RELAY

We started last October's "News of the World" London-Brighton relay race (Southern clubs)as holders of the trophy,and the pundits gave us a good chance of retaining the title - although Portsmouth were at almost full strength. We found the opposition to be a little too strong but it was a close thing - in fact only fourteen seconds separated us in the end and we were catching up rapidly.

The lads ran extremely well. Laurie O'Hara taking the first leg for the first time gave us a good start with third place only seven seconds behind the leaders,Walton; and John Thresher, who now knows every inch of the second stage,made no mistake and put us into the lead at Mitcham. At this point Portsmouth,through John Lindsell,had moved up to our heels. Trevor Hart,however, soon sorted out this position and ran away from Fudge, the Portsmouth runner,to put us into a thirty eight second lead at the Wallington take-over.

The fourth stage is a new and crucial leg being one of the longest and Charlie Dabbs tackled it in workmanlike manner with the knowledge that Dave Cooke,one of the Portsmouth twins,was chasing him.Charles ran well to keep out in front although our lead was now cut back to five seconds.

Mel Thorpe,running in Belgrave colours for the first time on the Brighton road,was faced with the problem of dealing with Reynolds of Portsmouth and John Snowden of Surrey. Both were in good form and were in fact the first and second fastest of the day,so there was no disgrace for Mel in having to give way. Our deficit at Earlswood was forty three seconds.

Pat Newell,back into the team again,ran a fast sixth stage and just caught the Surrey boy at the end of the leg. He had to share the fastest time with the Portsmouth runner so we were still forty three seconds behind.

Alan Black pulled back nineteen seconds across Gatwick Airport with a stylish performance which gained him the second fastest time of the day, and Gerry North set of on the long trek to Handcross to Handcross with Martin Hyman in sight. Unfortunately Gerry was not at his absolute best and instead of picking up Hyman as we had hoped,he was forced to drop back ten seconds leaving Bill Kerr the invidious task of chasing the other and better of the Cooke boys. Bill set off slowly whilst Cooke went like a bomb. and the gap widened rapidly. Our spirits dropped but were raised again when we found that the fast start was telling badly on the Portsmouth runner.Bill slowly pulled back and we had visions of him finishing close, but a great effort over the last mile kept Portsmouth in the lead by sixty five seconds.

This lead was further increased with a fast run by Bruce Tulloh on stage ten despite a great effort by Geoff North and with only two stages to go we were almost one and a half minutes behind - practically an impossible position.

Tony Fairclough had different ideas on the subject and caring little for the reputation of Frank Salvat,produced the best ever run by a Belgrave Harrier on this leg;and in knocking four seconds from Dave Baulch's club record became the first Belgravian to beat twenty minutes. This excellent effort reduced Portsmouths lead to fifty seconds and put new heart into the Belgrave supporters.

Although Jack Brown (back into the team again after missing a number of Brightons) was our oldest runner;his great experience soon became apparent,for against a Portsmouth newcomer on his first Brighton, he closed the gap rapidly. The leeway was just too much,however,and Portsmouth got home by a mere 100 yards. Jack ran his heart out and was only thirteen seconds outside his own club record set up twelve years ago when he was at his very best. Obviously there is life in the old dog yet - we shall look forward to a repetition next April.

ROAD RUNNING

In the summer of 1963 Derek Crookes was stricken with a stomach ailment which necessitated major surgery. From an active athlete at all distances up to a marathon this keen Belgravian became a pale shadow of his former self. Prospects for a continued athletics career were decidedly remote. Thanks to a fighting spirit though,and faith in the human frame,Derek was running again within 6 months.

He is now keener than ever,and at our last AGM Derek Crookes was elected Road Running Secretary. He not only enters names,organises teams and keeps in touch with the road running world,but also competes himself and writes reports and results for your Hon.Editors! It is on this latter score that we are particularly indebted to Derek,but we feel sure that on all scores he will serve Belgrave well.

THE '5¾' ROAD RACE

Wimbledon - September 26th

Bill Kerr showed a welcome return to form when he took the Autumn "trial" in convincing style from Fairclough and Dabbs. Several of our leading runners were not taking part and it was apparent that any potential "Brighton" man would have to make the first eight to be worthy of further consideration.

By the time Copse Hill had been reached the eight were sorted out from the rest. Fairclough set the pace for a fair time and the opposition gradually dropped away until only Kerr and Dabbs were with him. Kerr then made his effort, and although Tony hung on for a while the scotsman proved the stronger, crossing the line with an 11 second margin. Dabbs pulled back ground on young Fairclough, but Tony's courage held off the assault and he preserved a well-run second place.

Jack Brown ran himself back into the Brighton team in convincing fashion and Bicourt, in only his second crack at the event, showed he will be well to the fore next time.

Outside,the knot had lengthened to the Ridgway, and so, as the hour drew nigh and reports filtered through it was apparent that the old adage "The pen is mightier than the sword" took on a more frightful aspect. Just the two words, "jumble sale", had awakened something savage in the breast of man. with a minute to go the fiends were hammering on the door. The International Championship

Clive Shippen

Clive Shippen reviews the history of...... The International Championship

On March 20th, 1965,a dozen teams of the world's best cross-country runners will line up in Ostende, Belgium, to fight out the 52nd International Championship.

It could be anyone's race. Arizmendi (Spain) holds the individual title and England the team award, but the Spaniard is unlikely to start favourite and the English team will be hard pushed to defeat the home side, all out to regain the title England took from them last year in Dublin. In 1963 only 3 points covered the first three teams - such is the standard that has made this event the Mecca of cross-country running.

It was a different story in 1898. The French Athletic Federation challenged the first eight in the English 'National'. The race was held over a 9 miles course in France and the English team filled the first eight places! The first Frenchman, A. Touquet, fell unconscious as he crossed the line and, to rub it in, their second man was an Englishman, H. Freeman.

Enthusiasm died down in the following three or four years. Then, in 1903, enthusiasts in the Welsh and Irish Associations planned the first International cross-country race. How confident were the organisers of success? Well, it was not until after the race that the LUMLEY Shield came into being and it was agreed to form an International Cross-Country Union. The Welsh/ Irish secretaries approached Scotland first of all, who readily adopted the idea, and it is not surprising to hear that the English were against it. After all, wasn't our own 'National' open to all amateur teams. "I do not see the good to be gained by such a race. It will be almost as much a farce .... as our unfortunate trial with France a few years ago" - so spoke an English official. It is significant, however, that France was not invited. Eventually England agreed to compete providing, of course, that the race was postponed a week to enable the great Alfred Shrubb to run! Shrubb duly won and England's scoring 6 were split only by the Irishman Daly, who was 3rd.

The inaugural meeting of the Union was held on the morning of the 1904 race where it was agreed that the venue for the succeeding year's event should be decided on the morning of each Championship. The race would be 'open to the world'. The 1904 race went the same way as the previous year's, except a second 'intruder' split England's six.

France got its invitation two years later and raced for the first time in 1907 at Scotstown. This was the occasion when a swarthy, thick-set Frenchman named Jean Bouin made his first appearance on British soil; it was not his last, and England's runners were to see a lot more of him in subsequent years - mainly from behind.

The first hiatus was brought about by World War I. England had won all 12 Championships and Bouin was the first to score a hat-trick of individual titles.

Contests were resumed in 1920 and the following year the England team notched up its 14th successive title. All good things come to an end, and it was fitting that France should be the country to end them. The memory of that 1898 "whitewash" was erased in 1922 and future championships became a France/ England battle.

In 1923, at Maisons-Lafitte, near Paris, the local Mayor presented to the Union a bronze statuette known as the Maisons-Lafitte trophy, to be awarded to the individual winner for 12 months. This year also saw the admission of Belgium and the temporary withdrawal of Ireland, who were sorting out North v South domestic squabbles amongst their officials. Perhaps it was fortunate that the Union met but once only a year; more frequent meetings might well have magnified the difficulties.

France and Scotland were not happy with the venue "going the round". The French wanted it every 3rd year in France and the Scots every year in the north of England and the result was "no change", whereupon the French delegate proposed that the 1924 race, to be held in England, should be held as near the French coast as possible - it was staged at Newcastle!

France was on a hat-trick at Newcastle but England chose this occasion to be the first team to score minimum points in the official championship (21) by having the first six men home. However, the French got their hat-trick plus a bonus win in 1926/7/8/9. In the latter year non-member nations competed for the first time; Spain, Italy, Switzerland, and Luxembourg, boosting the entry to ten.

England, however, were ready once more for a long run of victories and replied by notching up the next nine; leaving France the last one before World War II (1939). Jack Holden was the individual winner in the last pre-war race and thus became the first man to score 4 wins.

Belgravians were notable contributors to this period of England supremacy. The roll of honour reads as follows:- 1931 Arthur Allum 9th 1933 Bert Footer 4th 1933 Arthur Penny 9th 1934 Arthur Penny 3rd 1934 Bert Footer 4th 1936 Tom Carter 37th 1937 Jim Ginty 3rd 1939 Arthur Penny 18th 1939 Ted Stimpson -

Now to the post-war era - food rationing, austerity everywhere, and the need to mould new teams. The effects of war had stimulated the desire for friendship and little time was lost in resuming contacts within the International Cross-Country Union. Even the Irish settled their differences and entered a combined team for the first time since 1932.

As in many other spheres of the immediate post-war era, England appeared to suffer more than her continental friends. For 3 years, the Championship was exclusively a French/Belgian contest and in 1949, France had 5 in 8, with Alain Mimoun scoring his first of 4 wins.

Most of Belgrave's post-war contributions were made during this period. 1946 Len Herbert 21st 1947 Len Herbert 40th 1948 Verne Blowfield 35th

To those who do not remember the days of food rationing, it might be of interest to quote the following minute from the E.C.C.U's A.G.M. of 1948. "The Manager of the English International Team in Paris last March referred to the very generous action of the officials of the Eire A.A.U. in providing necessitous food to the members of the team, without which they would have been insufficiently nourished."

Belgium won the championship for the first time in 1948 and provided the first two home in J. Doms and E. Renson.

Holland entered for the first time in 1950 and Yugoslavia in 1953 when their No. 1, F. Mihalic, took the individual title. This latter year was the Jubilee Year of the I.C.C.U. The Championship was held in Paris and over 20,000 people were present, including the President of the French Republic M. Vincent Auriol. The heat was intense and 13 runners retired with sunstroke.

England celebrated the jubilee in the best possible way - by beating their old rivals and recording their 27th win in 40 appearances. Surprisingly this was Gordon Pirie's only outing in the International Championship - his 19th position being 11 places better than his father's effort in 1926.

The early nineteen-fifties were notable for the emergence of England's finest cross-country runner since Jack Holden - Frank Sando. His remarkable sense of judgment and a "wise old head" on a young and supremely fit body enabled him to finish inside the first two places on five occasions in seven years. He was 9th in his first championship (1952) and 8th in his last (1960) - a record of consistency that can only be matched by Holden and Mimoun.

Mimoun had his first outing in 1949 when he took the individual title by inches from team-mate Pujazon, who was chasing his 3rd consecutive victory. The latter made no bones about how he felt! Mimoun was beaten in the Jubilee race by Lucien Theys of Belgium but in 1954 at Birmingham he regained the title at the expense of Ken Norris. Nevertheless, England finished 6 in 8 and retained the Lumley Shield.

In 1955, Portugal became the 13th country to compete in the race and their 5th place out of eight teams was a fine start. England completed yet another trio of victories with 6 in 8 and Sando recorded his first win. France's 4th place was their lowest since 1920. Mimoun did not compete this year, but he came back with a vengeance in 1956 at Belfast where he beat Sando and K. Norris by 60 yds. and led the French team to victory; despite England having 3 in 4!

These extremes of form in the England teams were even more apparent the following year when we had Sando 1st, Heatley 2nd, K. Norris 3rd, Chorlton 5th yet could only finish 3rd! Firth and Driver who finished 4th and 2nd in the English 'National' could only manage 52nd and 60th and it was left to Perkins 34th and reserve Humphreys 39th to complete the six. Belgrave's Jack Brown was next reserve on call and would certainly not have done worse than this supporting cast. His absence from the race also meant that the Club had gone 9 years without gaining an international vest over the country.

1958 saw the entrance of Tunisia and the start of another England hat-trick. The race was held at Cardiff and Eldon, Sando and Mimoun entered the final straight together. Mimoun forged ahead 50 yards from the tape but in a last desperate effort, Eldon stormed home to a win that prevented the Frenchman from being the only man to score five victories.

In 1959, at Lisbon, Morrocco became the 15th country to have competed in the Championship and England turned out another of their 4 in 5 efforts. In the following year, Gerry North had his first I.C.C.U. outing finishing 15th (3 ahead of Mimoun) and was England's sixth scorer.

Excellent packing by Belgium in 1961 at Nantes prevented England from gaining a 4th consecutive victory, but we had the consolation of seeing that fine cross-country runner Basil Heatley, gain his first International title.

At Sheffield in 1962, Gerry North was hoping to be the first man to win the Inter-Counties, National and International titles - the first two were already his. Early in the race he fell at a sharp bend and lost a dozen places. The effort in fighting his way back to 3rd at half-way brought on a 'stitch' and he had great difficulty in holding on to a gallant 8th place. However, England re-gained the title and Gerry gained his second team-winners' medal. The South Africans, who were over here for the London/Brighton road run and who had stayed at Arthur Whitehead's cottage for many weeks, entered a team; the 16th country to do so. They ran exceptionally well to finish 6th and had great pleasure in beating France by virtue of their last scoring man. This was France's lowest ever position.

1963 saw Belgium, France and England within 3 points of each other - Fowler the individual champion - a record entry of 11 teams - and last, but not least, the 18th consecutive appearance of that amazing Belgian Marcel Vandewattyn. Last year he made it 19; will he make it 20 at Ostende?

I have purposely left this 40 [?] years old veteran until last; although this is not the position to which he is accustomed. Vandewattyne's first international was at Ayr Racecourse in 1946, when he finished 2nd to Pujazon. Scottish conditions must have agreed with him, for 6 years later at Hamilton Park Racecourse, he was 2nd again; this time to Mimoun. Ten more I.C.C.U. appearances went by until at Sheffield in 1962, team-mate Roelants became the 3rd man to push the unfortunate Vandewattyne into 2nd position. To be a runner-up in this class of competition at each end of a 16 years' span is an achievement that even Jack Holden couldn't match. It is ironical though that, unlike Holden, Vandewattyne has not recorded a single win in his 19 outings. The sands of time are now running out for this remarkable Belgian and his 26th position of last year is just about as high as he can now aim. All lovers of cross-country will be hoping to see the familiar figure of Marcel Vandewattyne in the 1965 line up in Ostende and, should he appear, I've an inkling that it will not be just Belgian supporters cheering him on.

HOW I TRAINED

Len Fletcher

We continue our series of training articles from Belgrave Newsletters of the early 1950's

(2) LEN FLETCHER

Next to judging I know of no more controversial subject apertaining to race walking. It is like asking a tobacconist which is the best tobacco,or a piano salesman which is the best piano. In short there is no best way;it all comes down to a point of suitability,and from my own experience,athletic training,or should I say the correct training methods used depend largely on the physique and temperament of the individual.

I commenced race walking at the tender age of 18 years;a tall,lanky and much too thin youth,and I was fortunate to be coached by my late father and his brother;two athletes of the old school who always deplored the general tendency to burn up youngsters before they even learned a good style.

Style comes first and if you show a tendency to push the pace,check yourself;speed will come if you are going to be a walker. This was hammered into me continuously. So remember,develop a good easy natural style and a natural stride and let anyone win the training spins. Such do not win races, as you leave your speed and consequent exhaustion in a spin,whereas you will want all your energy for the race.

For the walker of the thin physique who must preserve his strength,I recommend the following methods:-

TRAINING FOR THE TRACK. Once you have developed a good style,approved by your coach,approach your training in a campaign attitude.First I recommend a very slow trot of two or three laps thus loosening the muscles in a similar manner to the runners,and at the same time indulging in deep breathing exercises. Then commence a walk of two miles starting very steadily and concentrating on your style and balance. On commencing the second mile,warm up the pace but still walk within yourself,but when you enter the last lap speed up and finish fast. This method I used for the first 4 to 6 weeks of track training,thus making sure I could stay the distance. As I got fitter and consequently faster, I reduced the steady pace to the first mile and speeded the second mile. Once fit and fast,my training was reduced to fast miles or at the most 6 laps. Eventually I got to a 6:52 mile and 14:2 for 2 miles. These performances on the tracks of Battersea Park and Stamford Bridge would have meant faster performances on the fast White City track. Once again I emphasise that the methods quoted here are those I recommend for the slight physique type of walker who must preserve his weight and husband his strength to last the season.

ROAD TRAINING FOR 7 TO 10 MILES. Road walking requires a combination of speed,staying power and judgement,particularly over the longer distances. First and foremost I do not recommend a walker under the age of 21 attempting any distances over 7 miles.

A COUNTRYMAN'S Jottings ......

Hugh Barrow completes his 14 months qualification period in March and thereafter will be first claim Belgrave. At one time Hugh had anticipated moving to London permanantly but finally decided to remain in Glasgow and travel as often as possible - hence his 14 months wait. In Scotland he is first claim Victoria Park AAC, which is Bill Kerr's home club. Hugh is a dedicated runner from whom great things can be expected. He already holds the British Junior mile record. For 2 years now he has been a keen follower of Belgrave, going to considerable expense to get down here as often as possible. Now he is first claim we can expect to see even more of him.

* * *

For the past two years Walton AC have taken the North Surrey League title with Belgrave runners-up each time. This year, with one fixture to come, we are well ahead of them. Unfortunately there is another team which has jumped right to the top and is almost certain of winning the trophy - Hercules AC. Their runners are good friends with Belgravians and I think they would admit that part of their success this last year or so is due to the collective pack runs of the two teams over Wimbledon Common and Richmond Park.

* * *

On the subject of pack runs, the Sunday morning outing from Arthur Whitehead's cottage sometimes numbers as many as fifty. Up to six clubs are represented. I sometimes wonder how Arthur manages to cope with such numbers and still provide hot showers, tea and biscuits. The Lauriston Cottage rendezvous is of great value to the runners but perhaps they would do well to ponder the moral of this issue's Editorial.

* * *

Barry Sawyer still seems to be getting in plenty of running in South-Africa. He is now living in Cape Town where he will remain until he moves to New Zealand in November. Unfortunately his running career is threatened by chronic trammatic teno-synovitis which occasionally pulls him up when an achilles tendon refuses to function. Although I would question his wisdom in running 11 miles flat out each night "to brutalize myself a little more and ignore the pain", his determination to win this year's Comrades Marathon is worthy of admiration.

Barry had a crack at the South African National Cross-country championship He led for the first 1½ miles but his leg trouble put him back to 34th at the end. Belgravians will be interested to know that Mike Coningham was 13th and Rex Chugg - a former Australian member - was also competing. Rex's brother Doug is now back in Australia running his own business but finds little time for training.

Barry is soon hoping to see Henk Altmann who goes up to Oxford this year, so with any luck there should soon be another Belgravian on his way.

Fred Paget, a familiar figure at our cross country, road and track fixtures these last eight years, has moved to Australia. So yet another Belgravian takes his claret and gold vest overseas.

He was also an accomplished cyclist and used this medium to keep fit when injury prevented his running. We shall miss his noisy, but determined, running - so adeptly called "fiery locomotion!" Good luck Fred!

Several Belgravians have lived there at some time or other; two in particular being Pat Newell and Bill Kerr. County Championships

SURREY - Guildford.

This was the first time for a long while that three Belgravians ran themselves into the County team with such ease. Roberts (SLH) got away right from the start and it was soon evident that John Thresher would be second. Tony Fairclough,although still below peak,was third until the last mile when he was overhauled by a fast finishing trio,but was well pleased with the result - as indeed was Dabbs who was surprised to make the team so easily. Unfortunately the rest of the team were not up to the same standard and we had to go back to positions 68,80 and 84 to close in. Our 6th place was one of our worst ever performances.

MIDDLESEX - Cockfosters.

For once the going at Cockfosters was fast and firm and should have suited our runners. It was a fine effort for our team,Cadogan AC,to turn out twelve men,even though they did not rank high in club form. Mike Shingles and Trevor Hart were both "under the weather" and trailed in 65th and 68th positions. Laurie O'HAra was only 43 seconds outside the county team,in 20th place,and this fast improving runner should soon obtain a red and gold vest. All twelve Cadogan runners finished,with the scoring six placed 8th.

WARWICKSHIRE - Baddesley.

We had two "first timers" in the Midland event - Geoff North and Mel Thorpe. Both were previously Lancashire thro' residence,but since moving South decided to adopt their County of birth - Warwickshire.

Geoff split the Coventry team most convincingly,beating Kilby,Taylor and Heatley,but had to give best to Adcocks (an easy winner) and Horne who beat Geoff in the run in. Mel Thorpe also ran particularly well finishing 16th and only 39 seconds outside one of the strongest County teams.

CLUB '10' to NORTH

GERRY TAKES "MACHER" SHIELD - January 23rd.

In marked contrast to last year, when Gerry finished 13th and brother Geoff retired, the North brothers set about the field with a fine display of determined running to take first two places well inside last year's winning time.

Gerry stormed into the lead at the start and was obviously out for a good time in his last race before the "Southern", three weeks away. At 2 miles he was 25 seconds ahead of the field and at halfway a full minute. Churning out mile after mile at a relentless pace he eventually finished almost 2½ mins. in the lead, having completed his second 5 miles only 15 seconds slower than the first.

Although the Championship was never in doubt there was an interesting race for the minor placings. After 2 miles Geoff North, Fairclough, Thorpe, Black, Dabbs and O'Hara were closely bunched with Black and Geoff North setting the pace; Bill Kerr was already finding their speed too fast. At halfway Geoff and Tony were still pushing the pace with Mel Thorpe hanging on extremely well. Black and O'Hara were dropping and the defending champion, Dabbs, did not look his usual self but was managing to hold his 5th place.

With one lap to go Fairclough was leading from Geoff N. with Thorpe 20 seconds down, then Dabbs, Black and O'Hara (7th). Bill Kerr retired at this point when 8th, as did Bromley(9th) - who had the misfortune of a shoe spike penetrating thro' his inner sole - after some good running.

It is interesting to note that of the 24 runners to finish this 4 x 2½ miles course the winner lapped all but the first 13!
