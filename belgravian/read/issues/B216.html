President of Belgrave Harriers 1990-91 — Win Shepherd writes ...

Win Shepherd; profile by C.D.

It gives me great pleasure to send you this message as your first Lady President. I send my best wishes to you all and hope that you will have a good Walking, Cross Country, Road Racing and Track and Field season. I should like you to remember that you are members of a great club but although you are an individual you are also a member of a team. When you are asked to represent the Club, please do so.

Within the club there is a band of officials who work hard for your benefit. At the moment please note that we are very short of officials for track and field meetings. A certain few are keeping things going at home meetings. It would be nice if those of you who have hung up your running shoes would come along and help at the grass roots of Athletics. You will certainly enjoy yourselves!

Let's make the rest of 1991 a good year for Belgrave!

Win Shepherd

Win is caught by the lens of Alex Apperley at a recent Club Championship meeting.

Win Shepherd, Belgrave's first Lady President, has been associated with the club since the age of three. Her father, Dick Savage, joined Belgrave in the year 1910 and became President 1944-1946. Her late husband Dave Shepherd, who joined in 1932, was also President in 1962-1963. Win became interested in race walking when she was fifteen years old but as there was no Ladies Section in those days she had to join London Olympiads. She was their Club Champion for three years, Surrey County Champion for three years, and won the Inter-Counties and was second four times whilst representing Surrey. In addition she was twice a member of the Champion National Road Walk team. As you can see, she competed at the highest level. Later, with her mother, Win organised the refreshments at Belgrave Hall on Saturday afternoons for a period of some nine years and when the Ladies Section was formed in 1976 she became our first Ladies Secretary. Win can always be seen at Belgrave meetings either officiating or scoring. With that claret and gold blood in her veins it would be difficult to keep her away! C.D.

Editorial · page 3

The "new" Belgravian

This edition of the Belgravian is "new" in several respects. In fact we had even considered naming it "The New Belgravian" but finally thought that that was just too much of a departure from tradition. You will have noticed the new size and format. This is the result of a fresh editorial partnership with Alan Mead and Charles Dickinson working together for the first time. "The Belgravian" has now been brought up to date with a new layout designed by Alan using sophisticated desk top publishing software on his computer. This allows pages to be "set up" on the screen and then output to a laserjet printer prior to hand over to the print shop. 'Pasting up' is now a thing of the past. The larger page size allows for a great deal more flexibility in layout to accommodate pictures and different column widths. We trust you like it. All this has, of course, freed up Charles a little to allow him to chase likely contributors for articles, badger Sectional Secretaries for results, get some more writing done himself - and even do a bit of extra training! Following on fairly closely from the last edition in March, we believe we have caught up on the backlog of news and results from last year and the winter season just past. What with early Track and Field results, including the 1991 Club Championships, why we are almost topical! We would like to thank those people who have contributed to the magazine. Items are always gratefully received. But let's not stop here; please let us know if you have any ideas which could be incorporated into the pages of our gazette. Write us letters, offer your services as a columnist, cub reporter or photographer, even as a seller advertising space - we'll consider anything..

Finance

Incidentally, what are the thoughts of club members regarding the finance of the magazine? Three issues per year is being considered, probably about 16 pages a time, and it does not take much in the way of mathematics to realise that this is likely to cost the club a lot more than has recently been spent in this area. Some folk have suggested that members might consider paying for their magazine outside of the normal subscription. This might be difficult to organise and anyway, it might not be considered fair on those, perhaps living away from the home counties, who continue to regularly pay their subs. without receiving any other material benefit. Once again, do write in and let the Editors know what you think.

"The team is on a roll ..."

So runs a quote to be found elsewhere in this magazine, and reading through the reports in these pages one cannot help but be struck by the mood of optimism in all quarters of the club. Ok, so in some areas we have not been through the best of times, and yes, we do need to find some more qualified officials, but there does not seem to be a corner anywhere where improvement is not expected in the near future.

Sidetracks

Bill Dance, who featured in our teams of the sixties and seventies, is now Assistant Director of the new Sheffield Stadium. He still runs of course, but is perhaps better known as a coach with Longwood Harriers who celebrated their centenary in 1988. His squad has several fine athletes, including Martin Steele the 800 metres international.

Our keeper of the Club archives is anxious to have any material held by our older members. Prior to 1930 a great deal of valuable historical information was lost as lofts were cleared and bon-fires lit. It is difficult to appreciate how the rubbish of one century becomes the museum of the next. We are particularly keen to collate all cine film held by members and to transfer selections on to video tape. Please contact Clive Shippen on [phone number withheld].

In spite of the fact that we usually seem to get together a willing band of helpers whenever the need arises, we really are becoming desperately short of qualified track and field officials. The time may well arise when turning up at a track and field match without the requisite number of officials could result in the Club being penalised by having their points score reduced! Please consider taking the necessary steps to becoming a qualified judge or timekeeper. By doing so you would become just as much a part of the team as those who wear the "Claret and Gold" so proudly - and you'll get a ringside seat at every match. Derek Crookes of [address withheld], telephone 081-[phone number withheld], is the person to contact to find out what your next step should be.

The Clacks turned out in numbers at the Surrey Cross Country Championships in what must be a club record. Derek, in his latest comeback, raced in the event for senior men whilst his wife Sue (if you can't beat him, join him) Clack ran very well in the senior ladies. His two children David (11) and Laura (12) also ran in their respective age groups. Unfortunately, in true Clack style they both ran with heavy colds and had to drop out but they will all be back next year to complete the race and possibly improve the record with the addition of Derek's other son Peter.

LONDON SCHOOLS TRACK & FIELD CHAMPIONSHIPS

SUE and HARRY PORTER.

BATTERSEA PARK, 8 JUNE 1991

When we heard that the London Schools Track & Field Championships were cancelled last year because of a lack of helpers and officials, we volunteered to help organise the event for 1991 with Helen Whiffen and Kate Hartwell of the London Schools Association. We were promptly elected onto the LSAA Committee. It was then that we received the backing from the Belgrave Committee to promote the meeting as a "home fixture" in order to muster as many of our qualified officials as possible. We aimed to encourage as many Mums and Dads, Past Presidents and current serving Committee Members as we could to help on the day so that everything would go smoothly. Our 30 and more officials and helpers bolstered the eight or so London Schools officials so that the programme ran to time despite a longer than advertised lunch break - which was needed to finish the strawberry cheesecake and apple pie! The end result was a very well pleased London Schools Committee and a big "Thank You" to everyone who helped - and would we like to do it again next year ? On the girls side our own Jessica Dash won the Girls 800 metres, and of the boys, Sean McLean and Stanley Osuide were first and second in the high jump while Peter Lloyd took the 800 metres. We would like to add our own thanks to everyone who turned out to help on the day. Please make a diary date for 1992 - Saturday June 6th (or possibly June 13th). Thankyou.

SUE and HARRY PORTER.

Two weeks Easter training in Lanzarote

STUART PATON

STUART PATON, in his own inimitable style, reports on -

Two weeks Easter training in Lanzarote

We all met at Gatwick in the small hours of the morning of 21st March, excited at the prospect of two weeks training over Easter in the warm climate of Lanzarote. The first mishap occurred just before takeoff when Stanley realised that he had forgotten his passport. He had to return home to collect it and unfortunately missed the plane. He eventually arrived in Lanzarote four days later via Tenerife! The rest of the party arrived safely and we knew that we were in for good weather when we were met by a very bronzed looking 'Morale Officer' - Vicky Peckham. Everyone trained very hard for the two weeks, which was expected. What was not expected was the amount of tennis played in the first week. It was hard to believe the number of Pat Cash, John McEnroe, and Ivan Lendl look-alikes. This may have been due to the fact that there was a party of 15 girls from a tennis club in Wales staying there. Further evidence was that the interest in tennis lapsed in the second week, after the girls had returned home. All of a sudden the game became 'boring'. In the second week Hercules Wimbledon challenged us to a triathlon - and we lost! When have we ever lost to Hercules Wimbledon? Answers on a postcard to Belgrave Hall please. A 'kangaroo court' was held and 'Boo Boo' (Stuart Booth) was found guilty of losing us the race. He was sentenced to three years expulsion from the club, although in the clear light of the morning this was thought to be a little harsh and he was reinstated. We also took part in an Athletes versus Surrey Cricket Club football match, Belgrave supplying six of the Athletes team. We lost 2-0 and afterwards found we had six injured runners. In was noted that Surrey Cricket club were as dirty as the club hall after a 'Gentlemens' evening! The two weeks shot by very quickly and as well as all the training (I ran a total of 170 miles) we all made many new friends. This was emphasised when Ron and Ann Wyld booked a table for 15 to celebrate their 30th wedding anniversary and over twice that number turned up. What with the Karaoke and the dressing up in womens clothes, it was certainly an evening to remember. In all it was a very successful two weeks. Not only did we train very hard, but we had a great deal of fun and got an early season suntan. I believe the trip will be organised again next year, so why not come and join us?

Obituary · page 5

John Martell

Regretfully we to have to record the passing of John Martell who died on June 1st after an illness which had troubled him for some years. John was a greatly respected man, having served as Treasurer of the Amateur Athletic Association from 1971 to 1975 and then again from 1982 until 1986. These years saw dramatic changes in the way in which our sport was financed. The fortunes of the A.A.A. were turned around in spectacular fashion and much of the credit can be recorded against John's name. He was a long time Surrey Athletic Club man, becoming a Belgrave Harrier when the two clubs merged. Our condolences go to his family.

W.G. "Bill" Webb (1894-1991) — A Biographical Sketch

CLIVE SHIPPEN

Bill Webb, Past President and our longest serving Treasurer, died on Saturday 15th June 1991, just eight weeks short of his 97th birthday. CLIVE SHIPPEN relates some anecdotes of this most charming of Belgravians who will be missed by all who knew him.

Bill was regarded with much affection by Belgravians and many will recall the welcoming letter they received from him during the 30 years or so that he handled membership applications. Throughout his 64 years of membership he took a keen interest in Club affairs. He was our longest serving Treasurer and held the Presidency for two years in 1948-50. He was a fascinating conversationalist, entertaining company and his sense of humour never deserted him; though he suffered many set-backs during an eventful life. It was a life that commenced in Fulham in 1894, when horse-drawn carriages trundled the streets of Victorian London. An active youngster, Bill Webb was frequently seen racing these horse-drawn vehicles sometimes jumping a lift on the back, but he had not thought of joining a running club. Leaving school at 14 years of age he became a Telegraph Messenger Boy and then joined the South African Office as a messenger.

Thankful to be alive

When World War I broke out Bill was just 20 years old and was soon drafted into the services where he saw action in the front line. It was an experience which left him just thankful to be alive. He was a corporal in charge of a section of stretcher-bearers and on one occasion was sheltering from a heavy bombardment in an old outhouse. It was not surprising that one of his men should request permission to visit the latrines, but permission could only be given in this situation if the applicant was accompanied. There were no volunteers, so Bill decided to join the man in a swift run to the lats. Immediately they reached the latrines a salvo of shells fell on the outhouse killing the rest of his section. At Ypres he emerged from a dug-out to obtain further medical supplies and was confronted by four enemy soldiers. When he recovered from the initial shock he realised they had been disarmed and sent to the rear. Bill decided not to accompany them back but gave the dejected quartet the remains of his biscuit ration and told them to follow a taped route through the shelled and pitted landscape. They had barely reached the other side of the nearest crater when a salvo of shells killed them outright. Once again he realised that he could so easily have been one of the victims. By the time the war had ended one of his brothers had been killed and another had lost a leg. He returned to England dejected and disillusioned. To compound his problems there was mass unemployment and it was some time before he could obtain a poorly paid clerk's job. During his time in the trenches his unit had been visited by the Prince of Wales who had engaged Bill in conversation. Several years later Bill was walking along Piccadilly feeling very depressed with life and short of money when the Prince of Wales emerged from a house he was just passing and walked alongside him. The future King exchanged pleasantries with Bill as they took the short walk to Hyde Park Corner, but Bill was too shy to reveal that he knew who his companion was and also to say that they had already met in the trenches; a decision he always regretted.

Belgrave Harriers

Some time later, after passing the Civil Service entrance examination and obtaining a permanent position in the Ministry of Pensions, Bill was persuaded by a colleague to join Polytechnic Harriers. He was keen to become his old active self once more and to mix socially, but he found the members rather aloof; not uncommon amongst the "better" clubs of those days. It was following an incident during a training run on Wimbledon Common that he decided to resign his membership of Poly. The training pack were wearing their usual freshly laundered white strip that day and Bill obediently followed the group over their familiar course. When the final run for home was signalled the tempo was raised and the pack, momentarily, split to avoid a deep muddy puddle. Bill was through like a shot, showering his companions with black muddy water; an action which was received with much annoyance. The bad feeling continued in the dressing room and Bill resigned. Some years later Bill encountered another runner at the Ministry. This was none other than our own Teddy Peacock who was on his way to a Civil Service match. When Bill expressed interest in the event Teddy persuaded him to not only go along but to actually fill in for a missing team member. His performance was good enough to revive his interest and he discussed training with Peacock. When Teddy suggested that he join Belgrave Harriers Bill told him about the Poly. episode and his apprehension about joining another club. Peacock was reassuring, telling him that the Bels was full of puddle-splashers and that a little man called Footer positively expected you to race through them; what is more they were a friendly bunch and he would feel quite at home. So Bill Webb joined Belgrave Harriers on 3rd November 1927 and never forgot the welcome he was given. It was the beginning of an era which meant a great deal to him throughout his life. At 33 years of age he was a mature newcomer who made his mark immediately by finishing 8th in the Yacht '3' with 15:55 (off 25 secs). In only his second match for Belgrave he closed-in our scoring team just behind first-team regulars Wally Bayliss and Micky Pyer. The Belgravian Scribe was induced to write

"The runners have lately been joined by W.G. Webb and A. Robertshaw and I suggest that the presence of these valu-able men gives our team a new impetus of enthusiasm."

Webb and Robertshaw were just the injection Belgrave needed and they celebrated by gaining bronze team medals in the South of the Thames Senior for 1928 - our first medals in the event for 24 years! Bill went on to be our second scorer in the "Southern" and our first man home in the "National" at Leamington. A final flourish in the London-Brighton Relay and he had in one season established himself as a remarkably consistent runner who would always respond to the occasion. Within a year he was elected Captain.

Paavo Nurmi

In 1934 he was appointed Honorary Treasurer, a position he held for 14 years. He was one of our most effective Treasurers, particularly when it came to collecting subscriptions. The perfect gentleman, he would always find a charming turn of phrase when he approached late-payers. When the great Paavo Nurmi visited England he asked if he could be provided with two training companions to assist him in his preparation for a new world record. Bill Webb and Bert Footer were the lucky ones selected to help assist him in his preparations. They did so by running alternate repetition miles with the Olympic Champion/World Record Holder. Nineteen thirty six was an eventful year for Bill on two fronts. First, he married Maud, his companion for the next 50 years or so, and secondly he was elected a Life Member of the Club which meant so much to him. It was in some respects a double marriage. During his time in the Civil Service he occupied, for some two years, an office next to James Callaghan, the future Prime Minister, and got to know him well. On one occasion they travelled to Stamford Bridge together, a judge and timekeeper respectively, for the Civil Service Championships.

Walking and cycling

Just as his first race was the Yacht '3' so was his last. In 1950, at the age of 56, he finished 47th out of 56 and decided to call it a day. But he still continued his running. As the years went by, Bill's exercise was reduced to walking and cycling. At the age of 92 he was walking round Bushey Park every day, and at 94 he would still take his bicycle for short rides. This gave him more time to read biographies and enjoy the novels of Trollope and Hardy, his favourite authors. But none of this stopped him from making occasional visits to Belgrave Hall to follow our events and resume acquaintances with other longer serving members. In August 1984 the Club held a 90th birthday celebration for Bill at Belgrave Hall. He enraptured his audience with a lucid account of those early days in the Club. To mark the occasion our President of the day, Tom Carter, presented Bill with a cut-glass decanter bearing a silver necklace engraved with the good wishes of Belgrave Harriers. A year or so ago he was advised to have his heart pace-maker replaced at the Westminster Hospital so that he could visit his grand-daughter's end-of-school ceremony in the U.S.A., where Bill's elder son Martin was living. Only a few days before departure he suffered an impact fracture of the spine and the trip he was so looking forward to was off. Sheer willpower enabled him to walk again with sticks, but another accident while he was gardening (!) put paid to his outdoor exercise. It was shortly after being told that he would have to leave the house in which he had cared for himself for so long and which contained so many family memories, that Bill died. Prior to the 1988 Seoul Olympics he had been invited to a reception at the Korean Embassy by the Ambassador. Bill's charm was such that the Koreans invited him to the Olympic Games as one of their guests. But at the age of 92 he felt it was a bit too much for him, so declined. He was later invited to other Embassy functions but did not want to be seen as an ex-athlete with sticks. He always kept up with the times and would ask after John Regis, John Gladwin and Gary Staines with the same fervour as he once spoke of Nurmi and Zatopek. There is no doubt that all who had occasion to know Bill Webb, whatever their position, regarded him with respect and much affection. His was a spirit which will be sadly missed by all who valued his friendship.

Amongst the many floral tributes at his funeral was one from the Ambassador of Korea. Our condolences are extended to his family and to his Belgravian brother Frank.

World Championship Changes?

ALAN MEAD

Recently the IAAF has been provoking a lot of discussion among track and field athletes and fans regarding its consideration of changing the World Championship cycle from four years to two. ALAN MEAD rounds up some of the views that have been aired - and throws in a few of his own.

Many of the fans are predictably delighted but while athletes of other continents seem to view the prospect with equanimity, the Europeans, and in particular the British, seem somewhat less enthusiastic. IAAF President Primo Nebiolo seems to be the man behind the proposal and powerful though he is, he must be pleased to find that an original opponent of his idea, British Athletics' Promotions Officer Andy Norman, now has come out strongly in favour. Norman has been quoted recently in "Athletics Today" as saying:

"We are looking at this from a British point of view. We have the European Championships as well and we value them. But if you are an American, an African, a non-European, the European Championships don't count ... I believe this World Championships thing will be passed."

About two-thirds of the United Kingdom team do not like the idea and one of the most outspoken has been John Regis, again reported in "AT":

"It's madness. Athletes need at least one fallow year in four when they don't want to have the mental torture of a major championships. And Britain is about the only nation which takes part in all the big championships ... Commonwealth and Europeans as well as the Olympics and Worlds. Also you're going to take away from the power of the World Championships. It would make the Europeans more prestigious, because they're only once in four years.

"It's going to be ridiculous. If this goes through you're going to have World Championships this year, the Olympics in 1992, World Championships in 1993, the Commonwealth and Europeans in 1994, and so on. We won't have time to breathe ..."

One has the feeling that Nebiolo's proposal will become a reality but while thinking along the lines of changing the cycle of "major games" athletics perhaps a few other ideas might be introduced into the equation.

Who qualifies ?

Why do we need to adhere to the Olympic rule stipulating that every affiliated nation is restricted to one competitor per event as of right, irrespective of standard, or up to three competitors so long as the set standard has been achieved by each of them? The next championships will be devalued by the exclusion of an athlete of the calibre of the USA's Roger Kingdom simply because at the time of his national trial, two months earlier, he had not reached a sufficient standard of fitness while returning from injury. When the 110 metre hurdles winner stands on the podium in Tokyo's stadium a slight shadow of doubt will be cast over his victory by the American's absence - and who knows, in a Grand Prix meeting just a few days later, the two-time Olympic victor might even beat the new World Champion. Similar stories could be told regarding other events. No, why not do away with qualification by nation. The IAAF should set a high qualification standard and then allow all athletes from affiliated nations who reach that standard during the 12 months before the games to take their places at the championships. No more "Coe must go" type problems. No arguments as to whether favouritism exists among selectors. And if the hammer throw ends up with five men from the USSR in the last eight doesn't that reflect the true state of the event? By all means allow one athlete per event from any participating country where the standard has not been attained, just to keep alive the possibility of an inspired performance by a complete unknown, and maybe allow the three medal winners from the previous championships entry without qualification by performance.

We would then have a complete contrast to the Olympics where the worthy notion of "taking part" is, supposedly, paramount. It may well be that in time athletes do not necessarily regard the Olympics as the Games to aim at, leaving them to become more of a celebration of sporting and cultural achievements rather than pure competition. Doesn't the sport of Athletics need a proper World Championships on a more frequent basis, leaving the "fill-in" Olympics to the the more circus like activities of synchro-trampolining and the spectacle of a man who is so far removed from being among the world's top ski jumpers that he actually makes news when he takes part.

GORDON DOUBLEDAY

Gordon likes to keep in touch with old and new friends at the club and has gained a remarkable insight into the whereabouts of club members by volunteering to address those envelopes which arrive from the club onto your doormat. He writes of:

Belgrave Geography

When the club was formed in 1887, it drew its members largely from Belgravia, Pimlico, Chelsea, Westminster and across the river from Battersea and Wandsworth. In those days people worked long hours and public transport was less available. Nevertheless, their enthusiasm and love of sport helped to lay the foundations of the Belgrave Harriers of today.

Leaping on half a century we find that in the 'thirties our membership had extended to places like Fulham, Hammersmith and Kensington, and over the river again to Balham, Brixton, Clapham, Streatham, Tooting and Wimbledon. Also to areas outside the London postal districts like Carshalton, Epsom, New Malden, Sutton and Wallington with a few more even further afield. Nowadays, with people having more leisure time, better transport and a higher percentage of car owners (we've always had a few who would cycle to meetings), our members are even more scattered. Also, many members, Past Presidents, Hon. Life Members and Vice Presidents have moved away from London. I have been through the members' address list and excepting London and the adjoining counties of Surrey, Middlesex, Kent and Essex, I have compiled a list of the various counties to which club mail is sent. The aforementioned area covers the bulk of our membership; the "Home Counties" include Colchester (Essex) and Canterbury (Kent) as well as other places not exactly Metropolitan. Jon Ridgeon - back on the track

CHARLES DICKINSON

CHARLES DICKINSON recently caught up with the man who raced to a silver medal at the 1987 World Championships and then suffered years of injury and illness.

" ... a mad passion for athletics ... "

" After recovering from a debilitating virus in 1988, he then suffered a ruptured left achilles in the winter of 1989. "

On Saturday May 11th Jon Ridgeon stepped out onto the track in the British League match at Barnet Copthall and raced for the first time since January 1989. When this filtered through to the athletics press, few items of news can have been greeted with such widespread relief and pleasure. For Belgravians there was also the satisfaction of seeing Jon racing in Belgrave colours for the first time since he joined the club in the summer of 1989. This appearance, hopefully, marks the first stage of a successful comeback after two and a half years of battling against illness and injury. Indeed, Jon has not really been fully 'right' since his wonderful silver medal in the World Championships 110 metre hurdles at Rome in 1987 at the age of 20. The following winter he was struck down with a virus which hampered his preparations for the 1988 Olympics. Despite this Jon did remarkably well to finish 5th in the final in Seoul, but since January 1989 two very bad achilles tendon injuries have kept him out of competition altogether. I talked to Jon just after he ran at Barnet; he exuded determination and a mad passion for athletics. It has been this single mindedness that has brought him through these years of injury. Now he has set his mind on returning to the very top level of competition, nothing less will do. As a boy, Jon was always been keen on sport and encouraged by his parents. His mother Margaret had been an English schoolgirl hurdles champion. He took up hurdling seriously at the age of 15 in 1982, while attending the local secondary school at his home town of Newmarket and won the English schoolboy hurdles title. He became European Junior Champion in 1985 when 18, and was runner up to Colin Jackson in the World Junior Championships the following year. After leaving school, Jon spent a year studying in America then returned to read Geography at Cambridge University. Here Jon combined serious study with very hard training and shot to fame in 1987 with his World Championships silver in what he considers to be his best season. He also lowered the United Kingdom record three times finishing with his best time of 13.29 and was awarded the accolade of British male athlete of the year. Jon feels that his injury problems may have been brought on by the combination of hard study and flat out training. Athletes at the very top level make such enormous demands of their bodies that they really need medical backup, physiotherapy and regular massage. Without these, weaknesses such as Jon's thin achilles tendons become easily susceptible to injury. Jon's injury problems certainly went on and on. After recovering from a debilitating virus in 1988, he then suffered a ruptured left achilles in the winter of 1989. It was operated on in June that year and with no medical aftercare offered or available in this country Jon was fortunate enough to be given a scholarship worth $20,000 from the Canberra Institute of Sport in Australia. The Institute is funded by Federal Government and has recently appointed Rob de Castella as it's director. The outstanding performances by Australians in the Commonwealth Games in Auckland bear testimony to the central support given to athletes in that country. Jon spent five months at the Institute receiving six hours of physiotherapy every day. At first progress was slow and painful but eventually recovery was complete. Jon was glad to be able to partially repay the scholarship by giving youngsters some hurdling coaching. Jon had only been back in England four months when disaster struck again, his right achilles broke down and had to be operated on. This time he financed the return to Canberra himself, leaving in September and not returning to England until April this year. Again he went through months of the same slow recuperation including weights, circuits, cycling and pool work. Life in Australia did have its compensations and in between sessions at the Institute Jon learned to love the Australian outdoors, made many friends and made sure that he enjoyed life. Jon has been a member of Belgrave since he left Cambridge in the summer of 1989. He moved into a house off Clapham Common and was influenced to join the club by the friendliness of such athletes as Marcus Adam, Abi Ekoku, and Phil Goedluck. Whilst recovering from his injuries he has been able to support himself by doing some work for the media including athletics commentating for the radio. This year Jon is simply glad to be back competing again. He is training at about 70% of the load he was undertaking in his best years, being very cautious not to build up too quickly. He still has the enthusiasm and ambition to want to become the best in the world and has targeted the 110 metre hurdles at next years Olympics as his goal. Jon is very philosophical about his future, if he is not able to achieve the top level he will be unable to support himself as a full time athlete and will have to look for a career outside athletics, possibly in the media. And if he has another major injury?

"If that happened I would just have to pack it in, I don't think I could face another six months of rehabilitation.''

But for the moment, Jon is gradually chipping off the hundredths with every race and although only training since April he is fast getting back to very respectable times. It may be too much to expect him to make the British team for the World Championships this year but he will be lining up at Birmingham on July 26th in the trial and giving it his best. We'll all keep our fingers crossed. It's wonderful to see him back. What a great asset he is to the club.

Club Championships

CHARLES DICKINSON

The Club Championships were held over four separate evenings in May and June at Battersea Park and as luck would have it every one of those evenings was cold, wet and windy; times and distances were badly affected. CHARLES DICKINSON recalls some of the events.

The Mens 10000m was dominated and won by Roger Alsop showing great confidence after a fine p.b. in the Surrey Championships two weeks earlier. In cold and very windy conditions, Roger led from the start and was easily able to cover attempted breaks by Martin Lake at 17 laps and Charlie Dickinson at 5 laps from the finish. Roger put his final effort in with 600m to go and won with ease. A very well deserved victory.

Easily the best Club 5000m Championship race for many years was won by Marcello Bizio with a devastating sprint in the final straight. A closely matched group of many of Belgrave's improving new middle distance runners assembled at the start of the race. This time the conditions could be described as cool and wet. A determined looking Gary Pitts, fresh from some good Spring road race performances and early track form, cut out the pace from the start. With 1600m covered in 4 minutes 50 seconds, however, it was not quick enough to shake off a bunch consisting of: Roger Alsop, Marcello Bizio, Martin Lake and the two New Zealanders Gavin Teahan and Mark Anderson. Still leading at 8 laps Gary Pitts surprisingly pulled out of the race which left Gavin Teahan and Roger Alsop gradually pulling away with Marcello from a hard chasing Martin Lake and Mark Anderson. Then, over the last lap, Gavin made his effort and it appeared for a while that he had gone clear. Marcello never once gave up the chase though. He had gone under 50 minutes for 10 miles on the road only the day before and now he unleashed a magnificent sprint to win in the last 50 metres. Roger Alsop chased hard for 3rd place and Mark Anderson used his 800m pace to overtake Martin Lake with a 61 second last lap.

There were some good 800m races on another cold and windy evening. In the Senior Ladies race Wendy Cotterell proved to be too strong for Jacintha Coppinger and there were other convincing wins by M. Hancock with 2:16.9 in the Boys race and an almost solo 2:02.1 by Youth F.Nico who looked very impressive. The Senior Men were led through the first lap by Nigel Clarke in a fairly modest time but the field began to stretch down the back straight when efforts came from Australian Brandon Reid and New Zealanders Gavin Teahan and Mark Anderson. It was Mark who made the decisive move at 200m and he just sustained it to the finish, holding off a fast final sprint from Brandon.

Veterans' Age Factors

BILL LAWS

by BILL LAWS

When Charlie Walker returned from the 1990 World Vets Championships, held in Eugene, USA, with an England team silver medal for the 10km cross country he didn't stop talking about it for a fortnight. Certainly he carried the thing to all his races and if you took the trouble to ask how he got on then you were in for a step by step repeat of every detail of the actual performance. It is astonishing that at the age of 60+ Charlie is as enthusiastic about our sport as when he was winning Surrey Championships 40 years ago. It is interesting to speculate whether age has diminished his relative performances. Not an easy question - well not until now that is. At Eugene the Heptathlon and Decathlon for all age groups were scored using factors to relate all age performances to that of senior athletes at their best. These factors have now been worked out for all events and ages up to 90! So now, if you think someone's performance is good you can equate it to a 'prime time' (or distance) using these tables. The full list is too comprehensive for this article but take the 800m, 5km/10km for men. The listed factors by which times should be multiplied to give 'prime time' are as shown at the foot of this page.

So if Charlie ran 39:20, his 'prime time' equivalent for the 10km would be;

(39 x 60) + 20 = 2360 x .8038 = 1897 secs. = 31 mins. 37 secs.

It would be interesting to know if he ran that time as a senior.

So there it is, you can compare yourselves directly with the John Regis's, John Gladwins and Paul Evans's of this world. Even more interesting, they can see what they might be doing in 20 years time!

The Gentlemen's Evening

It is my job as Social Secretary to try to get members of the club to socialise. To date we have held discos, race nights and the odd meal. My special thanks here to Gerry Adams and Chris Sweeney, even if the latter is a Tipton Harrier. Those two became the marketing department and anyone who saw the posters and literature displayed in the club hall couldn't fail to be impressed! With Gerry and Chris helping we sold enough tickets to break even so when the 4th May came we crossed our fingers and it was all systems go! On the night, three girl friends of mine volunteered to run the bar and so much was drunk that the bar was drunk dry. It is doubtful whether that has happened before even when Keith Sinclair was in his prime! It is fair to say that we took a great deal of money behind the bar. The entertainment lasted about three hours and was enjoyed by all. Once everyone had left, the hall looked as though a bomb had hit it. It was a real mess. The clearing up was left until the next day, Sunday morning, and Marcello led the way by washing and hoovering all the floors and Gerry and I washed every single glass. Thanks lads! The general consensus was that it was a very successful evening so another one will be organised in the future. If you have any ideas for a social event, or wish to hire the hall, just give me a ring. If I can help I certainly will.

Paul Evans: fastest half-marathon in the world for 1991

Paul has been showing some cracking half-marathon form. On March 10th in cold, wet and windy conditions at Hastings he set up what was the fastest time by a Briton for the year - 63:30 - despite the course being hilly and not particularly to his liking. Put firmly into the minor places were Chris Robison, Simon Robert Naali of Tanzania, who was third in last year's Commonwealth Games and who has a best of 61:47, and Nick Rose. A £10,000 bonus had been on offer for anyone breaking 62:00 at Hastings and Paul's thoughts of possibly having had a go for it proved to be perfectly realistic when just a week later in Lisbon he ran an astounding 61:44! Four thousand runners took part but as the finishing stretch was reached it was down to a bunch of five until Paul kicked his way clear. Some well known names featured among the vanquished - Dave Long, Steve Jones, Hugh Jones - and his time placed him at 6th on the all-time British list for the distance and fastest in the world for 1991 so far. Other classy runs came on April 20th when the Belgravian clocked 47:17 for 7th place in the Trevira Twosome '10', set in New York's Central Park, and May 22nd when he failed to match the final kick of Paul Davies-Hale and Dave Lewis and was forced to accept an 8 second defeat in the Blackburn 5k road race; his time was 13:53.

ENFIELD, 22 JUN 1991

Bels. 2nd Again

With the GB v Germany match on the previous Wednesday, the Belfast meeting the day before and the selection meeting for the World Student Games team the day after, it was clear that we would be well below strength in several events and so it proved. But although we were lying last for half the match, some excellent performances squeezed us up to second place, (the first time we've gained two second places in one season) to put us in clear second spot in the league. In the sprints it was great to see Marcus back winning the 'B' with Lennie proving the point that he should have the 'A' slot with a marginally faster time. But in the 200 metres Marcus powered round the bend, coasted the straight, and ran a new BAL record of 20.86 to erase John Regis' 20.88. The wind, at +2.2mps was reminiscent of the positive conditions at Auckland but nothing should detract from Marcus' welcome return to form. Jon Ridgeon jetted in from Belfast to win the 'A' 110 metre hurdles in a best time since his welcome return from injury while Paul Beaumont made it a unique hurdles double by winning a fine 'A' 400 metre hurdles in a season's best. Stuart Paton is back! He's been sidelined for some time with illness but after keeping off the pace and following for most of the race we saw a flash of the old change of speed and our man was there. Another win. Marcello had a bet with Bill Laws that he would break 9:20 but in bad conditions it wasn't to be. He did, however, run a pb to get 3rd in the 'A' and the bet was off! A fitting reward. Paul Evans ran a superb '5000'. Winding up the pace five laps out, he recorded a sub-14 effort running the last mile in 4:16. Ollie Foote ran a 62 second last lap to reel in Gentry of Newham to gain a fine second place in the 'B'. The field events saw several of our new members making their debuts. Ray Chambers acquitted himself well in the javelin while Darrin Morris threw a lifetime best to win the discus in 58.58 to push the UK's European representative down to second place. Sadly Abi missed his first BAL match due to injury but Chris Symonds showed what a great Club man he is by doing all throws and massing vital points which helped us to beat Birchfield by just two points. Another newcomer, Dylan McDermott, provided us with a unique double, winning the 'B' pole vault - again in a season's best of 4.70 metres - behind the ubiquitous Mike Edwards who somehow managed 5.25 metres despite a stomach muscle pull. What a competitor! Otherwise, the jumps were generally decimated by injury with the exception of the high jump where Warren Caswell leapt a season's best of 2.15 metres to get close to Dalton Grant. But perhaps the most gratifying moment was seeing Ian Wells gain his 400 metre hurdles place on merit. A team manager's dream - always turning up and covering a whole range of events having scored many points at his specialist events. This year he has run several personal bests, much to our pleasure. The team is on a roll and its members are glad to be part of it!

Feature · page 26

22.79 for Regis!

It was in an unaccustomed 3rd place over 200 metres that John Regis found himself at the Welsh Championships on June 1st - and with a time of 22.79 as well! There had been the little matter of ten flights of 2'6" barriers to negotiate along the way, however. The race had been set up as an attack on the 1960 World Best of 22.69, set by American Glen Davis when he was Olympic Champion. The other main contenders, apart from John, were Colin Jackson and Nigel Walker. Our man used to hurdle at school and was reported before the event to have stated that once around the turn, Jackson and the rest would "just see my back". As it turned out though, European 110 metre hurdles Champion Jackson was in top form in his first race of the season and it was he who set new World figures with 22.63. Walker came home in 22.77 while John's time equalled the old European Best set in 1982 by Axel Schaumann of the Federal Republic of Germany.

Marcus finding good form

Marcus Adam has been laying down a platform of solid performances during the early season and it could well be that we will we see him spring right back into the spotlight come the races that really matter. In Europe's first IAAF/MOBIL Grand Prix of 1991, held at Bratislava on June 4th, he placed 5th over 200 metres in 21.18. At Dijon in France on June 15th he raced to a clear victory over the Bulgarian champion Nikolai Antonov, 20.63 to 20.82. For good measure at Dijon he also took the "C" 100m race, facing a 2.3mps headwind, in 10.64. Incidentally Marcus's 200m time at this meeting equalled his legal pb, although his Commonwealth Games winning 20.10 was only marginally wind assisted.

Gary Staines cracks his 10,000m best.

Grand Prix meetings get off the ground a little earlier in the States, of course, and at San Jose in the Bruce Jenner meeting on May 25th Gary Staines clocked an outstanding 7:47.20 for 3000 metres. In the GB v Germany match at Crystal Palace he clocked an almost identical 7:47.95 but was outkicked in the closing stages by Rob. Denmark. Gary went on to place second in the European Cup 5000 metres, the only man in the field prepared to attempt to go with Antibo of Italy, and he was rewarded with 13:35.08. But then the real stunner came at the Bislett Games in Oslo on Saturday 6th July when he smashed his pb and club record for the 10000 metres, finishing 6th in 27:48.73 and removing just over 24 seconds from his previous best in the process.

European Best and then the fastest man in the world falls to Regis.

John suffered a defeat by Linford Christie over 200 metres at the Palace in the match v. Germany but bounced back a few days later to break the European Best for 300 metres in Belfast on June 21st.
