EDITORIAL

There has been much talk in recent months of the need for an inter-club competition during the summer season. Many views put forward seem to favour a league system with, we presume, promotion and relegation; or a super-league including the top clubs in the unofficial ranking list - a list insufficiently broad to reflect true team strength.

Whilst we do not agree with the form of the proposals so far presented we fully understand the motives that prompt them. Despite the excellent, conscientious work of our National and Area coaches and the efforts of those many others who endeavour to produce medal-winning Britons, British Athletics is waning. And when compared with many other sports, waning fast.

At a recent symposium on Recreational Planning, representatives of the Sports Council and National Playing Fields Association claimed that participation in Track and Field had shown little increase in recent years while the progress of many other sports was described as no less than "explosive". It was also revealed that some local authorities are being advised against constructing running tracks - many of which, to date, have been nothing more than "white elephants".

In our view cross-country, long-distance and road-relay running are not suffering to the same degree because they are more closely identified with team participation. But Track and Field is a sport of many activities, and here lies it's weakness at club level. Furthermore, because of a chaotic summer programme of trophy meetings, inter-club events, various championships "invitation" events (of which there are hundreds) in the midst of which another programme of races is fitted to facilitate selection of our International teams, it is no wonder that team competition in the summer months is little short of farcical. Which is the most important event for the Club to win? Everyone has his own idea and club spirit during this period is at its lowest ebb; yet it is the clubs that form the base of the Athletics Pyramid. It is at club level then that the interest, indeed fervour, for Athletics should be engendered and thus enable the pyramid's apex to be pushed higher.

A NATIONAL A.A.A. CLUB CHAMPIONSHIP is urgently needed. All events, except perhaps the walks, marathon and six miles, should be included. All flat races could be relays, except the 3 miles (decided on Cross-country scoring) and all the remaining events have 2 men per team. Field events could be decided on the aggregate best heights or distances of each pair. Similar competitions are held in other countries and the Army A.A. has held their inter-unit Championships on this basis for many years, and with great success.

The Area Associations of the South, Midland and North would need to stage one or, at the most, two eliminating rounds with the three winners going through to the final and the three remaining places being decided, perhaps, on performance scoring tables.

Studded with some quality invitation events such a "Cup Final" would attract a large following - and if it attracted the bell-ringing, flag-waving, football-type support the "Brighton" knew (and it could) then it would be a certain success. As for venue, well let us say that last August's British Games could have done with such a Championship around which to frame it's programme.

It is our belief that this prestige event would persuade clubs to pay more attention to the Cinderella events, create at least one TEAM red-letter day in the summer and be a useful first step in a long overdue re-appraisal of the British Athletics scene.

* * * * *

We may take it that the much-discussed and much-reviled Individual athlete registration scheme is now dead. The A.A.A. have accepted that there are many inherent difficulties in such a scheme as originally planned,- not least being the interference with Club life and activity and the heavy expense ratio involved - but the fact remains that the A.A.A. needs more finance to carry through its programmes.

It is probable that we shall all be asked to give more financial support to the A.A.A. than in the past; and it is possible that all members may be asked to pay a higher Club subscription permitting a per capita payment to be made to the A.A.A. on behalf of each subscribing first-claim member.

This would be in accord with the A.A.A. and Area Associations' need for more finance; and the Government Department's (from which considerable grant-aid has been forthcoming) suggestion that athletes themselves should provide more money for their Sport than heretofore.

HARRY EVANS

A.A.H. (acknowledging the help of that other great character the late Jimmy Belchamber and his notes of years ago)

(Born 5.5.1881, died 27.1.1967)

It is with profound regret that we advise the passing of Harry Evans in his sixtieth year of membership; and it is with gratitude that we recall his many years of service to the Club and the Sport. His unfailing good humour, his contempt of the overcoat, his absolute dependability and complete integrity and high degree of competence endeared him to us all. To be disqualified by Harry Evans was to put the issue beyond all doubt; his decisions were accepted without the merest shadow of a protest for everyone accepted that his judgements could not be improved upon. He was without malice or guile. Uprighteousness, integrity and sincerity were to us synonymous with Harry Evans; and we shall miss him tremendously, whilst thankful for those very full years from 1907 to 1967 when Belgrave meant so much to him and he gave so much of himself to us.

It was in 1907 that Harry was approached by a fellow baker and asked to support the Baker's Charity Athletic Meeting at the old Kensal Rise Track by competing in a walking race. Harry agreed, and surprised himself by finishing a close second to the trade champion.

Another baker named Savage, who had been the World's professional champion and whose records made under the name of "Billy Franks" in 1882 still stand, thereupon became interested in Harry and, having a son in Belgrave, he introduced Harry to our club.

Up till this time the Bels had never been a power to be reckoned with in Championships of any kind. It is true that their stationery and Entry Forms showed that once they had been placed third in the South of the Thames Cross Country Championship, but that was their sole team honour. In those days the betting element had invaded the world of Amateur Athletics and became fairly well rooted there. The Authorities were striving to suppress it but despite their efforts it was easily possible to back one's self or one's fancy at any athletic meeting of note. At Stamford Bridge itself there was a spot near the rails in the back straight where, despite both Police and Authorities, a brisk business was done for many years. In most of the prominent clubs there was a betting coterie and many young athletes found teachers willing to supervise their training who expected them to run or hold back as ordered. Now it is undeniable that this canker had eaten deep in the Belgrave Harriers as in many other clubs; and where handicap wins and odds as long as possible are the objective, it naturally follows that the attainment of high team honours is extremely unlikely. The aim is not high enough.

From the outset, Harry fought openly and strenuously against this, and in my opinion, any tribute of thanks we pay him should commence with the acknowledgement of this, as the club's numerous team victories have all been achieved since the days when he showed his fellow clubmen that true sportsmanship demanded that one should give of his utmost in every race.

The Walking Captain of the club at that time was F.W. Coomber, a fine walker and a good sportsman, who quickly realised Harry's potential ability. He taught Harry so thoroughly that in the next club race to be decided the pupil walked away from the teacher. So Harry was urged to enter for the two-mile handicap at the Stamford Bridge Open "Good Friday" meeting. Hot Cross Buns, being a National necessity at that time, Harry had to put in a stretch of 36 hours on them. During this period he had only two short rests. The race commenced only a few hours after he left the Bakery but Harry won the race by 75 yards.

The Belgrave urging and cheering throughout the race was insistent and incessant, and he learned afterwards the Bels had backed him very heavily. The results recorded later will show that he kept on winning despite the frowns of those who wanted his wins to be accompanied with bets at favourable odds. Harry certainly had no time for the athlete who was not in the game for the love of it and for the glory of winning or fighting to do so.

Shortly after the Good Friday race Harry competed at Herne Hill and was a close third in the very fast time of 13mins.32secs. Next he won a 2 mile Walk at Redhill. Here Tommy Eaton, whom many of you will know as our pre-war masseur was the virtual scratch man together with Alf Pateman. Harry followed with a 2nd prize at the Tottenham Hospital Sports and a first prize at Sutton.

This Sutton race was memorable in as much as Harry was receiving a small start from A.E. Rowlands, the New Zealand Champion, who was over here for the 1908 Olympic Games and who had been recording some very fast times. He was a strong favourite for the race and actually caught Harry, but Harry fought on to gain the victory by a yard.

This race was followed by a second place race at Epsom. During this season Harry had won every Club handicap from the scratch mark. During the Winter Season he won the club 10 miles Championship in 78 minutes.

Following this he walked in the A.A.A. 7 Miles Championship and recorded the fast time of 54 mins.30secs., and in the 2 miles Championship recording 14mins.10secs. He followed this up with a win at 2 miles on the old White City track of 3 laps to the mile. This was a very fast track, certainly faster than Stamford Bridge, but not quite so fast as the present track there.

A second place in the L.A.C. Open Walk at Stamford Bridge came next and this was followed by a first at Clapton. This was undoubtedly one of Harry's finest performances. E.J. Webb, a winner of five A.A.A. Championships gave Harry a short start and caught him with a lap to go. Harry held him and one of the judges ran the whole of the lap in case either broke contact. It was neck and neck all the way with Harry just winning.

By now we have got to the Winter Season of 1909 and here we find Harry winning the first Junior Individual and Team Race promoted by the Road Walking Association. Belgrave lost the team race by one point, but hereafter there was another honour recorded on our letter headings.

This race started at Brentford and the Course mapped out was one passing Gunnersbury Park, crossing Ealing Common, climbing Hanger Hill, dipping down to Alperton and climbing part of the way to Wembley before returning. The road over Hanger Hill was then narrow with a very second-class surface. About two miles of tar-macadam and eight miles of rolled granite were generally to be expected in a ten mile race at this time, and in this race the course was typical. In winning Harry recorded the splendid time of 81mins.37secs. Harry was more than disappointed with the team's position of 2nd as earlier in the day there had been a two mile handicap at Stamford Bridge and several of the team had raced there before participating in the longer race for their club.

Following this, the club arranged a fair number of inter-club races and in these, although Harry was generally first man home, our successes were few as usually only three or four Belgravians turned out.

In the years 1909 to 1911, Harry gained six first class standard medals in the A.A.A. Championships, three at seven miles and three at two miles, and did well against the Champions of the day who included G.E. LARNER, E.J. WEBB and H.V.L. ROSS.

Now came a long break in Harry's athletic career. He married, and a growing family, his wife's ill-health and night work kept him out of the sport for some time. This period of inactivity was followed by the 1914-18 World War. Harry was an early volunteer for the forces. He was captured and spent a long while in a German prison camp. On returning to England in 1919 he was talked into training again by Jimmy Belchamber. Although he did not get back the pace that had formerly made him so formidable he soon became a very sound and reliable team man at seven and ten miles. In the Winter seasons of 1919-20 and 1920-21 Belgrave were the winners of most of the seven and ten miles scratch team races. We had seven walkers who could be relied upon to finish well up. Usually we could rely upon at least six of the seven turning up. These seven were Jimmy Belchamber, Harry Evans, Harry Hanger, Bill Hawker, Bob Ricketts, Alec Spendlove and Charlie Wells. Amongst these Harry was at a disadvantage in respect of age, but he was in our scoring four many times. He found pace enough also to win some more prizes at 2 miles on the track, a 1st at Paddington just after the 1920 Olympic Games being especially commendable. In this race the British, Australian and South African Olympic walkers were competing. Harry's handicap mark was by no means a long one. He also won a first prize at Writtle and a second at Hornsey. On the road he was awarded the style prize in the Islington Borough Walk in March 1920.

Twice Harry attempted the 20 Miles R.W.A.Championship but found that this was not his distance. This was before 1914. When he made his post-war come-back he could not be persuaded to try this distance.

As a walking judge Harry had no superior. He had been on the R.W.A. list since early 1921 and officiated at many A.A.A.Championships, the 1948 Olympic Games, and at thousands of races on track and on the road. His worth to the Club as an adviser on style and fair walking was inestimable. As a committee man he served a life-time and held the Presidency of the Club worthily for two years, 1942 and 1943, in addition to being a Life Member.

He was a great believer in breathing exercises and in physical exercise for the developement of the stomach muscles, and in his earlier days profited by weight lifting. Harry was acutely aware as a young baker of the high mortality rate in the trade, largely due to chills etc. developed by working at high temperatures during the night and then going out into the early morning cold; and he sought to protect himself by being physically fit. Indirectly this brought him into sport and the Belgrave Harriers, where generations of us have learned to regard him with great affection.

And so we take our leave of one of Belgrave's noblest sons....... R.I.P.

A.A.H. (acknowledging the help of that other great character the late Jimmy Belchamber and his notes of years ago)

CYRIL YESCOMBE

AAA

(BORN 9.10.1919, DIED 21.1.1967)

We mourn the passing of Cyril Yescombe, aged 47, and our sincere condolences are extended to Mrs Yescombe, who has helped us so often, and her family.

Cyril joined us in early 1955 and we had come to know him as a great hearted walker who whilst not aspiring to the higher placings, yet gave stability in the middle reaches, and was a source of encouragement to us all.

Cyril competed in the club 10 miles Championship on January 21st and covered the course in 92 minutes. Two or three hours later a heart attack seized him whilst at the wheel of his car on his way home, from which he died almost immediately. We are indeed sorry to have lost such a good Belgravian.

Among his best walking performances were his eleventh place in the RWA 50 Kilometres Championship at Leyland in 1957, in sub-tropical conditions, which gained him his Club Honours Badge; and his 100 miles walk recorded in our current Handbook.

Inspector Cyril Yescombe was very popular indeed amongst his Metropolitan Police colleagues and was one of the two Vice-Chairman of the Metropolitan Police Walking Club. In recent months he had done valuable work in training the young Police Cadets and his splendid example will have left lasting impressions for the good on a new generation of policeman.

He will be sorely missed by us all. R.I.P.

HON SEC'S NOTES

A.A.H.

This year sees the Centenary of Cross-Country Running and no doubt more will be heard of this when the Autumn comes again. It is interesting to read the words of WALTER RYE, the first President of Thames Hare and Hounds. He wrote in 1894: "At the end of 1867 a few members (of whom the writer was one) of the Thames Rowing Club at Putney conceived the idea of holding some cross-country steeplechase during the winter season with the idea of keeping themselves more or less in condition until rowing began again. As may well be imagined the arrangements of Thames Handicap Steeplechase No 1, as it was called, were primitive in the extreme and indeed the whole affair was treated more as a joke than anything else. The competitors were taken up to the starting place on Wimbledon Common, the edge of the Beverley Brook by the bridge in a bus, and had to dress how they could, and the race was run in the dark over 2¼ miles of the roughest and boggiest part of the Common, then very different indeed as to its surface from what it has now become after recent drainage. Still there were a dozen starters out of twenty entries, and the affair being the first cross-country steeplechase (not being at a school) attracted much attention".

In the following winter (1868) it was decided to try paper-chasing and Walter Rye continues:- "Our first run took place on October 17th 1868 from the 'Kings Head', Roehampton, then as it still is, our headquarters, and the beau ideal in many respects of a paper-chaser's home, being a quaint old wooden built inn, squatted behind a great wych elm, covered with creepers, and in the middle of real open country. We had secured the written leave of Earl Spencer, who then owned Wimbledon Common, to run over it, and still claim to be the only club who can drop paper over the heath, as having the right before there were such things as conservators and their bye-laws; and all existing rights were saved by the Act."

Rye writes on the first championship races thus:- "It was not till 1870 that the first real championship race was started though this attempt to bring it off in the wilds of Epping Forest proved a great failure, everyone losing his way separately on his own account. Next year a carefully laid out course was chosen from Roehampton, over the Common by Merton and Morden, West Barnes and Crooked Billet Lane, about 11¾ miles. The Thames H & H won the first time scoring 35 as against South London H. 58 and the Spartans 94"

Much more of a fascinating nature follows but we must conclude....... "When the run is over, the tub - lukewarm if it can be had - is in universal request, followed, if possible, by a cold douche by means of a bucketful of water from the hands of a stable-helper. If the run has been extra wet or cold, a steaming glass of port negus may be wisely taken as a precaution, but it is a singular thing that both before and after the meal which terminates the evening, 'ginger beer and gin' is the favourite drink, having probably been found by long experience to best carry off the extra heat of the body caused by a long run."

* * * * *

Our own eighty years of history have been much in mind lately, and a letter published in "Athletics Weekly" and many local newspapers asking for details and relics of our very early days, whilst widely seen and noted by many interested persons has, as yet, added nothing to the sum total of knowledge of our first twenty years or so. Not that this surprises, for a stripling of 20 years in the Clubs earliest Embankment races, would now be awaiting his 100th birthday!

It may be, however, that a second or third generation have preserved details of Belgrave of the late 1800's; and if this is so we should be glad if they could find their way to the Hon. Secretary, and so assist in his researches.

If you know of any such persons please try and obtain their co-operation, for time is all too short.

* * * * *

One reads of the recent discovery of Chaucerian manuscripts, and of lost and unknown works of Leonardo da Vinci coming to light after centuries- and wonders what happened to the Belgrave Harriers Minute Books, 1887-1907!

* * * * *

Editors our story from 1920 onwards has been lovingly recorded in the pages of the Belgravian in its sporting results, its comments, its humour, and its exhortations. The historian of the future has bricks in plenty from which to fashion his edifice - lets hope he doesn't drop too many of them!

* * * * *

Many members will know that the Amateur Athletic Association has recently entered into an arrangement with a professional football pools organisation whereby Amateur Athletics will receive some monetary benefit, the participating clubs also receive an addition to their funds; and of course the individual subscriber have his chance of winning one of the prizes offered.

We in Belgrave have, of course, enjoyed the fruits of our own scheme and the work of many dedicated members for over thirty years; and our own freehold Headquarters is a monument to the many Club helpers who have participated in this great Club effort since the 1930's. We now have the task of maintenance and improvement.

A sense of duty and a wish to help the AAA and BAAB in their financial problems prompted your Finance Committee and General Committee to give detailed consideration in all its aspects to the new Atlas Pool; and thought was given to the possible termination of our own scheme and the transfer of interested members - if they so wished - into the newly introduced Pool. However, a conscientious and unbiased appraisal of the merits of the two main money-raising schemes under discussion (and of other subsidiary competitions) and their detailed workings convinced your Committee that we should continue with our own scheme; and intensify our efforts within the Club to ensure the maximum efficiency of our own Belgrave Supporters Scheme.

This decision was agreed unanimously and it is intended to continue our existing scheme at least until the end of the 1968 Football Season. Meantime the situation will be kept under review.

Les Cohen, in addition to his AAA Championship Hon Secretary's duties and hundreds of other official chores, still finds time with the assistance of his wife, to conduct our scheme and has agreed enthusiastically to carry on. To Les and Mary our very great thanks are due and also to those members who have given such loyal support, which has resulted in our unrivalled facilities and activities. There is room for much more support and Les Cohen our Hon Treasurer will be pleased to give further information - addresses are in our Handbook.

We have also decided to give token support to the newly introduced Pool and members who would like to participate in this Pool should contact Frank Webb - another great worker for the Club and its finances.

JUNIOR SECTION

REG.HOPKINS

We have a large group of Boys meeting for training and competition at Battersea Park track every Sunday morning. Why don't you join us? Every three weeks a field events meeting is organised by Bill Couzens and Dave Ansell in which all the boys take part. Regular events that are most popular are the Shot, Javelin and High Jump. We intend in the coming weeks to put on some races over most distances, including relays. We even have our own Bamboo pole for vaulting, must admit nobody does it very well yet, but it does cause a few laughs.

Welcome to William Curtin who has just joined us. We hear he recently won the London Schools Cross Country Champs. so he will certainly help to strengthen our team.

No less a welcome to Ray Gale who although only a novice, looks promising. He certainly must be keen for he cycles all the way from Bethnal Green, East London to be present at our Sunday morning meetings.

We are sorry to hear that Robert Poole has gone to live in Reading. He wishes to remain a member of Belgrave and will get down to race for us whenever he can. Good for you Bob!

Although we have built up quite a large Boys Section in the Club, we have fallen low in numbers of Youths. We are now canvassing for new members so put the word about and see if there are any friends at school you can introduce into the Club. They don't have to be good, that they will become when they have been in Belgrave for a short time.

Harry Hare reminisces

This Club photograph was taken in the early days of 1921 on the occasion of the 7 mile C.C.Championship for the"H.T.BLACKSTAFFE" Shield, This was the ninth contest. First competed for in 1910 with a lapse during part of the 1914-18 War. The winner was J.F.Gray, with H.Parker second and R. Savage third.

1 F.FORD 2 "THE HOST" 3 C.COOK 4 E.GORDON 5 C.C.ARNOLD 6 A.F.W.MACHER 7 H.WRIGHT 8 H.FEARN 9 BOY (?SON OF) 10 J.B.BELCHAMBER 11 - 12 A.E.MACHER 13 H.GOODFELLOW 14 G.STILL 15 - 16 R.J.SAVAGE 17 J.F.GRAY 18 H.PARKER 19 A.L.PORTER 20 J.W.MACHER 21 C.H.SPEECHLEY 22 - 23 C.P.SEXTON 24 - 25 J.F.SATCHELL 26 - 27 - 28 W.D.PRITCHARD 29 - 30 J.PRITCHARD 31 A.E.CHORLEY 32 - 33 - 34 R.W.RICKETTS 35 J.W.GILBERT 36 C.C.HARRIS 37 J.K.CHALK 38 - 39 "THE POTMAN" 40 J.J.CRICHTON 41 W.H.HARE 42 E.G.COOPER

As can readily be seen, the group is in the foreground of "The Swan Hotel", Ridgway, which was "Winter Headquarters". At this period, part of the premises was fitted up as a Skittle Alley of the old type; nine pins and bowls of heavy wood, flat and rounded in the form of old Dutch cheeses. Along the sides of the narrow alley, plain wooden seating provided the dressing facilities. At the end it opened out to a small room where the pins were set up. This provided further accommodation which on busy days would overflow into the adjoining Saloon bar (closed of course for normal business).

A sash-corded window looked out onto a small open yard beyond which was a small wash house or old coach house. Here the Potman would set up a couple of zinc hip baths topped up with a few pints of hot water. So it was a case of up with the bottom sash, hop out and dash across for the after-run or walk wash down. Needless perhaps to mention that on a busy or wet afternoon the density of the water rose and the temperature fell at depressingly rapid rates.

My most lasting impression of this era was a 10 mile, time started handicap walk. As a comparative new member and novice, and favoured with a generous allowance, I entered the last 3 mile lap with only the fear of the unknown pack of fast backmarkers after skin, and won, literally by a "street and a half". With unchecked haste home, I was through the window and (with the absence of runners on an away fixture) into the luxury of near constant hot water. This I thought was sufficient reward. I was however to receive the unusual Club pot, a small hot-water jug E.P.N.S. - Happy days.

It may be wondered "Why did we leave this home with the "Beer and Skittles?" Why? That would be another story. A moving story,very.

O'HARA Gets His REVENGE!

Club 10 miles Championship - Wimbledon, January 21st

It was as though Laurie O'Hara had had this race on his mind for 12 months. Laurie was a deserved winner and a worthy champion and both runners should be congratulated for making this contest so exciting on two consecutive occasions.

GREAT WIN BY GERRY ! ..... but BELS fail to topple POMPEY

SOUTHERN CHAMPIONSHIPS, Parliament Hill Fields, February 11th.

"His best ever", "outstanding", "scintillating", "devastating", "a great champion". These were just a few of the superlatives heard at Parliament Hill when Gerry North won his first Southern Counties C.C.Championship by a margin that would have made an hour-glass more appropriate than a stop-watch! Surging through the quagmires and gliding over the firm stretches this veritable power house of a runner made the opposition look mediocre. So impressive did he look that even when Johnston was at his shoulder half way round the first lap one had no doubt that their company couldn't last. And so it proved to be. In the space of a mile a gap of 50 yards appeared and despite a fall on the second lap Gerry increased this lead to 200 yards. A final circuit of undiminished power doubled the gap and North crossed the line to the applause of a fair sized crowd, who then settled down to await the arrival of the competitors who had been left struggling in the wake of the new Champion.

Belgrave got off to a fine start when the large field raced up the first hill. Bill Dance was first to hit the top, with Gerry and Chris Steer close at hand; Bill and Ghris were to pay for their fast beginning, but we still had 8 in 25 and things looked well.

The field soon sorted itself out and it was clear that our hopes depended on forward packing to offset the superior individual performances of the leading Portsmouth men. At 2 miles Thresher was 13th, with O'Hara, Williams, Hart and Bicourt all in the twenties. Fairclough was 35th, not at all happy, but John Dear who ran only because of Alan Black's last minute withdrawal, was running the race of his life in 59th spot. Bill Dance had not yet recovered from his fast start and was already in the sights of Bill Kerr in the eighties. Brian Buxton looked quite distressed and it was unfortunate that our "twelve cup" chances should disappear with his withdrawal early in the second lap.

With only 15 points separating the Portsmouth and Belgrave teams, the battle really hotted up on the second circuit but our rivals got the better of the exchanges - mainly due to that fine team runner, Martin Hyman. Continuously making ground, he rallied his last three scorers, particularly J. Cooke and Plumpton who were not looking too good, and promptly doubled his team's lead.

So, although we went into the last lap with eleven runners in seventy-six, team chances looked bleak. Thresher suffered with a bout of stitch on the final circuit and conceded ten places, but with O'Hara and Fairclough producing strong finishes the loss was offset. With 2 miles to go Tony was only our 7th man, running along side Pompey's 6th man, Plumpton, but at the finish Fairclough had shot through to 4th scorer (25th) and the Portsmouth man faded to 44th - but even this could not shake Portsmouth's command of the situation and they ran out deserved team winners, with their reputation for being a formidible team with six to score considerably enhanced.

FOOTNOTE: Gerry North's penalty for being such a good winner was to have his Great Britain tracksuit top stolen and then, after the presentation, to find his car and £200 worth of sports goods also stolen.

DAVE ANSELL WRITES:

DAVE ANSELL

There are always some athletes who continually complain about lack of facilities and to them I would tell this story:-

Snowy Brooks - one of our first class track and field men - wrote for a training schedule a couple of months ago and it was duly worked out for him.

As he is serving in the Royal Navy, on an aircraft carrier, certain alterations had to be made from a normal routine. Firstly he could only train in the open air when there was no flying as I suppose he didn't fancy being sucked into a jet engine or chopped in half by an aircraft's wing during his warm-up jog. Then again he couldn't train on the flight deck in very bad weather (participants in the Boxing Day 2 Miles handicap will understand best what this must be like, only this time he keeps still and the "ground" moves). Jumping was also dodgy, for if you jump up when the boat goes down then a simple hop could send you soaring up in the air and when you come down - Ouch! Any running he could manage would have to be at irregular times and spikes, of course, were out, so all his sprinting has to take place on solid steel in plimsoles - not to be recommended.

I thought his weight training would prove to be the only part of his schedule which would follow a normal course, as there is a fully equipped gym below decks. I had made allowances and adaptions for all of these drawbacks except for one thing - the consequence of my ignorance was a letter from him thanking me for the training plans but he was going to have to leave out the various jumping and jump squats in the gym, as he would hit his head on the low ceiling if he attempted them!

Well I don't know how many of you have as much trouble in trying to carry out your training but I can tell you that Snowy has shown the true Belgrave spirit and is somehow training every day and is now in Australia where he hopes to compete in some local meetings. I am sure that you will join me in wishing him Good Luck with his athletics, and if his performances compare with the way he has overcome the lack of facilities for training which he has experienced then I am sure that he will be carrying Belgrave colours to victory on many occasions.

* * * * *

I shouldn't think that anybody has every training facility on their doorstep so we must make our training schedules fit in with what is available. We in Belgrave Harriers have an immediate advantage over other clubs because of the existence of Belgrave Hall. This is ideal for the sprinters and field events men to train from in the winter and we meet there on Saturdays at 2.30 p.m. and of course we are also at Battersea Park track on Sunday mornings at 10.30 a.m. There are usually four coaches passing on their personal experience and know-how to the wise members who take advantage of all these facilities.

We have an exciting season ahead of us and everybody will have to do their best if we are to fulfill the President's desire for a triumph in a Trophy match. If you put in the groundwork then you must surely get the results.

Good Luck for the coming season. DAVE ANSELL.

Postbag: Dear Mr. Editor,

W.H. HARE

The original photo of that reproduced on page 35 of the December 'Belgravian' was taken on Easter Saturday, 10th April, 1925, previous to the start of the third contest for the Club's 20 mile Championship.

The complete and corrected names should read:-

Back row - J.N. THOMAS, R.E. WILLS, C.A. WATTAM, W.H. HARE, J. RATHBONE, J. MILES, E.L. REEVE, W.J. HAWKER, T. EATON. Front row - F. RICKARDS, E.A. WALL, R.W. RICKETTS, E.G. COOPER, R.D. McMULLEN. (A.P. Spendlove and J.K. Chalk were not in attendance).

F. Rickards, holding the R.W. Ricketts Club Trophy, retained this, with E.G. Cooper second and E.A. Wall third.

R.W. Ricketts (President) displays the R.W.A. 20 Mile National Championship Shield, won by the Club in 1924 for the first time, at Windsor.

Bob was to retain this trophy for the Club, in a unique way, in the next race held at Derby on May 8th. Walking as a member of our 'B' team, he finished in front of the fourth scoring member of the Surrey Walking Club and thereby gave our 'A' team the vital one point by which our victory was won.

The upper photo was, in fact, the start of the fourth Club 10 Mile C.C. Championship for the 'Macher Shield', held on 27th March 1926.

Of the five unidentified runners, M. Hasler is on the inside of H.E. Footer, and C.J. Davis on the inside of J.G. Flatt. The runner in the background is probably L. Hartland, who finished fourth. Of the remaining two, one is O.L. Borrett.

The report of the South of Thames Junior, of November last, reads "It would be interesting to know....". The short answer would be the 9th February 1929, when the Club won the Championship for the first time, at Gatton Park, Merstham.

Dear Mr.Editor,

A.A.HARLEY

May I congratulate you on an excellent December issue of the Belgravian? And may the many future successes in store jostle and fight for inclusion in the pages to come!

On page 40, in your description of the South of Thames Junior event you point out that our Bill Lucas as this years Association President, had felicity of handing five awards to Belgrave runners and ask rhetorically if any other President of the S.T.C.C.A. has had similar pleasure in the past.

Yes, indeed! In 1929 Harry Hare, the first Belgrave President ever of the S.T.C.C.A., handed to his club mates the DEWAR Shield for winning the Championship (for the first time), and the six team winners medals to:- BILL MITCHELL (4), ARTHUR PENNY (7), ARTHUR ALLUM (12), BILL WEBB (16), PAUL KHLOPIN (23) and ALEC ROBERTSHAW (26). In addition Belgrave also won the COLMAN Cup as the first club to finish its complete team of twelve men, and great was the satisfaction when Harry called forward STANLEY DRAKE, our twelfth scoring man, to receive this trophy - also for the first time.

Yours Sincerely, A.A.HARLEY

P.S. Further research would also indicate that on November 7th 1928 at Epsom Downs, Harry Hare also had the pleasure (in advance of the above mentioned triumphs) of presenting winning medals to another Belgrave team in the Junior race, with two place medals in addition. The team was:- BILL MITCHELL (2), ARTHUR PENNY (3), ARTHUR ALLUM (4), BILL RICE (13). Actual winner of this event was LES COHEN , then running in Ashcombe colours and the writer was 97th! A.A.H.

SOCIAL - December 31st CARNIVAL NIGHT

New Year's Eve at Belgrave Hall saw one of our best ever social nights. We had a new group - Tim Healy's Jazz Band - lined up for the evening's entertainment and they proved to be so popular, joining in with the spirit of things and at one time even marching round the hall playing their instruments for all they were worth, that you can rest assured we will see them again.

The fancy dress competition was disappointing, only a dozen or so making the effort. Those who did bother, though, had really gone to town.- Charlie Manning won the men's competition as an Egyptian mummy (Tutan Khamen/Tooting Common!) whilst his wife, Jean, took the ladies prize in a topical prisoners garb with the caption "It's in to be out". Bill and June Laws made it another family double by being voted the best "group" in some fabulous Elizabethan gear.

Tim Healy was by now obviously feeling the heat and stripped to the waist, wearing a pith helmet, he led his band into the 'Hokey Cokey' with a very merry Gerry North dancing like a dervish in the centre and looking suspiciously as if he was trying to 'nobble' Bob Holt of Hercules (the County Champs were the next week-end). 'Auld Lang Syne' took about twenty minutes with each member of the band taking a solo, and the night finished gloriously on a patriotic note as all Belgravians joined Tim in a doubtful rendering of 'God Save The Queen'. Great stuff!

WALKING

It has become increasingly difficult of late to obtain any results, news or information relating to Walking matters. Your Editors are well used to prodding, persuading, cajoling, pleading and even begging for material from some contributors to your Gazette and what success we have, coupled with our own personal knowledge of track, field, cross-country and road-relays, enables a fairly thorough coverage to be made.

Unfortunately it is not possible for us to attend walking fixtures as well, so if the bare result even is unobtainable then we can publish nothing. Such is the case for much that has happened since our last issue.

For many years the walking section kept Belgrave's name to the forefront when their fellow-Belgravians were struggling thro' a lean spell. Now the wheel has turned half circle and, relatively, the roles are reversed---yet Belgrave's walkers are still amongst the most feared and respected of teams and are still enjoying some success. Those halcyon days of the late fifties and early sixties should not be forgotten. Members should be continually reminded of their rightful place in the Walking World--- i.e. the TOP. Space is also needed to record our current successes and to give encouragement to those promising walkers who are only too eager to get amongst the medals.

Our General Honorary Secretary has said on many occasions that we should do our utmost to avoid becoming too sectionalised; that members should feel they are party to Club activities in all spheres; that we are first and foremost Belgravians all, that this is best fostered through our social functions and the medium of this Gazette.

Your Editors are not happy with the coverage our walkers are getting and therefore appeal for someone to come forward and provide us with the news we require. He will not be obliged to write reports but, merely, every few weeks or so provide us with results and, if he prefers, only report verbally on the latest happenings in the Walking World. Articles, reminiscences and tit-bits of information are still welcome from any others but, most importanty we need that "Walking" reporter. Any offers?

* * * * *

The tragic loss of Cyril Yescombe is a blow to us all. It is men like Cyril that carry a club through it's lean years, and his sense of humour and loyalty to Belgrave will be sadly missed. His concern in the training of Police cadets played a large part in the latter years of his life and one felt sure that on Belgrave's behalf he had one eye open for possoble walking potential.

With the loss of Cyril fresh in our minds, and only six days gone, we suffered another loss with the passing away of Harry Evans, our most revered Past-President. No words need be added to our coverage in earlier pages except perhaps, those few that make up that brief, but fitting, epilogue of Edward Nazen Parker:

"Life's race well run, Life's work well done, Life's victory won, Now cameth rest".

* * * * *

Eric Hall, Ray Hall and Ray Middleton have all had bouts of sickness since Christmas but are now recovered and 'rarin' to go for the R.W.A. and Inter-Counties '10' on March 18th. Eric hopes to have a pre-race work-out in the "Ipswich" the previous week. Well settled down South again, he is now training regularly with Ray Hall. They manage to get to Belgrave Hall on some training nights, but the real regular there these days is Pat Duncan who is in attendance every Tuesday and Thursday. How about getting a real "Duncan" pack out those evenings?

Bob Barnes is walking very well these days and could surprise in the National '10' With Gerry Cameron also walking into form the teams could well click at the right time.

We do not see a great deal of Doug Fotheringham and Bert Stevenson these days. Both Inspectors, Police duties seem to have restricted their appearances. Dave King has faded into obscurity, temporarily we hope, but living as far distant as Hull must make it difficult for him. Unfortunately, no new members have yet appeared that can fill these gaps but if Messrs. Hall (E), Hall (R), Middleton, Barnes, Cameron, Duncan and Farley can "bring home the bacon" then our problems will look after themselves.

NORTH SURREY LEAGUE

Belgrave retained their League Title by what at first appears to be a comfortable margin; but with one race to go they were placed only 3rd! Last year we went into the last fixture with a lead of 111 pts and increased this to 380 pts. This year we changed a 101 pts deficit into a winning 174 pts margin, thanks to a repeat of last year's excellent turn-out.

Once again, well over twenty different runners scored for us in the four matches and our depth should carry us through to a succession of titles in the coming years. None of our top runners was able to take part in all four fixtures so the individual award went to Fred Stebbings of Croydon.

The first race held at Wimbledon was reported in our last issue. The remaining three fixtures went as follows:-

RACE NO. 2 - WALTON (ESHER), DECEMBER 10th.

Belgrave went into the second match of the series no less than 200 points down on Hercules and 100 odd on Mitcham and Surrey. This had been caused by the first race clashing with the Bristol/Weston Relay where we had sent our strongest eight. Now the battle was on to put things right - but where had all our supporting runners gone? Whilst Gerry North, Williams, Thresher, Fairclough and Bicourt produced great efforts to spearhead our team, the burden of support fell upon runners who would not normally have filled such a role. The missing men (and their were nine or ten who could have occupied the space between 12th and 37th) ensured that Surrey A.C. and Mitcham A.C. remained ahead of us in the overall table.

The Course was at it's muddiest best and, after a comfortable first lap, Gerry North moved well away over the last circuit. Feature of the race was the running of that other Gerry - Gerry Williams. Revelling in the conditions he finished ahead of those seasoned campaigners Thresher and Fairclough; there will be many dust-ups (or mud-ups!) to come.

RACE NO. 3 - MITCHAM (MORDEN PARK), DECEMBER 31st.

The fight-back continued over the fast grassland course of Morden Park but just to make it more difficult for us, seven of our scoring ten at Walton did not appear, but were fortunately replaced by another seven who found themselves in a similar boat - 7 in 25 and then a long wait to close in. Perhaps the shift system has arrived to stay!

Thresher tuned himself nicely for the county event the following week and O'Hara showed his liking for a fast 5 miles course. Bill Dance produced an excellent run, but broke down with achilles trouble next day so will have his work cut out to regain this form in time for the "Southern".

Our final score was sufficient to push Hercules further behind us but, with one match to come, Surrey and Mitcham are 105pts and 2pts respectively ahead of us. The final race is a week before the "National" so many runners will probably miss out. The League can be won though if all those runners on the fringe of the National team turn out at Lloyd Park.

FINAL RACE - CROYDON H.(LLOYD PARK), FEBRUARY 25th

With seven of our National '9' turning out, and no less than a further 20 providing support, Belgrave swept to a match win and an overall league win in devastating fashion. Any hopes Surrey AC had of clinging to their 100 pts lead were soon dashed as the claret and gold phalanx swept to the front providing, at one stage, ten in twenty. It was great to see such team spirit - "National" next week or not, we were going to retain our title and to indicate our confidence our captain did not even bring the trophy along! Gerry North was out for nothing more than a training run but, just to shake up the leading group during the race he put in a half-mile spurt to open up a lead of 100 yards and then eased up to await training companion Bob Holt of Hercules, whereupon they crossed the line together.

"I Was There" NO.2

Ernie Duffett

NO.2 - by Ernie Duffett.

When your Editors asked me to write the second of these mementous occasions, my mind fairly 'boggled' at the thought. After serving for 25 years as Running Hon. Sec. of the 'Bels' I was present at so many of our most famous victories.

After consideration I selected our 'National' win at Sheffield in March, 1948, when we regained the title from the holders, Sutton Harriers.

We knew we had more strength in depth than our rivals, and so it proved after a most exciting race both for the individual and team prizes.

It was a fine afternoon in Graves Park and many thousands of people who came to see some great racing, paid over £500 for admission.

Sydney Wooderson, the one-time holder of the 880 yards and 1 mile world records, was a firm favourite, but before he eventually won his first 'National' title over the country, he had to contend with one of the severest races of his career.

His challenger was none other than our own Vic Blowfield who was always up with the leaders, and when they came round for the last of the three large laps, Blowfield and Wooderson were together with Shorrocks of Hailesowen some 25 yards behind in 3rd place.

With barely half a mile to go, Blowfield went away from his rival and Wooderson was looking very tired, and Vic opened a lead of about 25 yards with only 400 yards of the undulating course to be covered.

Then the large crowd broke onto the course in their excitement, and Blowfield literally had to fight his way through them. In the 'rugger' scrum Wooderson came again and managed to pass Vic and being the faster finisher he came home just four seconds in front of Blowfield.

If the course could have been kept clear it is uncertain whether our man would have been the winner, as Wooderson naturally had the greater speed on the flat.

Then came the fight for the team title, and with Len Herbert running one of his finest races to finish in 12th team position, and well backed up by Ted Stimpson in 15th place, we had three men home to Sutton's two. Then they had four home to our three, but Frank Abberton scored 27th to make it four each. But Sutton still had five in before our 5th man (Tom Carter 41st) and then praise be, Tommy Clinton came along in 57th place to complete our scoring six, while Sutton's 6th man finished 65th!

We thus won the title with a total of 154 points to Sutton's 174, and Blackheath Harriers were 3rd with 183 points.

Our other men to finish, with nine to compete and six to score, were Les Stokell 64th, Albert Frost 95th, and finally Dick Tobin 132nd.

It is interesting to note that Denis Brickwood finished in 80th place, and was then running for Mitcham A.C., and it is perhaps sad to reflect that of our team members, only Tom Carter and Ted Stimpson, are still active within the Club in an official capacity.

International Cross Country Race HANNUT, BELGIUM

Olympian

February 5th 1967

We were delighted to receive an invitation to compete in the club team race associated with the international event at Hannut for this is one of the plum Continental races and attracts representation from Belgium, France, Germany and Holland in addition to England and Scotland. This was our first foreign cross-country race since pre-war days and the boys rose to the occasion to make sure that thirty years would not go by before the next invitation arrives. In the face of strong opposition and scoring five aside, we won by six points to take the beautiful trophy presented by the Belgian newspaper Le Soir.

The weekend began with five runners meeting at Wimbledon and four runners and the team manager assembling at London Bridge to converge by road on Dover for a night sea crossing to Ostend. The two cars disembarked at 5.30 am. on Saturday morning and after a quick run on the motorway we arrived in Brussels just as the restaurants were opening and were able to obtain a first class breakfast to set us up for a sight seeing tour of the Belgian capital. The centre of attraction was a shoe shop where most of the team equipped themselves with a continental version of the Chelsea boot in first class (?) suede which no doubt will be proudly displayed en masse at the next Club Social.

At midday we set off again for the last fifty miles drive to Hannut which was reached at about 1pm and after reporting to the headquarters (a cafe in the centre of the town) of the Hannut Football Club, who are the organisers of this cross country event, we were taken for a steak lunch with wine and all the trimmings. Thoroughly satisfied with our reception we then strolled off to look at the course for the following day's race before again assembling to be allocated our "digs" for the weekend. Everyone was most comfortably housed with local residents and there is no doubt that we received very hospitable treatment.

Saturday evening was spent quietly over a drink or two at the cafe of Roger Gustin the organising secretary and again the kindness and hospitality of the local people was much in evidence, for most of the refreshments consumed, whether coffee or otherwise, were "on the house". As we had had only limited sleep the previous night we were all ready for bed and an early retirement was the order of the day.

A briefing for team managers was called for at 10.30 on Sunday morning for team declarations and distribution of numbers. This was followed by a further inspection and a gentle run over the course by all but the team manager who preferred to view rather than sample. The general consensus of opinion was that the race would be fast for although there was a generous helping of plough the weather had been dry and this would soon bed down to a firm surface.

By midday the town of Hannut was a mass of people attracted to the afternoons programme of racing which comprised seven races in all for various ages and even for the local tennis players, culminating in the international event at 4.15pm. The number of spectators was estimated at 10,000, swelled no doubt by the fact that this was the 25th promotion and was being honoured by the presence of His Majesty King Baudouin, obviously a popular man with his people.

Continental races are generally on short lap courses and this was no exception for the full distance of the big race, which was a little under six miles, was made up of two short laps and six large laps so the spectators who were mainly assembled in the football stadium, where the start and finish were sited, were able to enjoy the racing to the full.

The number of starters in 'our' race was about sixty, made up of international teams from the leading cross-country nations of Europe plus club teams from France, Holland, Germany and Belgium in addition to ourselves. The international race scored three a side and the club race five a side.

Marcel Vandewattyne, one of Belgium's best ever athletes was the starter and in the field was Gaston Roelants, Belgium's current top runner and present Olympic 3000 metres Steeplechase Champion who was the favourite and himself anxious to win to establish a record of four wins in a row and five wins in all to break the existing record which he held jointly with Vandewattyne. It is interesting to record at this point that the latter was in the first five in this race for every year between 1948 and 1963, an extremely good run of performances.

As expected the pace in the early stages of the race was very fast, dictated by Roelants and Scotland's Ian McCafferty who only the previous evening had taken from Roelants his indoor 2 miles European record with a great run at Cosford. Our own boys were not too prominent early on but it was interesting to see John Bicourt just ahead of Gerry North after the first half mile when they were ninth and tenth respectively. Gerry battled on gallantly throughout the whole race and although he never got to grips with the two leaders he slowly worked his way past Jim Alder, Bruce Tulloh, Tim Johnston, Lachie Stewart and the strong foreign opposition to come home a comfortable third which achieved for him the honour of being presented to King Baudouin who showed considerable interest in our own captain.

At the half-way stage McCafferty raised a cheer from the British supporters by taking a thirty yard lead over Roelants, who at this stage appeared completely shattered, but two laps later a super human effort took him again to the front and he stayed ahead to run in a popular winner with the crowd by a five second margin.

At two miles our runners had established themselves in or about the positions they were to finally occupy and little or no change took place for the remainder of the race. The scoring members other than Gerry who was well ahead were all in the twenties and although this may not appear high in so small a field it should be remembered that the majority of the first twenty-five are established internationals.

It was difficult to diagnose the progress of the race from the team scoring angle in view of the wide diversity of vests but it was pleasing to see the claret and gold standing out well and the Belgian crowd obviously appreciated the forward running of our team. They seemed to be very discerning in the arts of cross-country running.

Although we thought that five in twenty-eight was good enough we had to wait for some time before the final result was announced and we were proved to be the victors. In the international race Belgium, led by Roelants were at their best and were easy winners over Scotland and England.

After the race all the runners assembled at the Town Hall to collect their prizes - everyone in the race gets one. The standard of prizes was very high and they were awarded by choice according to the order of finishing. All our runners were extremely pleased.

On the Sunday evening a banquet with plentiful food and drink was held with the usual speeches and our members came into contact with the local "talent" for the first time. It seemed that most were well satisfied - it is thought that Thresher and Williams will almost certainly wish to return - we all hope the telephone numbers they collected are not changed by 1968.

After the banquet some of the ladies were persuaded to join our party at the local discotheque where the frivolities were continued until 3am. on Monday morning.

A quiet but by no means dispirited team assembled at 8am after only a few hours sleep for the drive back to Ostend for the boat home. Wimbledon was reached late on Monday afternoon with sufficient energy for most to partake of a training session.

On the whole a most satisfactory and successful weekend. Belgrave's name is now established in Belgium and we have made many good friends and created much good will. It is hoped we can return in 1968 but there is a possibility that this race may be discontinued. Perhaps we will get an invitation from another town. If we win the National we are assured of other visits to Europe.

MINOR FIXTURES.

With so many "major" fixtures in the Winter Calendar these days, and with the North Surrey League occupying a large part of the remaining space, the inter-club mob match is fast fading from its prominence of yesteryear. Apart from SLH, Blackheath and Ranelagh, who have a long tradition of mob match "feuds" and turn out every available able-bodied man, most clubs now use these races as training or social runs. Consequently the home team is always much the stronger. With only three such fixtures in our Winter list many Belgravians chose these occasions to train in the morning and have a "holiday" in the afternoon. Some observers may question whether these races are worth while, but it should be remembered that we have a large number of runners who never make the major races and it is for their sakes that these meetings are arranged.

Our first such match was at Hillingdon's HQ, Ruislip on Dec.17th, and although we had five of the first six home we were beaten into third place by the home club and Shaftesbury in a close result, scoring 8 a side. COSTESSEY, NORWICH - March 4th

BELGRAVE WIN SOUTHAMPTON/BOURNEMOUTH RELAY.

Running in atrocious conditions, of driving rain and sleet, our 'A' team ran out good winners in a time only 48 secs outside their own course record. Our 'B' team also did magnificently to finish sixth.

After being tripped up in the early stages, Tony Fairclough finished sixth on the first stage, 28 secs down on Heaver of Blackheath. Trevor Hart knocked 17 secs off Pettit's lap record for the next stage to move us up to 2nd and a back-in-form Gerry North followed suit by lopping 24 secs off Ellis's record to put us way out in front. Kerr had no difficulty in holding the lead with a very good time, but the sensation on this stage was the running of Chris Steer who absolutely hammered the opposition to move our 'B' team from 16th to 8th with a time only one second slower than Bill's.

Laurie O'Hara had a shock on the penultimate leg when his minute lead was wiped out by new International Bob Holt who sped tp a new lap record, 13 seconds better than Gerry's existing mark. Gerry Williams had no difficulty in restoring our position and ran in a comfortable winner. John Bicourt produced a fine stint for the 'B' team to bring them home sixth and was helped in his effort by his determination to beat the time of his 'A' team counterpart----that's the stuff!
