Editorial · page 3

"The team that trains together stays together": a statement that contains more than a little truth. Our walkers are small in numbers but they have a closely knit fraternity that goes hand in hand with success. Our runners, meanwhile, have not been successful since the 60's and although laudable attempts have been made to foster the pack training system, with due respect, it is not the 'A' team runners who have responded. Track and field tends to be even more individualistic and true to form, our track men rarely even see each other apart from match days; the social event of the year is a quick drink together after our single home league fixture. Not surprising then that when the result sheets are handed out Belgrave Harriers features a little lower down the list than we would like.

We do have a problem in that our membership is drawn from a large catchment area and this is further aggravated by our head-quarters being ten miles from our track base but more could be done to engender a sense of togetherness or team spirit, call it what you will.

Linda Nicholls writes in this issue of her stay with a Swedish club which regularly goes out into the countryside to camp and train together. Arnold Bentt, in "Spotlight", harks back to the days when we had a group of successful jumpers all working at their events together. The lesson seems obvious.

At present we have a fine bunch of young athletes who should go far if we can only keep them together. So let's liven things up a bit, plan a few training trips, and what about an athletic holiday on the Continent next year. Or are we all too old and staid and unable to climb out of the rut?

Linda NichollsArnold Bentt

grapevine

We are sorry to report that Gordon Doubleday, a key member of the "Belgravian" team, has had a spell in Kingston Hospital thanks to a recurrence of the heart infection that laid him low in 1969. Apparently the trouble was caused by some decayed fragments of teeth left behind after extractions. Now on the mend and at home again, he will have to take it easy for a while but we look forward to seeing his familiar figure, stopwatch in hand, on the finish lines this summer. One thing that cheered him enormously during his incarceration was the sight on TV of Ernest Obeng bearing a Belgrave Club Honours Badge to the 60 metre finish line at Cosford ahead of Allan Wells.

Our already busy General Hon. Secretary Derek Crookes was elected Secretary of the South of the Thames Cross Country Association at their AGM in October. He is obviously doing so well on the Surrey County Committee that his fame is spreading.

An interested spectator at the Autumnal '5¾' was Charlie Smart and it was good to see him chatting away with old friends. Happily he still has some mobility and the fighting spirit which used to strike terror into our old rivals on the Brighton Relay is still evident by the way he tackled the stairs for welcome refreshment at Belgrave Hall. He was the darling of the crowds at the White City - they seemed to think that the balding Belgravian who was carving his way through the field was old enough to be the father of the others! Let's hope we see more of you, Charlie.

In the 1978 Belgrave Draw, organised by Ron Langheim, there was a healthy profit of £275.18 for the Club coffers - £100 up on last year. Thanks and well done Ron!

We hear that Bernard Eglington pulled off the biggest fiddle of his athletic career in October. He has been walking second-claim with Lancs. W.C. for about seven years and had twice won their "Alison Trophy" for a 10 kilometre handicap. When he turned up to compete on October 14th he was astounded to find only officials and himself there. Someone had blundered and no other walkers had turned up. Although he was all for postponing the race the officials insisted that he should set out on his own - which he did - to win the cup for the third time.

One way of getting your name into the Club's minutes is to change your address and one change recently notified was Dick Towndrow who has moved from one address in Mere, Wiltshire, to another in that delightful village. Dick was a member of the old Ashcombe AC, and when they disbanded (they used to run in Richmond Park from Sheen) he came to us via the Poly. During Army service in the war he was stationed at Gillingham, Dorset, and married a local girl. Why mention this? - Some years later a lad from Gillingham came to London to seek fame and fortune and, amongst other things, joined Belgrave - his name - Carl Lawton. Dick was a fine cross-country and road runner and on one occasion won the now discontinued Wigmore '15'. Some years ago he left London to take up a teaching job in Wiltshire.

The Southern Counties Cross Country Association have taken advantage of the tendencies of that compulsive committee member Derek Crookes, our General Hon. Secretary now being elected to that august body's committee.

Good news for speedy tipplers! Haigs, distillers of fine whisky and sponsors of our Belgrave Road Relay (April 7th), have upped the size of the team prizes. In the past the first team has received a 1 gallon bottle of the 'hard stuff', second team half a gallon and third team one quarter of a gallon. This has done nothing to slow down clubs like Aldershot & Farnham and Thames Valley H, so let's see what the new measures can do - first team to receive one whole bottle each, second team half a bottle each and third team quarter of a bottle each.

Congratulations to Doug. Fotheringham on being elected President of the Southern Area of the Race Walking Association. He has been walking for a quarter of a century and was a regular in our "Garnet" Cup teams until he scored in our winning team in 1977 to gain "senior" status. One of his greatest performances was in the 100 miles race promoted by the M.P.A.A. at Chigwell in 1965 when he covered the distance in the fine time of 21hrs. 31mins. 9secs. to become Centurion No. 373. More recently he has been Race Hon. Sec. for the popular M.P.A.A. Open 11 kilos. (formerly 7 miles) held from Imber Court.

John Thresher has joined the ranks of the marathoners. Now 39 years old he lined up in a field of 205 at Regina, Saskatchewan, and finished 5th in 2hrs. 39mins. 25secs. Great stuff John! As a vet in 1980 he says he will be looking for Gorman and trying to avoid O'Hara. John has now moved to Winnipeg and we wish him, Mavis and their children the best of luck in their new home. If anyone wishes to write to John his new address is [address withheld]. Incidentally, John is still our Club Record holder for 1,500 metres and 1 mile with 3:44.6 and 4:02.1, dating from 1968 and 1964 respectively.

We note that John Blair-Fish, now representing Edinburgh, was 37th in the Barnsley Marathon in 2hrs. 40mins. 22secs. There were 282 finishers.

Poor old Charlie Speechley! At 91 years of age he was competing in the Highgate One Hour Walks, we hear, when someone had the nerve to disqualify him. He was most disappointed and is now said to be thinking of changing his style.

Belated congratulations to two Belgravians who have taken the marital plunge. Howard Satchell (can he really be 20 years a member) has married Chris from Canada, and Arnold Bentt has married Pat. Best wishes to them all.

In November Derek Crookes did not get voted onto the committee of any athletic organisation. When asked by our correspondent what she thought about Derek's "failure", Mrs. Mary Crookes replied, "Who is Derek Crookes?"

It was good to see Len Marchant at the A.G.M. A Club stalwart of the 30's and 40's and a great middle-distance runner who has represented the A.A.A. (regarded by many as the stepping stone to an International Vest). He would probably have been at his best during World War II, when he served in the R.A.F. and picked us up many members. He was a good cross-country and road relay runner and on one occasion was second in the South of the Thames Junior - the winner being Bill Lucas.

An absentee from the A.G.M. was Frank Webb who has recently undergone an operation. However, you can't keep chaps like Frank away for long and it was good to see him at the '5¾' run.

The Chief Judge at the Chippenham to Calne Walk was George Bentley now living at Portishead, Avon. He looked fit and well and told us that his brother Fred, one of our great long-distance walkers of the 30's and an international in 1938, was also fit and well. Also at the race was Percy Wright who is busy planning the English Schools Road Walking Championships due to be held in Southampton. We hope that many of our younger walkers will be there competing for their counties.

A steering committee was formed in January by the London Borough of Merton to discuss the setting up of a Merton Sports Council. It is not yet resolved whether "steering committees" are allowed to count towards a record attempt but if so, Derek Crookes now needs only one more committee for a World Record and his own niche in the Guiness Book of Records!

Fame at last. Peter Hardwick and Alan Painter were featured on the front page of "Athletics Weekly" for March 10th. Well, perhaps featured is overdoing it a bit, just their rear views being shown along with many other rear views tackling a hill in the 1978 National.

One sees some funny snippets of information around. According to the financial columns "for the six months to July 31st the turnover of Belgrave (Blackheath) turned in at £1.39 million ..... At Last We Win

DECEMBER 9th 1978 - EPSOM DOWNS - SURREY C.C. LEAGUE RACE 3.

At Last We Win

TAPE MEASURES RULE!

TAPE MEASURES RULE! (from "Sport In The USSR)

In recent years science has provided sportsmen and trainers with a huge variety of technical gadgetry to help them hone their skills by flawlessly registering times. Competing with the noble tape measure are the latest technical devices, which make it possible to register information with degrees of precision which at the present are not even all that necessary. The tape measure is not always laid and stretched evenly, and is affected by temperature. Of course, its influence is not great enough to really affect the measuring of javelin or discus throws, but it does exist. Optical devices measure this distance with mathematical precision.

This type of equipment was first used at the Olympic Games in Munich. The unusual-looking device was reminiscent of a movie camera sitting on a tripod. The official went over to the javelin, which had left a mark in the grass, stuck a reflector into the ground, and the scoreboard would light up, indicating the result. The work here is being done by a laser. An electronic optical tachometer makes it possible to measure simultaneously horizontal distance, curves and a host of other parameters.

Of course no one forbids the use of optic laser devices at sports competitions. Curiously enough, however, they can be utilised only until a world record is set. In this case something rather comical takes place: the judges run for the tape measure and "check" the result measured by a laser device. As of now, the rules prohibit the registering of world records with anything other than a tape measure

HARRY HATHWAY

Cecil Gittins

HARRY HATHWAY

A few weeks ago, as I understand it, Harry was (as so very often) helping out at an athletic event, and he felt not too good. Well, the weather had not been kind and Harry was in his seventies. When he was at home that evening he collapsed and when medical help arrived he was dead.

Harry had been the country's top timekeeper for a long, long time - twenty years to my recollection. It was he who developed, organised and arranged proper testing for timekeepers - and those who have read his articles will appreciate the immaculate detail and assembly of factual data which backed up his plans. As an unsure novice timekeeper myself, he was one of those who gave me instruction and encouragement and for that I am grateful. Harry was always firmly and quietly confident in himself but never bombastic; indeed he was always willing to listen to what someone else had to say.

Harry was always around with his multitude of watches at the top internationals but he never neglected the "small" meetings which are the basis of our sport. He was to be seen at Belgrave events often.

He was a retired mathematics teacher and a bachelor, as well as a Roman Catholic of deep conviction. The timekeepers' stand will not be quite the same without Harry.

Cecil Gittins

from the Belgravian ... ... 25 years ago

The 5¾ mile road race ..... 27th March 1954 ..... Over 40 runners turned out and there was a surprise winner in Ron Linstead. His time of 28mins. 25secs. was a new course record and once his National Service training is finished he will prove a handful to the best in the country. Charlie Smart who is now back in England for good - we hope - finished 2nd, with John Bromley 3rd. (Editor's note: Ron was a Youth when winning this race in 1954).

The National 20 miles Walking Championship ..... May 8th 1954 ..... The Championship was held at Birmingham this year and what a grand day it turned out to be for the Club. Once again we are holders of a National title and it was grand to see in a weekly paper "Belgrave National Champions". Our heartiest congratulations to all, particularly George Checkley (9th) who celebrated his birthday by gaining his honours badge. Bernard Eglington (13th) missed his by one position. George Beecham (17th) gained his first National winning team medal, Hugh McGreechan (4th) the Sidney Bayliss Cup.

... and 40 years ago

The National Cross Country Championship 1939 ..... As we go to press comes wonderful news of the winning of the English Cross Country Championship at Manchester ..... 43 teams lined up for the start. Our lads were reasonably well away and at 1½ miles Stimpson and Penny were near the leaders, with our other runners well placed. At three miles the same state of affairs existed, but the large field passing in a solid mass of mud and humanity, with the rain pouring down, prevented any very definite conclusions being drawn. We knew that both Birchfield and ourselves were doing well..... On the next lap (5.3 miles) rough calculations showed us to be leading by a few points from Birchfield. Our runners were advised of this and seemed to run with more determination than ever. As they came round again (7.6 miles) to start the last lap we still led, but the Birchfield crowd were urging their team to gain positions.

Then to the finish to await the arrival of the runners. First home was Jack Holden, of Tipton H., a worthy winner. Our first man home was Ted Stimpson, in fourth position, scoring 3 for team purposes. He had improved from 14th to 4th position over the last lap of 2.3 miles, passing seasoned runners as though they were not in the race. His effort was an amazing one, and our team had an excellent start. Then came Arthur Penny (5), another to rise to the occasion on an awful day. A few seconds delay and Tom Carter (17) appeared, followed a little later by our Captain, Bert Footer (28), who had gained place after place in the second half of the race.

We now wanted two others to complete our team, and three claret and gold vests came along together. Harry Shields (34) and Dan Patience (35) managed to catch and beat A.W.Jerman on the run-in, and we had won the "National" for the second time!

JACK BIDGOOD M.B.E.

G.S.D.

JACK BIDGOOD M.B.E.

We were sorry to learn that Jack Bidgood died early in December; he must have been well into his seventies. He was born and brought up in Devon and followed the normal sporting life of a Devonian. Later he came to London and joined the Metropolitan Police where he became interested in walking and joined Belgrave. This was in our days of walking supremacy and he figured in many of our teams in National Championships (20 miles and 50 kilos only in those days), the London to Brighton and many provincial walks. As a policeman he was, of course, a regular competitor in the Barking to Southend Walk which he won in 1950. He also won the Club's Long-Distance Championship - held in conjunction with the London to Brighton Walk.

Obviously then a great performer himself, he was always ready to give advice and encouragement to newcomers to the sport. He also found time to help on the administrative side and served a term of office as Chairman of the Southern R.W.A. During this period of office the Southern Committee decided to launch a magazine for walkers only - "Race Walking Record". Remembering that this was in World War Two it was a brave decision. After retiring from the active side he was a Judge for many years.

He had a great career in the Metropolitan Police, finishing with the rank of Chief Superintendent. He was awarded the M.B.E. for meritorious service to the Force.

Volumes could be written about his personality. He did much to foster the idea that the off-duty policeman could be as good a club man as any. He insisted that whilst engaged in sport he was called Jack - and Jack he was to everybody. He was as likeable and as pleasant a fellow as one could wish to meet.

He had suffered indifferent health over the last few years, but his passing came as a surprise. We say farewell to a fine character who not only set an example by his sportsmanship but was a stalwart friend.

G.S.D.

WALKING

Carl Lawton

WALKING

A few seasons ago the walking editorial queried "where have all the walkers gone?". Since that time there has been a promising upturn in our numbers and as a result the allocation of National numbers issued to Belgrave has almost been used up.

Results have, on the whole, been very good since the previous issue of the magazine and turn-outs for races have improved though we seldom manage that magical 100% - a feat we did achieve in the National J/Y/B at Coventry. On studying the results it will be seen that we have not been having to rely on the basic four as some clubs do. In fact in the four "opens": Chippenham, Vauxhall, Enfield and Belgrave, no less than ten permutated to pick up team prizes. In addition to that another eight have appeared in placed teams in recent months. It has on certain occasions given the Secretary problems in deciding who to put into the 'A' team.

Although we did not win a Senior National title last year the Juniors did their bit by winning the 10 kilometres. The second time in three years that we have won it and as a result congratulations go to Richard Dorman, Paul Blagg and Trevor McDermot and commiserations to the four behind them who, had they walked for other clubs, would have been in the scoring teams. In the Youths we did not do so well but it was not for the lack of trying. We could well have placed 3rd had we not had the misfortune of disqualifications but at least we started a team and congratulations to Mike Nicholls for his 3rd place.

We have had our problems with the Girls this year. With our small numbers it does prove difficult to raise teams on every occasion through work and other reasons but when we have succeeded we have usually come away with a prize: first team at Chippenham and 3rd in the National 5 kilometres. The "National" was a very good performance but had we a full and fit team we could have done far better.

It is rather impractical to mention everyone who has done his or her bit for the Club (it might be easier to mention those who have not) but outstanding this year is undoubtedly John Newnham. Although he dabbled about six years ago and reached the echelons of six miles an hour in the Highgate Hour walks, he decided to have another go last year and much to his own and the Club's suprise has turned into one of the regular team men. He has no secret of success - just dedication.

Others worthy of mention are Paul Blagg, Junior International; Richard Dorman, 2nd in the National Junior; Mike Nicholls, 3rd in the National Youths; Graham Seatter and Allan Callow, Commonwealth Games competitors; Derek Howie, Scottish International; Linda Nicholls, 2nd in the W.A.A.A. Championship. We had County Champions in Graham Seatter, John Hall, Carl Lawton, Richard Dorman, Kevin McDermot, Trevor McDermot, Linda and Mike Nicholls. Derek Ellis was the Police Cadet Champion, Shirley Rook the National Police Champion (for running but she walks mostly) and John Dunsford placed 3rd in the European Veterans Championships.

The Annual Belgrave promotion, the 'Open 7 mile walk', had as most of you know a revised course this year. This was to ease the worsening problem of the traffic around the Common. Generally it proved successful and once again thanks are due to those within the Belgrave circle but not actually members: Joan Goswell, Wyn Cook and Jean Middleton, who do such sterling work. We did have a small problem with pointsmen but that should not happen again. Thanks, in no small way, also go to those who, with their generosity, enable the Club to keep up a high standard of prizes. The cost of prizes last year came to nearly £120, with receipts from entries just £60. Thus another £60 had to be raised to cover the cost of the promotion and much of this came from the prize fund and the rest (total outlay was nearly £160) from teas, programmes and the raffle.

Although very little is done within the Club with regard to coaching for walking, it being carried out on an individual basis, it is anticipated to try and hold a training camp over a long weekend. By the time you read this, plans should be well advanced. In addition it is planned to make more of an impact on races which the Club has not competed in for a while. The turn-out for the Leicester Mercury last year was rather poor so that is a good starting point. There is also the Birmingham 35kms plus possibly a couple of Northern races - the Morecambe 20kms and the Sheffield '15' or the Bradford 50kms. With our strength in depth there is no reason why we should not support more races out of London. If we expect other clubs to come to us we should return the compliment. It is a few years since we won the Leicester Mercury on the Saturday and then placed second in the Bradford event two days later with a completely different team. COMMON KNOWLEDGE

COMMON KNOWLEDGE

About halfway between the Kingsmere pond and the Windmill lies the site of an experimental railway track laid down in 1845 to test the invention of a Mr. Prosser. This part of the track was built in a circle but a further straight stretch leading off the circle probably extended well down Windmill Road, along which our Yacht '3' course also travels.

The rails were rectangular in section and made of a chemically hardened wood. The train designed for this track had flangeless wheels but had guiding wheels set at 45° to the horizontal with right angled grooves which engaged the rails. The train was said to be more economic than the "iron railway" due to the lack of friction; the power needed was only about three-quarters of that required for a conventional locomotive.

TRIP TO SWEDEN

Linda Nicholls

Linda Nicholls writes of her

TRIP TO SWEDEN

In September, as a result of an invitation through my family, I travelled to Sweden for a three weeks training holiday. During my stay I trained with the Swedish club Enhorna I.F. They are an extremely pleasant group of people and were very helpful and friendly during the period I was with them.

Their training sessions, usually two evenings a week, are held on the outskirts of Stockholm. The sessions are similar to those at Belgrave Hall but with more emphasis on exercising and sprinting. It was interesting to see that youngsters of eight and nine were training very seriously and walking quite long distances.

At weekends when there were no races there would be a two day camp in the country. I was fortunate in being invited to one of these and it was again noticable that general exercises were a major part of their training. All of the walkers would carry out the same exercises and this would be followed by a long steady stroll over forest paths which are plentiful in that area. The distances covered over the two days were in the region of 25 kms. although the youngsters would only do about half that. Although the training was harder than I have ever done before it was an enjoyable weekend.

There was an excellent club spirit amonst the walkers and Siw Gustavsson, with whom I trained and who is one of the leading Swedish walkers, made me feel very much at home.

I was invited to walk in one of the major championships held at Boras There I found that many eight and nine year olds were racing. In fact there were seventeen different grades of walkers including the leading Finns and Swedes. Regrettably, with so much talent about, I suffered an inferiority complex.

During my three week stay I managed a few trips around Stockholm and saw many of its notable sites, discovering that the city is made up of a number of islands all linked by a number of bridges.

Overall I had an interesting and enjoyable trip and if all goes well I hope to have a reciprocal arrangement next year with some of their walkers coming over here.

SPOTLIGHT ON - ARNOLD BENTT

by Alan Mead

SPOTLIGHT ON - ARNOLD BENTT

Five times England Schools competitor 1965-1969.

by Alan Mead

One of the most talented and regular members of the Belgrave track and field team for the last decade and more, Arnold is second to very few other Belgravians when one considers the total number of points he has amassed for the club in League and inter-club matches during that period. Now twenty-eight, he admits that he hasn't really trained hard since he left school.... in fact more than that ....he now rarely trains at all! For Arnold getting fit now means beginning to compete in May, gradually overcoming the sore muscle stage and working up a competition edge as the season wears on.

A typical trophy meeting will see him arrive well after the match has started (when travelling by coach we tell him to get there half an hour before anyone else), go straight into the fourth round of the pole vault (it's always worth declaring him even if he's not there yet), lazily soar over about 3 metres and then start looking round for something else to do.

* * *

Arnold was born in 1950 in Kingston, Jamaica, and came to London at the age of nine. He went to St. Gerard's School and by the time he was thirteen was already showing an interest in athletics. "Little Joe" Harvey, now long since fallen by the wayside but then one of Frank Simmons' regulars at the Ravenstone School weight training sessions, introduced Arnold to the Club and he became a member in June, 1963. Before long he was sweating his way round Frank's circuits like the rest of us, being shouted at to "Get your knees up!" and showing his enjoyment of this treatment by coming back for more punishment at Battersea Park track on Sunday mornings.

Third place in the Boys' "Mrs. L. Ryan" Cup marked his entry to competition in 1964, covering thows, jumps and running events from 100 yards to half a mile; the ideal way to start off for a youngster in athletics - having a go at everything. By the following year though, it was obvious that jumping was to be his forte.

From this period on Belgrave Harriers were fortunate in having a remarkable squad of long jumpers training under coach Dave Ansell's supervision: John Mitchell, Cliff Brooks, Bob Jones and later Alan Lerwill and Steve White. It was not surprising that in such a fertile atmosphere and under the careful nurturing of one of the best jumping coaches of the time, Arnold's talent bloomed.

Having won the London Schools long jump in 1965 he went on to place second in the All-England event but "coach" was particularly impressed with his high jumping potential. The "Belgravian" reported that fourteen year old Arnold Bentt had reached 1.72 (5'8") at a time when the top ten juniors in the country ranged from 1.90 (6'3") down to 1.80 (5'11") but Arnold had another four years in hand over many of these top rankers and therefore had excellent prospects of leading the junior lists before reaching senior status. (Seniors were eighteen and over in those days.)

In 1966 Arnold began to be plagued by a heel injury that was to hold back his progress for the next two years. Nevertheless, he took several long and high jump wins in junior trophy meetings, represented London Schools again in the long jump, set youth and junior Club records in the high jump, won his first Club Championship in the junior "high" with a silver medal in the senior event to boot, and even beat Steve White over 100 yards!

He had more or less switched to the high jump full time by 1967, finding that jumping for length continued to pound his injury prone heel. In spite of not really improving his best leap since 1965 his competitive record was good. Retaining the junior Club title, he also annexed the senior event. The Surrey County junior title was added to his list of achievements and he represented Surrey in the Inter-Counties, placed 2nd in the London Schools and made his 3rd trip to the England Schools Championships where he reached 6th. Probably the highlight of the season, however, was his selection to take part in the Inter-Cities match in Denmark where he returned to the long jump.

* * *

The big break-through came the following year after concentrating on the technique side of his high jumping. Arnold was always an exponent of the straddle style, particularly admiring the art as executed by Russia's Valeriy Brumel before that man's career was wrecked by a motor-cycling accident. Paradoxically, it was this year, 1968, that the athletics world became introduced to the "Fosbury Flop" when Dick Fosbury (USA) won the Mexico Olympic event with his then unusual but now most common style; but for Arnold the practised discipline of the straddle was the key to his success.

In a leisurely inter-club match at Tootirg he went over 6 feet for the first time. Then came the South London Schools title which he took in his stride at 1.86 (6'1½"), followed by his burst onto the National scene by winning the London event in 1.91 (6'3½") to become the United Kingdom's number one junior (fulfilling the "Belgravian" prediction) - seventh on the senior list.
