COMMENT

The Editor

You hold before you a new editor's first edition of 'The Belgravian'. I hope you will bear with him as he picks up the threads from those who have gone before and who have done so much to make this magazine one of the best of its kind. Do remember though, that it's success depends upon a team that includes you! Your articles, ideas, letters, photographs and even your criticisms will be well received.

At the A.G.M. it was mentioned during a discussion that there seems to be a state of apathy existing in the Club. Whilst we cannot lay claim to being athletic earth-shakers, we are certainly not doing badly. On the other hand, when it comes to organisation, fund raising, stepping forward to help with timekeeping, etc., it is the same old faithfuls every time who make the effort and I think the accusation is fair.

You may well say that you joined this Club because you wanted to walk, throw or run but the fact is, if nobody did anything towards keeping our Club going we would be in a fine state. Alright, some people really do not want to become too involved in our activities but if every member took just one weekly football ticket, took one item of jumble up to the hall next time we hold a jumble sale, turned out to support our team at one home fixture, well, the possibilities are enormous.

Let's all push towards that improvement in our Headquarters that we badly need; let's sell ourselves to any prospective new member that we can; and let's all strive towards making Belgrave Harriers a vibrant club that knows where it is going.

The Editor

THE PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE

Clive Shippen

Fellow Belgravians,

I am very conscious of the honour, and indeed the responsibility, which you bestowed upon me when you decided that I should occupy the Presidential Chair during the coming year.

It is an awesome feeling to be faced with the duty of presiding over the affairs of the finest club in the land; and it is my fervent hope that I will be able to fulfill my duties as effectively as my predecessors did.

Over 200 years ago Benjamin Franklin observed, "At twenty years of age, the will reigns; at thirty, the wit; and at forty, the judgement." As your 40th President, and at 40 years of age too, I hope for the Club's sake that JUDGEMENT does reign supreme; also wishing of course that my WIT has not entirely deserted me and that my continued training has prolonged the WILL!

During our eight and a half decades of history we have had many years of exceptional success, and I doubt whether our greatest years will ever be matched again, by any club. So it would be unrealistic for me to call for our most successful year yet. I would much prefer to rely on our excellent band of honorary secretaries and captains to produce the best of which we are capable - we can ask for no more. To do this they must have your full co-operation, and I appeal to you all to give them the support they need -- and, who knows, we might yet emulate our best years.

Wishing you all the best of success in the coming year,

Clive Shippen

ANNUAL GENERAL MEETING

SEPTEMBER 21st 1972.

The evenings imperceptibly shorten, the air strikes more chill as one leaves the Hall for a canter over the Common; but nothing heralds the approach of the winter season more than the Annual General Meeting, bringing with it the close of another Club year.

Speculation was rife amongst those who were there early. Would the Hall manage to hold the massive turnout of Belgravians that were expected to show up and air their views on the running of the Club's affairs, just as they all congregated for that other extravaganza - the Club Track Championships. Well, somehow most of those who were interested managed to get in. Perhaps the 450-odd members who couldn't make it will be luckier next year.

Our able Gen. Hon. Sec. opened the proceedings and we reflected upon the reports covering the year's activities. Yes, it had been a slightly disappointing cross country season. Road relay results were better and our track and field team had finished the summer season very strongly. Good to hear that our walking section has been so full of esprit de corps.

Somehow though, this was pretty tame stuff. Merely a preamble to the more contentious issue of the Committee's recommendation to raise the subscriptions. Here the meeting suddenly came alive. Two camps were quickly established and verbal battle ensued.

Led by some of our financial wizards, the "againsts" took up the initiative.

"It's all very well to put the subs up but what is going to be done with the extra. I know we eventually want to improve the Hall but for years we have talked of this and gone no farther. Costs rise and the dead money sitting in our Building Society account becomes worth less and less."

Subdued murmers of approval changed to roars of laughter when it was added, "And I'm a life member - I don't have to cough."

The voices of the "fors" rose above the clamour.

"The subs should keep us going on running expenses, that's why they should go up. Any cash gained through other fund raising must go towards the building fund."

"We live in the age of the 10p minimum "tube" fare - I don't consider £3 goes far enough. Make it £5."

A vote was eventually taken and the recommendation passed by the sufficient two-thirds majority.

Those worthy and energetic members Bill Couzens and Chas. Manning were elected to life membership and the services of John Baker and Joe MacDonald were also recognised - they were made vice presidents.

So to the election of Officers and Committee for 1972/73, with Clive Shippen taking over the President's chair from Mickey Pyer. "No change" was the cry as most offices came to be filled but a special note should be made of Bob Taylor's retirement from the Track Sec's job after many years of invaluable service.

Suddenly it was over. Chairs scraped in unison as newly elected officers led their followers out into the September night to celebrate their rise to power in the time honoured style.

OUR PRESIDENT

Olympian

Although mildly interested in athletics, largely as a high jumper, during his school days at the Brixton School of Building, our new President did not join a club until he came upon the Belgrave scene at the age of twenty. He was at that time a Rover Scout whose troop visited Belgrave Hall for a few weeks prior to their annual cross country race for special training. Bill Merryman was deputed to lead this pack and was responsible for Clive joining the Club in March 1952. For the first year of his membership he did little or nothing but the Rover Scout championship in 1953 again raised his enthusiasm and he began to enter into club life and first donned the claret and gold vest seriously in the South of the Thames Junior Race in November of that year when he finished 172nd.

Due to his engineering training his period of National Service had been deferred but in December 1953 we lost his services to the Army, in which he was commissioned in the Royal Engineers and was responsible for his unit athletic team reaching the Army finals in 1955.

The year 1956 was spent settling back into civilian life as a Constructional Engineer and it was not until 1957 that Clive again took athletics seriously. In that year he was 138th in the Southern and 207th in the National. For the next six years he represented the Club in most of the major cross country and road relay races his best performances being in 1961/62 when he won his Surrey County colours for cross country and finished 56th in the Southern and 135th in the National. One of his best efforts was on the Brighton Road in the London to Brighton Relay when he produced the 2nd best time of the day on the Dale Hill slope in a time which stood as a Club record for a number of years.

Clive's activities were by no means confined to cross country and rad relays for he began to earn a reputation as a steeplechaser and for a time held the Belgrave record at 9:28 - this will no doubt bring a smile to the face of John Bicourt but in 1962 it was good enough to place our President 3rd in the Sward Trophy race.

Unfortunately, late in 1962 Clive picked up a liver virus which kept him out of major events and although appearing in Club races it was not until 1968 that he again trained seriously and took up distance running on the road. In 1970 at the age of thirty-nine he ran a marathon in 2hrs 41minutes 20secs.

The highlight of our President's athletic career came recently when on August 24th 1972 at Crystal Palace in the Veteran's International Championships he finished 3rd in the 3,000 metres steeplechase in a British veterans record of 10:04.8. Two weeks later in Cologne he beat the Australian who had finished 2nd at Crystal Palace to finish 2nd in an international race in an improved time of 10:01.6 which still stands as the present British record.

Not only has Clive performed well for Belgrave as an athlete but during his twenty years as a member he was Editor of the 'Belgravian' from 1959/1970, was cross country captain for three years and track vice-captain for two years. He has served on our own committee since 1957; was made a Vice-President in 1966 and a Life Member in 1969 and has also found time to serve on the committees of the Surrey County A.A.A. and the South of the Thames Cross Country Association.

Everyone who knows Clive respects him as a hard working member of our community always willing to give of his best to help the Club. Any work he undertakes can be guaranteed to be of the highest quality, carried out in a most efficient manner. Clive is endowed with a natural charm which endears him to us all and under his leadership the Club is bound to prosper. We are also grateful to our President's wife Elizabeth who has always graced all our social events and has been prepared to assist with tea making and at jumble sales for many years.

GRAPEVINE

Let us put on record our admiration for our recently retired President, MICK PYER, and express our thanks to him. Mick attended most of our meetings, summer and winter; coached our junior javelin throwers and displayed throughout the utmost enthusiasm for the Club and its activities. And all the while his sight has been fading, and the once champion marksman of the London Irish finds himself groping in the shadows and having difficulty in recognising his friends of many years standing. Needless to say he accepts this situation with courage and humility - and merely asks for continuing opportunities to serve the Club and its members.

What a pity there was no photographer present to record an historic occasion at our Club Track and Field Championships, when MICK PYER as President presented the Garnett Cup for the 1500 metres championship to the winner, Peter Carton, fifty years on from the inaugural championship, which Mick himself had won back in 1922.

On the occasion of the R.W.A. 20 miles Championship at Worsley, near Manchester we were delighted to see Past President SYD ROBERTS, with his wife who had travelled the long journey from St. Annes-on-Sea to see the Club in action.

Our Vice-President EDMUND DONOVAN OBE who has resided in America for fifty years, was able to attend a Southern League fixture at Wimbledon Park Track, and tell us of Belgrave of a bygone age and of the characters who helped to build the Club.

BERT MITCHELL, always associated with our Club Marathon Championship Shield, paid us a visit on the occasion of the 20 Miles Open Run and promises that his next visit to Wimbledon will not be so long delayed.

At the same event CHRIS STEER showed himself (or the greater part of himself!) and threatened further visits in the future.

HAROLD KING, a Club walking champion many times over, celebrated his 51st year of membership at the Club Track Championships.

Past President FRANK SIMMONS was able to attend the Annual General Meeting and we were very pleased to see him.

Another AGM visitor was SYD SPENCER former 'B' Division Police and Belgrave walker - now on the Judges list.

It was a great pleasure when our Vice-President from Delhi, RANJIT BHATIA paid us a visit in late September and competed in our road race. He will be remembered as a great asset to our teams in his Oxford University days, which led to Olympic honours for India in the Rome Olympics. He is striving hard to establish and encourage Athletics in his part of India, with some considerable measure of success.

Additional Belgrave representation in the Olympic Games to John Bicourt and Cliff Brooks was provided by WILLI WESCH who represented W.Germany in the 20,000 metres walk.

Our thanks and good wishes to JACK OLIVER on his retirement to the Portsmouth area. Jack was of considerable service to us as a walking judge for many years.

Having an Olympic steeplechaser in the Club in John Bicourt, reminds us of a former Belgrave Olympic steeplechaser of the 1930's: JIM GINTY. Jim competed at Berlin in 1936; and had the distinction of leading the English team to a win in the 1937 International Cross Country Championship, after finishing 3rd (and scoring 1) in the E.C.C.U. Championship at Stratford-on-Avon. Incidently we scored 94 points and lost to Birchfield by four points! We hope John will succeed in getting double International honours as did Jim many years ago.

JACK GOSWELL (with FRANK JARVIS at his right hand as Hon Secretary) has survived a difficult year as President of the Race Walking Assn., and is to be congratulated on the sincerity and efficiency with which he tackled the job.

BOB TAYLOR bowed out as Track Hon. Secretary at the AGM, but it is generally understood that this a temporary respite only. Bob is much too good a man to have kicking his heels on the side lines.

ARTHUR PENNY, the Empire Games 6 miles Champion of 1934 and an International cross country runner, has officiated as our starter for many years past but he is now anxious to relinquish the position into younger hands. May we have some volunteers please who will approach this office with the seriousness it merits?

Timekeepers also are needed if our athletes are to fill up their little black books, and it is emphasised that 100 guinea watches are by no means necessary. Regularity of appearance and the conscientious approach are much more important.

DANNY WISEMAN - the very name brings fear to the hearts of those who consider themselves 'two-lappers'. Danny got down to 1:52.2 for 880 yards some years back but then disappeared to qualify as a solicitor and then see a bit of the world. He is now getting back into training and hopes to run on the track next summer.

Cheerful letters from ALF TAYLOR and TONY WHINCUP remind us of two stalwarts of years ago. We wish them well.

Feature · page 9

Dave Ansell goes North

P.B.H.

It's always a bit of a wrench when one of the "old contemptibles" packs his bags and leaves Belgrave territory. Dave Ansell's departure to Leeds, where he is chief design executive for the Burton Group, was no exception to this generalisation. Dave has been a Belgravian for 21 years both as an active athlete and a coach. In both activities he achieved some distinction, particularly the latter where he is the chief jumps coach to the A.A.A.

Coaching caught Dave's interest when he was approaching the end of his active career and he quickly built up a young jumps school, where members went from strength to strength. Best known are internationals Alan Lerwill, Britain's successor to Lynn Davies, and Derek Cole - both second claim Belgravians, and Belgravians John Mitchell and Steve White.

I think Dave's appeal to the younger athlete is founded on his cheerful philosophy of life coupled with an intense attention to detail. Both John Mitchell and Alan Lerwill testify to his successful ability to harness natural ability to discipline - much of the early spadework taking place in the often depressing surroundings of Battersea Park.

Outside his coaching Dave was a good supporter of the Club. I remember several National League meetings where he was to be seen by the trackside, in the pub, or elsewhere. He was not only a cheerful companion on many occasions, but had a becoming modesty about his abilities, from which many could learn. His presence will be missed greatly.

P.B.H.

Obituary · page 10

ERNEST H. NEVILLE

A. A. Harley

These days we regularly come across the phrase - " a legend in his own life time" - and this could well have originated with Ernest H. Neville who has recently passed on at the age of 89, after a lifetime of inspiration to others and positive action in the service of others. To do justice to such a subject demands a first class biographer with far-reaching research facilities which would go back to the late 1800's and early this century. It is to be hoped that someone, perhaps one of his old Surrey Walking Club or Blackheath Harriers colleagues will be able to fill in the early gaps in such a full life and give us a picture of Ernest in his early days of legal and business training and his earliest contacts with Sport and particularly that of Walking. It could be a really fascinating study.

My own personal recollections of Ernest only carry back to the late 1920's when I remember his attendance at the Annual General Meeting of the Belgrave Harriers of which he was a Vice-President - when he enthused over the Club's successes and encouraged to even more success in the future.

It was perhaps in the committee and conference room that Ernest was at his absolute best. His erudition combined with his wide knowledge of meeting and administrative procedures, his solid reassuring physical appearance, his sincerity and enthusiasm for his subject, made him formidable indeed, especially when all this was backed by a willingness and an ability to work hard and long in the implementation of committee decisions. He was assisted in this by his wide range of personal contacts, and his ability to persuade Lord Mayors, Members of Parliament, Newspaper Editors right down to the humblest club members to serve and assist with whatever projects he had in mind at a particular time.

His enthusiasm communicated itself to all he met, and this was the key to his personality and the mark of a man of true greatness. This quality allied with a determination " to see it through" plus the skill and ability to translate committee decisions and draft plans into positive action and realistic results were in the Neville mould.

His physical bulk, command of language and manner of conducting himself gave him a sense of authority wherever he chanced to be. To be with Ernest was to receive the highest possible service and attention normally reserved only for the vippiest of V.I.P.'s. His manner was ever polite and courteous and his personality impress brought forth response from all he met, and his generosity was all-pervading and really something to be seen to be believed. The R.W.A. and our Sport benefit[illegible]d very considerably from Ernest's generous impulses which flared outwards, especially in his 62, London Wall office days of the late 1920's when his business abilities earned him phenominal rewards.

Even as an older man Ernest was constantly setting an example to his juniors of undiluted enthusiasm (without which nothing can be achieved), and a willingness to work effectively whether it be as Chairman of an important Committee or as manipulator of the loud-hailer at the conclusion of the Brighton Walk. His enthusiasm was always a delight to see and one can only hope that some of it has "rubbed off" in good measure on to our later generations.

Of the respect in which he was held throughout the world of Athletics much could be written, and the influence he exerted in sport is beyond computation. I have confined myself to jotting down some thoughts on Ernest as a man and a friend and a personality who has left his indelible mark on our Sport and bequeathed us something really worthwhile which can be maintained and improved in the way he would have wished in perpetuity. Others will write of Ernest's influence and work on R.W.A. Committees, of his ungrudging services at Club, County, Area and National level, of his yeoman efforts within the Centurians and the Road Runners Club, and over many years internationally, where his work and thinking have earned sincere appreciation.

At various times I served on Rules Revision Committees with Ernest and invariably we agreed to commit our ideas to paper and then invite the comments of the other members and so make slow but sure progress. Usually I found myself thinking that there was no great hurry for our work could not be ratified for another six or twelve months - and so my reaction was one of qualified procrastination. Not so with Ernest however! Within 36 hours one received an important legal looking missive timed usually at 4 a.m. (a typical Nevillism) and beautifully handwritten in thin thick copperplate style in black and red ink with marginal title and underlinings in red and black, and embellished with legal red tapes. A real work of art in fact, and although one might disagree with every suggested clause and sentence, nevertheless one was compelled to do something about it and make ones comments, and draft possible alterations.

So did Ernest sham his younger colleagues into doing their duty rather quicker than they would otherwise have done, and providing an excellent example for others to follow.

To finish with an anecdote which I think deserves to be commited to paper. At an R.W.A. Committee meeting Ernest started the discussion on some subject or other with the words "This morning I consulted my Oxford Dictionary of the English Language....... He was interrupted by Charlie Megnin who had a reputation as a wit who said "I'm shocked. I never dreamed Mr. Neville consulted dictionaries - I thought he wrote them." Ernest enjoyed the joke as much as anyone!

Good-bye! Ernest - We cannot fail to miss you!

A. A. Harley

Obituary · page 11

G. S. Doubleday

We are extremely sorry to record the death, in his 90th year, of our esteemed Vice-President, doyen of long distance running and walking, Ernest Neville. His death occurred on Wednesday 1st November, a few days after a stomach operation at St. Georges Hospital, Tooting, where he was a patient in Belgrave Ward. A member of many athletic clubs he joined us before World War I, and was proud of being a Belgravian.

He was attracted to athletics in 1897 when he saw the late Teddy Knott walking to Brighton, and, at the age of fourteen he walked from London to Brighton. Four years later he did the double journey, having a meal and a rest at Brighton, within twenty-four hours. In 1902 he promoted the first London to Brighton and Back Walk on behalf of Surrey W.C. From that time on he has been associated with walking and running promotions until a few weeks ago.

He was a formidable competitor at running, from the sprints to the marathon, and at walking, from the Mile to 100 miles. He did many feats of long distance walking, and for many years he walked, all duly recorded, 100 miles a week. In 1903 in a Brighton and Back walk, promoted by himself, again for Surrey W.C., he finished third in 21h.13m.50secs. In 1911 he, and other walkers who had walked 100 miles inside 24 hours founded the Centurians, a fraternity restricted to those who had accomplished this feat, he became number seven and at the time of his death was Life President. He was a Past President of the R.W.A. and of the Surrey County A.A.A. and Surrey W.C.

His sporting interests were not confined to athletics and he took part in other sports and could speak with considerable authority about them. A great worker behind the scenes he served on many committees of Athletic Associations and Clubs. When the A.A.A. needed information or guidance on matters concerning long distance racing they instinctively turned to him for advice. He was a keen supporter of athletics at County level, and was a tower of strength to his own County, Surrey.

Up until 1924 there had only been track walks up to 10,000 metres in the Olympic Games, there had been so much controversy that they were left out of the 1928 Games. It was largely due to Ernest that walking was restored to the 1932 Games at Los Angeles. The race was a 50 kilometres Road Walk, won by our own Tommy Green. He continually pressed for walking at International level and at the 1936 (Berlin) Games he was Chief Judge.

After World War II came the Festival of Britain in 1951 and amongst the sporting events was a running race from London to Brighton this was organised by Ernest and was a great success. Subsequently, in order to draw long-distance runners together and to further their interests he founded the Road Runners Club. There were few long distance races in those days and the R.R.C. can claim a large credit for the healthy state and popularity of Road Running today. Not only was he Founder but for many years he was General Hon. Secretary of the R.R.C., and served a term as President.

As the years advanced his enthusiasm remained undiminished, most of the present World Best Performances at both Running and Walking were made at events largely organised by him. He was the organiser par excellence, he did the groundwork very thoroughly writing to all potential helpers. His instructions to competitors and officials were models of clarity and precision. After the event letters of thanks would go out to all who had helped.

He had a prodigious memory and could recount an event which happened at the turn of the century as if it took place recently. His vast experience of athletic affairs must have been unequalled and his counsels were much sought for and appreciated.

Right up to his death he attended meetings and played a full part in the deliberations. His attitudes were always forward looking and he was always receptive to new ideas.

This year's London to Brighton run was his last promotion and was a very happy day for him - a great race, a new record, and a surprise presentation to him of a plaque from the Mayor of Brighton for his many years of promoting races from London to Brighton.

In the usual letter of thanks to helpers he announced his retirement from promoting, happy in the knowledge that he had found, encouraged and taught others to carry on the good work he had done, as no other man could over 70 years.

He also led a very busy life in business, largely connected with Insurance, Finance and the Law. For his services to the City of London as a Fireguard Commander in World War II he was made an Honorary Freeman, an honour reserved for the few.

In closing this tribute we say "Good-bye and Thank you, Ernest for all you have done", your name and memory will be honoured by Belgravians for many years to come.

G. S. Doubleday

SPOTLIGHT ON LAURIE O'HARA & BILL WELLER

Gordon Biscoe

It is more than appropriate at this time that our spotlight should fall jointly on Laurie and Bill, for not only are they both completing what has been the most successful year in their respective careers but it is interesting to note that although from different athletic backgrounds and age groups their approach and outlook to the sport is so similar.

With the same consistancy with which our various press reports never let us forget who Lionel Mann's employers are, so since June of this year the latest 'in thing' has been to refer to "Veteran Laurie O'Hara". However, with equal consistancy Laurie has produced a string of performances on track and road that have served to prove just how ridiculous it is in these times to stick a 'Veteran' label on 40 year old athletes.

Following a very successful cross country and road relay season, Laurie produced a personal best of 30:57.6 to take the Club 10,000 metres championship with an almost solo effort and then proceeded in the following three months to lower his 5,000 metre time on three separate occasions finishing with a time of 14:28s. The highlight of the season, however, was, no doubt, in August and September when at two separate international veterans meetings at the Crystal Palace and Cologne he shattered the opposition to take the 5,000 metres titles. Of the two occasions, the Crystal Palace produced possibly the best veterans 5000 metres field ever assembled with such people as former Australian champion, Dave Power, in contention. As one observer has noted Laurie met the challenge by most uncharacteristically bursting to the front in the first lap and proceeded to open a gap of some 80 yards from the rest of the field. Although this had been cut to 4 seconds at the bell he still won by some 10 seconds having run the heart out of his rivals.

Having briefly looked at Laurie's achievements in the last five months since becoming a vet., one must not let these performances overshadow the tremendous years of service he has given to Belgrave since joining, supposedly at a late age. It was in 1961, after a successful career at amateur football, having played for Reading, Bexley Heath and Harrow Town, that Laurie joined the club. Having reached about as far as he could go in football he decided to try athletics, and struck up training acquaintances with Fred Paget and Pat Newell in Fulham and it was these two who were instrumental in getting him to sign the necessary piece of paper! From his first outing on the track in Belgrave's colours his tremendous talents were obvious and that he would be a force to be reckoned with, in and outside Belgrave, was in no doubt. He quickly graduated to our 'A' teams, has effectively stayed there ever since and on his present performances how many are there who can keep him out?!!

To try and enumerate Laurie's performances over the years within the limitations of these pages would be an impossible task, the Brighton, Southern and National Relays; Southern and National C.C. Championships; League Races; Local Relays; Club Championships; and just plain inter-club meetings, he has left his mark on them all in his own quiet efficient manner. Possibly his greatest inspiration has been in the Road Relays, a last leg specialist, many are the instances when in a closely fought contest at the closing stages of a race he has driven our supporters wild with delight as he has unleashed that now famous burst of acceleration to drop an opponent and put Belgrave back in the medals. One feels that if Laurie had ever graduated to international ranks on the track, this characteristic alone would have made him a great crowd puller at the White City. Laurie confesses though, that it is Road Running which he enjoys most and recalls that the performance which gave him the most pleasure was winning the first stage in one of the old London - Brighton Road Relays. In spite of first class performances in the country he insists"I only really do it to keep fit - I don't like the country really".

Although doing his fair share, he is not one of the present day mileage fanatics, and he's always had to fit his running in with being a family man and his career as an Electrician, which over the years has always involved him in shift work with the result that many's the time he's turned out to race with hardly any sleep behind him.

This Summer's bonanza for Laurie was brought to a climax in September when he made his debut over the Marathon distance. Running in the Harlow marathon he finished in a commendable time of 2hr.35mins, but admitted to going too fast early on, very blistered and very tired it was summed up with the comment, "I learnt a lot this time and I'll know better next!"

Unlike Laurie O'Hara's 11 years service, Bill Weller has now been with us for exactly two, but characteristically has integrated into the Club so enthusiastically that the time seems much longer and he has long since been considered as part of the furniture.

Born in October, 1946, Bill's interest in running stems from the age of 8 years old, when one of his earliest recollections were 2 mile runs along the coast road near Goring, Sussex, having arranged for his father to drive two miles up the road in the car and wait for him. The main awakening came, however, when he changed from primary to secondary school at the age of 11 and found that running was a compulsory sport. To his surprise he found that he could keep going long after his contempories had been forced to stop and drop out, from then on he was 'hooked'! At the age of 13 running seemed a good excuse for not playing football or hockey. Having satisfied the master he would be participating in some such game Bill would quietly slide off for a one and a half hours run over the Downs from his school in Epsom. Unfortunately games periods lasted 1 hour so by the time Bill arrived back search parties were being organised to look for him. However, he maintains that it was these early runs of 9 - 10 miles over the Downs to Headley and back which laid the basis for his stamina in later years, these workouts being augmented by the daily runs from his school into Epsom to catch the early bus home at 4 p.m., those less fleet of foot being forced to wait another half-hour.

His early club days were with Redhill and Reigate A.C. from 1961/65 and he recalls at the age of 15 having races and training sessions of 9 miles or more with their Senior team members in Reigate Priory, and anyone who has ever run in the Reigate Relays will know what that involves. Originally Bill confesses to having illusions about being a track star over 880 yards. However, after a considerable amount of track training in 1963 he remembers coming 2nd to Steve Badgery (now Hercules Wimbledon A.C.) over 880 yards in 2:05, so "I soon gave that up".

In 1965 he joined Walton A.C. and it was while he was with them, having tried his hand at track, road and country without any great success that he developed primarily into a long distance road man.

Spotlight....

Coinciding with the time he joined Belgrave from Walton in 1970 Bill, a Civil Servant, moved with his job from Kingston to Redhill and thus being nearer his home in Reigate was able to devote more time to training. From then on a steady improvement has continued in his performances over the last two years, resulting in rather an impressive tally of placings in this year's road races. These include winning the Belgrave Open 20 miles, Surrey 20 miles Championship, Worthing 11 miles (breaking his own record) and Wimbledon 10 miles, as well as placings in the Bracknell 10 miles (2nd), Walton 10 miles (2nd), Epsom 11 miles (4th) and at the start of the year 4th in the Mitcham 25 Km.

Most of these races have been against tough competition and apart from individual achievements Bill has anchored our team to similar successes on these occasions as our results sheets show.

After joining us from Walton, he had to serve his waiting period of 9 months but once eligible and once in our teams, like Laurie O'Hara he has stayed there, producing many sterling performances for us earlier in the Spring and this Autumn. Bill also is no lover of cross country running and being subject to injury over it, tends to 'disappear' in the winter months, however, if a league race or the Surrey Championships turn up and we happen to be under pressure, team wise, he'll answer the call and is ready to do battle to, as he puts it, "help keep that other lot at bay". (The 'other lot' being anyone not Belgrave).

One could possibly describe Bill Weller as the complete athlete, for when he joined us he brought with him his own supporters club. I refer, of course, to his mother and father who enthusiastically accompany him on most outings and in all weathers, supporting not only Bill but all of the Belgrave team. Having managed, so far, to avoid the marriage stakes, his main interest outside athletics is horse racing; and remembers putting his training to good purpose while still at school to race across Epsom Downs on Derby day to place a bet! Nowadays on Saturday afternoons prior to a race, the way he adjusts his warm up with the checking of the latest results from Kempton or Newmarket etc. on the car radio, is an exercise in precision timing.

There is no doubt that in Laurie O'Hara and Bill Weller Belgrave has two great clubmen to be proud of. Both are of that quiet type who do not herald forthcoming performances with selfcentred ballyhoo but who, with a professional-like no-nonsense approach, set out to give of their best with every performance, so characteristic of another Belgravian - Gerry North.

From a team managers point of view their general reliability and attitude is a refreshing change to some, and one that others would do well to copy; and to our younger members I would say that these are the characteristics they should seek to mould themselves on.

Watching Bill Weller in action during one of the relays this Spring someone remarked to me "I'm glad he's on our side now"! - I would now like to modify this remark and say "I'm glad they're both on our side"!!

Gordon Biscoe

ROAD AND COUNTRY

Gordon Biscoe

To try and comment on our fortunes etc. over the last eighteen months, when the Belgravian was in danger of becoming a collectors item, would be almost an impossible task and in any case much of this has now been collated as history in the recent edition brought out in November. It would thus appear more practical to look forward to what lies ahead, although at the moment it is still a long way off from the major cross country and road relay races, and thus too early to try and predict our chances. However, whatever else may transpire this is no time to relax our efforts for once again we have the unfortunate task of saying good-bye (one that seems to happen all too often for us) to two of our most promising younger stars namely, Tony Binda and Tony Brien. These are two names we can ill afford to lose for I had hoped that we would be able to call upon their talents for many years and thus help rebuild the younger Belgrave team we have been seeking since our disasters in 1971.

Tony Binda returns to his native Jamaica and although he has been featured elsewhere in this issue, I would like to add personal thanks for all his efforts, from his races as a youth to his appearances in the senior teams. His cheerful personality will be missed as much as his ability.

Tony Brien is now studying in Kansas University, following his departure from the R.A.F. earlier this year. He had already established himself as a 'resident' member in our senior team and his potential was probably more fully realised this summer when he only just missed selection for Ireland in the Olympic Games Marathon.

In wishing them both well and every success can we hope that we have not yet seen the last of their appearances in Claret and Gold.

For the immediate future, however, who will fill the gaps they leave? - will the Spring of 1973 see Adrian Major and Tony Fairclough back in full cry once more? Can Bill Curtin exploits the talents he's got by making the effort to get fit again and can someone like Doug Jardine, Doug Maughan, Leo Coy or Bob Dixon (a recent but welcome addition to our ranks) do a 'Trevor Hart' of former years and suddenly break through into the 'big time' - all of them have shown considerable improvements this year.

The answers for them, and others, will lie in the training they do, but it is worth remembering that our successes as a Club will depend upon our successes as a Team in competitive races, not in private titanic struggles, fought out with Brian Gorman in Richmond Park.

COUNTRYMAN'S JOTTINGS

Countryman

The first of the big cross country championships is the Surrey County event, to be held at Morden Park on January 6th. We are well overdue for a win. Our last seven performances read 1,1,2,2,3,2,2. Five years without Surrey gold is a famine indeed, so what are the prospects this year? Well, John Bicourt has not fully recovered from the bout of hepatitus which he suffered on his return from the Olympics, and Bill Weller is expecting to undergo and extensive course of dental treatment at that time. As they were 6th and 13th last year they will take some replacing. Fortunately, Bob Smith and Jim Rimmer are going very well and Trevor Hart should repeat his 20th place of last year. Leading us home, we hope, will be Gerry North with his regular place inside the first ten. It will be a tough fight for gold this year.

My picture opposite shows the start of the County race of 43 years ago which Belgrave won from Surrey A.C. and S.L.H. The winner was our own Bert Footer, and three others joined him in the County team. Les Cohen (then Ashcombe A.C.) also made it. How close will we get to that in 1973.

In the front row of the line-up can be seen the famous Laurie Weatherill, Jimmy Green (later Belgrave and managing editor of Athletics Weekly) and George Tolley, who went on to become President of the E.C.C.U., S.C.C.C.A., S.C.A.A.A. and the Surrey County A.A.A. It was a sad loss to British Athletics when George died suddenly only last month.

We have suffered a severe blow in the loss of three of our leading runners in recent weeks. TONY BRIEN, who was our first man home in last year's National, is now resident at Kansas University, U.S.A., TONY BINDA, a fast improving Junior with tremendous potential has returned to the West Indies just as he was beginning to show the form to get amongst our senior scorers, and JOHN PHELAN is our third loss, having returned to Ireland after finishing his studies at St. Mary's College. However, in his case, we hope he will make the trip for the Southern and National.

Another likely to have to travel for our major races is Lionel Mann. Lionel, a chef in the Queen's household, is presently being groomed for a principal position and this necessitates his working in Switzerland and France for lengthy periods. Fortunately, he has now regained full fitness and looked very lean and sharp on his recent return from the Alps. In Paris at the moment, he is most keen to get back for the big races.

Whatever our runners do in the Surrey County race next month we are assured at least one place in the team. Yes, I mean the team manager. This position has been held for several years now by Bill Lucas and my picture shows him with Surrey's winning team at Leicester in 1971 which he managed to engineer during his year as Surrey County President! This season he takes the County team to Leicester once again - can he repeat the feat?

Tony Fairclough and Bill Kerr now live within about six miles of each other in the Gillingham/Sittingbourne area. Nearby, of course, is Geoff North who was actually seen watching a race recently - though it was difficult to tell whether it had any effect on him! Is it possible that this formidable trio will form a training pack this winter and then let themselves loose on an unsuspecting world next Spring?

FLEA POWER

(Sunday Times)

FLEA POWER Bob Beamon's long jump and Pat Matzdorf's high jump - world records at 29ft 2½in and 7ft 6¼in respectively - are fairly feeble feats when compared to a flea. If a flea were a man, we discover in the British journal Nature, he could leap clear out of a stadium. In fact, size for size, an Oriental rat flea can high jump 275ft and long jump 450ft.

"Although fleas," write Nature's flea-watchers, "have lost their ability to fly, they have retained and modified several important features associated with a winged mode of locomotion and incorporated them into the jump mechanism." Which is to say, the flea doesn't rely on muscular strength at all, but cocks and locks itself back on a store of spring like energy in its jumping legs before it lets fly: its peak acceleration is 140 times the force of gravity, which is 14 times the acceleration that would cause a man to black out.

That rules out the application of flea power to athletics. In fact, if Nature magazine had scratched deeper they might have discovered, as a subsequent issue of New Scientist did, that another bug is a few jumps ahead of the flea. The click beetle, it seems, accelerates at 380 times the force of gravity when it has to flip over from flat on its back.

TRACK & FIELD

Philip Gee

A season which started quietly and somewhat disappointingly, with third places in our first two Southern League matches, gradually gathered momentum and we finished by winning the last four league matches. This effort placed us third out of the twentyfive clubs. In between these victories we also won the Brockman Trophy which was an extremely acceptable feather in our cap.

It was very noticeable that as the season progressed and our results improved that the team spirit (something that is often looked upon as a joke but which is nevertheless an essential ingredient of success) improved and with it the willingness of members to turn out and "double up" if necessary in our important matches. If this spirit and determination can be continued next year then there is no reason why we cannot resume where we finished and win the Southern League.

It would be difficult and indeed unfair to pick out anyone for special mention because our success was due to everyone who competed during the year. However, I would like to welcome back after periods of absence Jim Vivian and Charlie Gillett both of whom turned out regularly thereby adding strength to the team.

Finally an impassioned if somewhat hackneyed plea. It would be a great help to all those concerned in trying to field a team if all members who are notified of selection to compete in a meeting made an effort to turn up or at least to let their sectional secretary know if they are not available.. There is nothing more detrimental to team spirit than a member not turning up either due to careless oversight or the opinion that he need only compete if he feels like it.

Philip Gee

O'Hara wins world vets. title

SEPTEMBER 13th - 16th 1972 - COLOGNE

Showing his usual flair and a speedy finish, Laurie O'Hara demolished the field in the 5,000 metres final at the World Veteran's meeting in Cologne. Third, was Geoff Warren (Australia) who ran in the Vancouver Commonwealth Games of 1954, and fifth our own Clive Shippen.

It was unfortunate that the 10,000 metres and marathon should be held the same day as this diluted the field. There were at least six other runners who could have made the first six. None, however, would have matched Laurie who not only won his heat with ridiculous ease but two days previously won a 5,000 metres against Dave Power and Geoff Warren (Australia) and Bill Allen (Canada).

Clive ran one of his hardest races for many years when he finished 2nd in the 3,000 metres steeplechase on the second day of this five day meeting, splitting two Australians.

Our third Belgrave representative was John Bromley who travelled solo and camped out, all for the privilege of tackling the 10 Km road walk. He beat all the opposition he had reckoned on, but three Swedes appeared and took first three places. However, the medal awards were gold, silver and four bronze so Belgrave did well for medals.
