EDITORIAL

Dear Fellow Members, Although a little late, we take this opportunity of wishing all members a happy and successful new year. Doubtless many resolutions have already suffered their inevitable fate, but at least two should still be foremost in our minds. Firstly let us resolve to support our Club in as many events as possible, whether as competitors or officials and secondly,we must resolve to train harder, this applies especially to our youths and juniors. The amount of punishment a properly trained athlete can absorb is fantastic. If we remember that most fatigue is purely psychological, then perhaps we can drag ourselves out for that training session which, although dreaded, invariably becomes most enjoyable.

Certain members stand out as likely to perform exceptionally well in Belgrave colours this year, and among them is C. Thompson, a junior, whose improvement during the last year has been great to see. He has a fine future ahead of him provided he is able to distinguish between good and bad advice as regards his training and choice of events. It is a pity that so much harmful advice is often given to young athletes when their careers are in the infancy stage.

C. Thompson

Obituary · page 2

TED GOOD

A.A.H.

In our last issue we gave news of Ted Good, whose years with the Club extended over half a century. We now learn with sincere regret of his death; it is evident that he never fully recovered from his most serious illness of a few months ago. Ted's passing means the severing of yet another link with our early history and it is pleasing to note that he was so recently able to put into proper perspective, for many of us, the early days of the Belgrave Harriers and those who made up the Club in those days.

Our sincere sympathy goes to Ted's widow.

A.A.H.

Ted GoodA.A.H.

Obituary · page 3

George Still

A.A.H.

We mourn the passing of one who had been a familure figure in the Club dressing rooms and over our courses for sixty years and more. Many of us had our first run over Wimbledon Common with George, and listened to his reminiscences of races and athletics of years gone by with glowing interest.

George had his first race at the age of eighteen at the Putney Velodrome in the 1890's and he continued to run for the next sixty years. Latterly he had contented himself with his second love - swimming and diving; and death came to him on the 4th of October when, after swimming three lengths at the Baths, he collapsed and died at the age of 82 years.

In his heyday George was a member of the Highgate Harriers teams which dominated the Southern Cross Country Championships for a decade, and he was a member of their winning "National" teams in 1904 and 1905. He was a more than useful performer at any distance from 880 yards up to the Marathon, and was honoured by an invitation to compete at the Olympic Games, but this he had to decline for domestic reasons.

We shall remember George Still with affection as a great character and sportsman and as a link with our early days when there were cross country courses to be found in the Battersea neighbourhood.

A.A.H.

George StillA.A.H.

To George Still of the Belgrave Harriers

L. Fabre

I taught you how to swim, you taught me how to run,

What a clean life it was, for it was full of fun,

You started before me George and you never did pause,

But watch for me Georgie for I am very close,

And again full of fun,

We may meet for that final great run,

Farewell George, you were a great sport and I respected you.

L. Fabre, Belgrave Harrier, Age 70, still running. November, 1958.

LONDON TO BRIGHTON RELAY (SOUTHERN CLUBS) OCT.11TH

Eric Hall

The Brighton, I am informed, is almost always held in fine weather, and this year was no exception. October 11th. was the sort of day when a trip to the sea is just what Doctor Brighton would order. As I am a newcomer to this business of road-relays, I needed quite a lot of persuading to enter into the spirit of things. After much coaxing I was waiting at Big Ben for the first stroke of ten to set 20 lightly clad figures on their way. At last Big Ben warmed up and the chime rang out clearly and they were off. (Thought - what happens if there is a false start? Perhaps they go home and wait till next year).

That was that, I thought, as they disappeared out of sight but my chauffeur was already revving his car after a Le Mans type start, so I joined in the chase.

Down Millbank and across Battersea Bridge where Brian Hallowell was nicely placed in ninth place after a leisurely start. Then satisfied that Brian knew what he was doing, we sped on to Clapham Common for the first change-over. Brian had moved up to seventh and away went Charlie Walker. Portsmouth had a slight lead from Brighton and T.V.H. Charlie did well in the early part, but was being closely followed. However, on arrival at Mitcham he had moved up to sixth. We had dropped further behind the leaders and the gaps had begun to appear. Lewis(Woodford) and Pape (Portsmouth) were in contrast here and it was surprising to see Pape go away quite easily. Dave Baulch, usually on a later stage, was having a change of scenery this year but as he was surrounded by Internationals he not surprisingly had to relinquish two vital places. At Purley it was Clive Shippen's turn to take his medicine and at nineteen miles we took a rough time check; Portsmouth led Woodford by thirty seconds with Herne Hill moving up well seventeen seconds behind. Then came a long gap until T.V.H. and Walton followed by Aylesford and Essex Beagles with Clive chasing hard. We were now four minutes behind Portsmouth - not a very bright outlook.

When we eventually caught up with the runners again, (those motor driven coaches are a greater nuisance than the athletic type) we found Jim Masters in command. The manner in which he tackled the long climb through Redhill was ajoy to watch, he made short work of two of the intruders and we were back to sixth again.

John Davies had the shortest trip of the day (hardly worth the bother of changing John!) and at Salfords Woodford had taken over from Pompey, we had improved slightly and were now three minutes and twenty seconds in arrears; it was felt that this was still too large a gap.

The next man to show his paces was newcomer to the Brighton road, Mal Robinson. He had what I consider to be a very unpleasant stretch past Gatwick Airport; he held on to our position but unfortunately the gaps grew wider.

Our next glimpse of the race was after Crawley, and Portsmouth were back in the lead. Pat Newell was doing the first of the two long legs for us and he was being closely followed by Ilford, Polytechnic and Hampstead in a bunch which looked rather ominous. However, Pat didn't let us down and we held our position. Just outside Bolney we had a grand view of the race and Pompey led by over a minute from Woodford. Eric Gebbett was doing his utmost to hold on to our place but the fifth team, T.V.H., were over three minutes in front. Harry Hicks was on this leg for Hampstead and looked very dangerous but Eric refused to be panicked and towards the end was moving away slightly.

For us the race was now over, as the gaps were such that we required at least three top class three-milers; we could only hope that our remaining runners would hold our position. Charlie Dabbs had other ideas and put up the third fastest time for the tenth stage. He was relieved on the tricky Dale Hill stage by "veteran" Archie Bedford who put us well clear of the rest of the field. The last man, but by no means the least, was John Moore. Not for him a dramatic finish as for some previous Belgravians, nor the feeling of truimph, just the steady plod home unheralded save by the faithful supporters who had cheered their club-mates all the way from Mitcham.

Our sixth position entitles us to compete in the National next April, but as we finished six minutes and forty-two seconds in arrears I do not hold out much hope for a Belgrave victory. This year's race was not particularly fast and we lost an average of eight seconds every mile.

The supporters as always did a magnificent job of work, but only those with long years of service can remember a Belgrave win. Surely its about time they had another?

I am not a very senior member, but when I became a Belgravian in 1948 we were National Cross Country Champions. We had many fine runners and as now a great tradition. Of recent years we have slipped rapidly down hill, but every club has its ups and downs, so what about an all-round effort; some serious training, then and only then will the results follow.

Our walkers have proved that it can be done. Only a few years ago we weren't figuring in the results, so Jack Goswell thought it was about time we got back to our rightful position. Someone could do the same for the runners. All it requires is a large back and the ability not to take no for an answer. We have enough men capable of handling this job of welding the lads into a TEAM and thereby getting the results that the "old timers" achieved. This is not a new recipe, it is one which brought Belgrave fame in the past and will do the same again we hope in the near future.

Eric Hall.

MATCH AT DARTFORD - 18TH OCT.

This five-sided fixture involving Dartford H., City of Rochester A.C., Maidstone A.C., Kent A.C. and, of course, ourselves was the first event of the cross-country season, and following so closely on the London to Brighton relay was expected to be something of an anti-climax. Also, with our forces split between the Walton "10" and the organisation of our own open Youths road relay, a strong turn-out was not envisaged for such a distant fixture.

However, all predictions were proved wrong with a splendid turn-out of seventeen, but which included only three of the previous week's twelve Brighton men. Once again we illustrated our great depth by winning both the five-a-side and eight-a-side matches. Owing to the uneven apportionment of Juniors in the five clubs it was decided to run both grades in one senior scoring race over 5½ miles.

Despite the brisk start up a half-mile incline, John Moore wasn't satisfied with the pace and, accompanied by Bob Glover and J. Lyne of S.L.H.(2nd claim Kent A.C.), forged ahead to establish a clear lead of thirty yards after 1¼ miles. Shortly afterwards he had the misfortune to go off course at a point which should clearly have had a marksman rather than a flag. By the time he had been called back and rejoined the race he had dropped to 25th place. It says much for his determination and ever improving form that he was able to fight his way back to fourth place at the finish.

Approaching the end of the first lap Miller, Thompson, Glover, Bedford and Shippen were in close attendance around tenth position with John Moore moving through fast after his mishap. Morrison of Dartford and Ellam of Rochester were sharing a lead of 75 yds.

Ellam eventually pulled away to win by eighteen seconds in a new course record time of 26.12. Shippen moved through well over the last mile and he was closely followed by Archie Bedford who had run steadily throughout. Bob Glover found out too late that one mustn't underestimate the finishing speed of those behind, especially when they include an accomplished half miler like Gerald Batty of Rochester!

With Miller, Thompson and Bob Taylor backing up very closely Belgrave were assured of the team race. One of our recent recruits, John Collier, ran well in his first race for the Club and should soon become a more than useful runner.

If the remainder of the winter fixtures are supported as well as this one, then Belgrave is indeed on the road back to their all-conquering past in cross-country running.

The first open race of the season must inevitably cause some anxiety as to how our teams have weathered and how they are likely to fare in subsequent championships.

CHELMSFORD ROAD RELAY 25TH OCT.

This was our first visit to Chelmsford since 1952 when we won the magnificent trophy for the second successive year. Unfortunately, the date now clashes with our five-mile cross-country handicap, but surely six of our best men can be spared each year to at least "show face" at such an important event; and so it was.

Our six were Bob Glover, Malcolm Robinson, Alan Black, Eric Gebbett, Charlie Walker and Dave Baulch; three relative newcomers to road relay racing and three "veterans".

Fifty-six teams started and it was soon apparent that many clubs treat this race as their event of the year. It was good to see Bob Glover turning out for us in this type of event, and his running justified the confidence many have in him. After a mile he was still amongst the leaders and a little later began to force the pace on Lewis(Woodford), Stacey(T.V.H.) and Cornell(Chelmsford). However, he was unable to maintain this speed and finished in 12th position.

Malcolm Robinson set off in great style for lap 2 and after about two miles had passed no less than seven men, being only ten seconds behind fourth place. He continued to narrow the gap before he in turn was caught and passed, after quite a battle, by Haith of T.V.H. Malcolm returned our fastest time of the day - 15.24.

Young Alan Black, undeterred by the star names around him, ran a very sensible 3rd stage, dropping places only to Sando and O'Gorman, each of whom broke the course record. White took Woodford into a lead here which they kept to the end, while Bert Ingles drew away from Alan to increase the Valley's lead over us to 29s.

Eric Gebbett took over in eighth position with three men in close attendance and promptly showed how well he runs in company by shaking off all three and gaining two further places into the bargain. All this over a distance which is little more than a sprint to Eric. J. Haslam, with a time only seven seconds faster than Eric's took T.V.H. into third place, five places ahead of the Bels who conceded a further place to Chelmsford.

Charlie Walker set off on leg 5 and soon passed Chelmsford to regain sixth place but was eventually overtaken by Mayatt of Aylesford and Irons of Victoria Park. This was not the Charlie of old, although his time was faster than many recorded in 1952 when 15 minutes had yet to be beaten. However, when he handed over to Dave Baulch for the final leg we were still within 14 sec. of sixth place. Unfortunately Dave was nowhere near his inspired best and we had to be content with our eighth position.

As a point of interest, our three experienced runners recorded an aggregate time of 46.45 while the inexperienced trio totalled 46.34! - the shape of things to come?

Of the 320-odd runners, thirteen bettered 15 mins.

CAMBRIDGE H. 7 MILES OPEN WALK. DEC.6TH

Random notes of an Idle Fellow

The cold and cheerless afternoon afforded an excellent excuse for turning up at Eltham and exclaiming "I am resting", as a reason for standing on the side-line and offering to all and sundry, "free of charge", words of encouragement, advice or derision, according to my fancy.

It was a wonderful change from ploughing my usual weary way, to achieve, after much sweat, effort and banter, some unmentionable time. The only reward being the tussle with "so and so" and the grand comradeship of those who "walk the roads".

What were my impressions of this band of brothers who wear the Claret and Gold with such distinction? If pride be the prerogative of bantam cocks, then I was a very proud bantam cock when battle was joined and our contingent, led by that superlative walker Stan Vickers, flay the enemy at every level

Space is too limited to more than just refer to the tenacity and pugnaciousness of Eric Hall, the daintiness of George Williams which camoufleges no ordinary resoluteness, the strength of Ray Middleton who must be a future champion, the potential powers of Terry Clark and Brian Cornick, and so through the team to our last man, Percy Wilson, who so typifies the fun and enjoyment obtained from the sport.

The ten prizes awarded to our men is a measure of their success and a study of the following results will clearly show how they dominated the proceedings.

In conclusion, I would emphasize the amount of fun and enjoyment that can be obtained from watching these athletic competitions and ask you, when free, to support the current fixture, be it running or walking. It will be a great encouragement to the competitors concerned and the effort will be well repaid by the comradeship obtained from a greater knowledge of your fellow members.

Here and There

Many members will remember Ken England, a rather tough determined cross-country runner of a few years back. Ginger Kennedy writes to tell us that Ken is a very ill man, now hardly able to walk due to disseminated schlerosis. We feel sure that all readers will join us in wishing him a speedy recovery.

They used to breed 'em tough in the old days, and the brothers Joe and Len Coleman were no exception. None of your fancy 9 to 5 hours for them; in fact at well past midnight both could have been found still hard at work in their respective trades, printing and catering. But training had to be fitted in and as both were walkers this was carried out by a brisk walk home from work at about 2 a.m. Needless to say they would both walk back to work the next morning. Can anyone beat this?

Len Herbert is apparently still running several times a week but says he has lost the urge to race. His brother Eric is now married to a Dutch girl and he has now fully recovered from his serious climbing accident.

Jack Brown writes to say how really difficult life has become in Cyprus; the strain on everyone is intense and being practically confined to their house Jack and his wife Betty and son Malcolm find their greatest enjoyment is derived from their record player. We hope it won't be long before commonsense prevails, bringing a return to normal conditions in Cyprus and that before long Jack will be back to don his Claret and Gold vest again to good account.

Letter · page 11

SINGLENESS OF PURPOSE

Harry Shields

Dear Fellow Members,

I wonder how many of us, with the approach of "Spring Cleaning Season", suddenly become filled with a desire to have a clear out of our accumulated momentoes aquired over the ages? How many of us start, only to get bogged down in browsing over those relics of the past?

Such was my fate when, a little prematurely perhaps, it was forced on me that, if domestic peace was to be maintained, then a major surgical operation was required on my heaped up store of minute books, nomination forms, programmes and all the other impedimenta gathered in club and association activities. Many papers recalling "life and death" contentions of previous years were ruthlessly destroyed, but I could not help dwelling on the three page "Schedule of training for Southern Counties and English Cross-Country Championships 1934"

It commences," In the past the cross-country members of our Club have entered the New Year without any definite idea as regards training for any particular race other than running twice or three times a week as members of a pack or otherwise" Then follows a summary of improvement which should arise from systematic training. A list of eleven exercises is tabulated, to be carried out every morning. These include dumbell exercises and hints on general hygiene and diet.

The week by week training schedule is graduated until immediately previous to the Southern Championships; it reads:-

Monday - 3 miles in 18 mins. Tuesday - 7 " " 37 " Wednesday - 3 " " 18 " Thursday - 3 " " 18 " Friday - Complete rest except for exercises. Saturday - 12 miles in 65 mins. Sunday - Stroll about 20 miles

In addition, every morning, a stroll part way to work is recommended.

Even in the light of modern experience it is an extraordinarily far-sighted document and of its benefits the performances of those who were influenced by it are perhaps the best yardstick.

You will perhaps excuse my nostalgic reviews of the past, but at Parliament Hill recently on the occasion of the Inter-Counties Championship there was not one single member selected to represent his County. It appeared particularly incredible when I thought of the ability of many of our athletes only to remember how quickly they break off at a tangent for some "out of season event". Such actions seem almost escapist from the realities of the principle targets.

This inevitably leads to the question, " what are the targets?"

The answer to this must be, "The Championships sponsored by the governing bodies at the particular time of the year". These Championships must be given prime consideration if the maximum benefit is to be obtained from our great sport.

I appreciate that taking lessons from the past is open to criticism but the conclusion is inescapeable that singleness of purpose is absolutely essential if the greatest success as an individual or as a club is to be achieved.

Is it not worthwhile to discuss your training ideas with your section leader with a view to formulating a schedule for modern needs? For my part the influence of these early schedules on a very varied athletic career has been considerable.

In conclusion, the best of luck and although the way to success may be difficult and weary, do not be diverted by the glitter of side-shows and above all ensure that there is thought and direction in all that you do.

Sincerely yours. Harry Shields

Harry Shields

Report · page 14

GAY PAREE

Eric Hall

A 10,000 metres walk was included in the Great Britain versus France match in Paris on the 13/14th. Sept. Stan Vickers and myself were lucky enough to get the trip.

Behind that bald statement lies a wealth of incident which could fill this magazine for the next few issues.

We arrived in Paris late Friday evening and found our quarters in the National Institute des Sports at Vincennes, some six miles from the centre of Paris. No doubt this was specially selected by the B.A.A.B.

The quarters themselves were comfortable but the meals were of microscopic proportions. After what was by even continental standards, a very meagre breakfast a few of us adjourned to the nearest market to stock up for the rest of the stay. Stan was appointed C.[illegible]. stores and the rest of us supplied the money and the lingo. After a short lesson on French Stan was pushed into a shop and asked to buy some honey. In less time than it takes Peter Radford to run 100 metres he was back, protesting,"I can't understand a word she's saying". Apparently the poor girl didn't speak Hindustani! Stan's next attempt was very much better, he wanted some grapefruit, so after yelling it in French for some moments he was 'bout to give up when the girl turned on a dazzling smile and said, in perfect English,"Ah, you would like some grapefruit". That was the last we heard of Stan's French!

Saturday afternoon was very warm, so after taking part in the march-past we decided to "do" the town. Only 5000 people were at the stadium so we didn't want to be conspicuous. Somehow or other we made our way to the Arc de Triomphe and there we made an error of judgement when someone suggested crossing the road, the person concerned being a sprinter- naturally! After a couple of attempts and some"lifting" by Stan and myself we made it.

Stan was most disappointed as nearly everyone was American so he couldn't practise his French. The stadium was some 12 miles from our quarters so we were quite pleased with ourselves when we got safely back. This is a feat which Jack Goswell couldn't do in London, let alone Paris.

Sunday was one of the hottest days of the year and in the morning we basked in the sun (that all too rare thing this summer). Stan and I duly performed in the afternoon and of course our European Gold-Medallist romped home in 47m.3s. with myself second, after some very anxious moments, in 48m.44s. Attane of France was third in 48m.57s. Not world shattering times but just good enough. Once again we forgot about the match in which, incidently, the walk did not count, and moved smartly to the Buffet- Bar to quench a hard earned thirst. On our return to the stands we found that while we had been emptying bottles, Stan Eldon had been doing the same to the steeplechase pit, much to the amusement of the 5000 staunch supporters who had turned up for the second day.

The match itself produced some very fine running, but it was a very one-sided affair, with England winning all the track events and finishing up with nearly twice as many points as the French. As L'Equipe pointed out the next day, our overall supremacy was equalled by our victory in the final event, the 4 x 400 metres relay, which we won by eight seconds.

As soon as the meeting was over we dashed back for a meal and were then taken to the Folies Bergere. I will not attempt to describe the various acts but leave it to what I feel sure are very adequate imaginations! We left the show at midnight and made for the Institute via several cafes and didn't finally arrive back until around 3 a.m. - much too late for bed!

Much could still be written of Stan's adventures - his near arrest at the Folies, his missing the way home after volunteering to act as guide to three female members of the team - it only took him two hours to do two miles that night! It wouldn't really be fair to blacken his character any more. He probably had the last laugh on me next morning. Before we left London someone had most unkindly told Stan that it was my birthday on the Monday. Stan remembered this at the most inopportune moment and so at Le Bourget a full choir of British athletes sang "Happy Birthday", it was much worse than the Boeing 707 warming up outside!

Eric Hall

A QUESTION OF AGE

A "Not so old 'un"

Having heard a number of comments recently regarding our older and less active members, with suggestions that we might be better off without some of them, leads me to wonder if these critics realise how much the Club owes to these enthusiastic supporters who, year after year, and without any hope of personal gain, continue to subscribe regularly to help provide facilities for the younger members, which, without such support would not be available.

A study of the Club Handbook will show that we have 43 Championship trophies, valued, according to the Balance, at £1200. These have, in the main, been provided by or in association with, some of the "old" members to encourage competition among the younger ones.

The financing of these Championships on track, field and road and also over the country, is only one of the many large items of expenditure which need the support of far more than the hundred or so active athletes who take part in them.

Since Amateur Athletics remains one of the few things which are not State aided let us be grateful to those who, because of their affection for the name of Belgrave and in return for the pleasure they have gained in the past, continue to give their support wholeheartedly, generously and in a spirit of comradeship.

A "Not so old 'un"
