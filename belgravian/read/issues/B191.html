Editorial · page 3

BILL COUZENS, President

The questionnaire forwarded with the last Belgravian resulted in a substantial majority of club members in favour of a Ladies Section. After lengthy discussions, and bearing this result in mind, the committee decided that a Ladies Section should be formed within Belgrave Harriers taking effect from 1st July 1976.

This decision was not taken lightly and when viewed objectively without the sentiment of our all male heritage there is no doubt that the future of club athletics lies in mixed clubs. This is endorsed by the attitude of local authorities who feel far more sympathetic to financially assisting mixed clubs in any immediate projects such as, in our case, upgrading Belgrave Hall and future ideas of a track headquarters of our own and the best possible track facilities.

The financial aspect, however, was only a small issue compared with the general belief that ladies in Belgrave will assist in the recruitment of athletes, especially the higher teenage group who will be needed for future senior teams, therefore the hope of recruiting a ready made good senior to strengthen our teams would only be a bonus and not a necessity.

As for the ladies, the intention is not to have a second rate Ladies Section but to build a strong section that will cater for all aspects of womens athletics with the future view of overall club strength.

You may wonder how this can be achieved, where will the ladies officials, coaches and athletes come from. In this respect I can report that negotiations are going on between ourselves and Selsonia Ladies Athletic Club with a view to their incorporation within Belgrave Harriers.

I might add that should our hope for a local authority grant fall through, or should our negotiations with our friends Selsonia fail, it will only make us more determined that our Ladies Section will succeed.

Ladies, welcome!

BILL COUZENS, President

Our total membership as at December 31st was 496, made up of 289 seniors, 27 juniors, 28 youths, 100 boys, 37 Honorary/Associate members and 15 2nd-Claim members.

CHARLIE GILLETT, one of our leading track men, continues to grow more popular on Radio London. His rock 'n' roll programme 'Honky Tonk' is now extended from 11.30am till 1.00pm on Sundays and on Friday nights at 10pm he now presents a review of the best popular music of the week with 'Singles File'.

It is a coincidence that BILL COUZENS and his wife GWEN share the same birthday - but even more remarkable is the fact that this date fell on the very day of the Club Dinner when Bill was installed as Club President.

The 88th Annual Dinner was again a night to remember and ran so smoothly that one would not have guessed that the organisation of the function had passed from TED STIMPSON (who has done it so well for around ten years) to BILL LAWS for the first time. The new format of dance band until midnight and then disco through until 2.00 am was greatly enjoyed by all. Bill thanks all those who helped him in this his first year - in particular GORDON BISCOE.

The jazz evening organised by GORDON was another great success and KEN COLYER and his band enjoyed themselves as much as the revellers. Instead of halting the festivities at midnight, the band played on for an extra hour.

If you finished further down the field than 11th in the Club 10 mile cross country championship you will not thank the 'Grapevine' authors for reminding you that DENNIS JONES has been a grandfather for the past 18 months! Of course that makes BILL JONES a great-grandfather - doesn't seem possible does it.

The A.A.A. General Committee have amended the rule regarding the wearing of club vests. From January 1st 1977 club colours MUST be worn by competitors in all open team and relay competitions unless the referee gives his permission for a change to be made. Colours will be registered during 1976 and of course ours will go on the books as 'Claret with Gold Hoop'. So if you bought your last club vest in the early fifties and it is now one of the pale pink variety with off-white hoop, faded after a quarter of a century of action on the roads, tracks and country of Britain, get a new one now. TOM CARTER ([phone number withheld]) is sure to have one in your size, aswell as shorts and track suits all in club colours.

As the 1976 Belgrave 20 miles road race will be the 25th such promotion, it is race secretary DEREK CROOKES' intention to make it a bumper affair and have prizes for the first 25 runners to finish. So if you have any unwanted Christmas presents or prizes that you would like to donate, please contact Derek ([phone number withheld]). He did mention this in the last magazine and of course was overwhelmed with the response - in fact he only has about 23 prizes to find now!

JOHN DUDMAN only appeared on the Belgrave scene just over a year ago but has soon become a popular member of the Duke's Joggers. He is off now for a spell in Australia but hopes to return before the end of the summer.

The JAGUARS must be one of the best semi-pro rock groups in London and when they visit Belgrave Hall we are always pleased to see and hear them. Their last appearance on February 7th surely was the best yet. Tickets were sold out during the preceding week and on the night 200 Belgravians and their friends packed themselves into the Hall and danced with complete abandon until midnight. Over £120 worth of beer was downed and the crowd ripped like locusts through the refreshments, supplied by CARL and BARBARA LAWTON and RICHARD TANNER. Our thanks to MARTIN BEST for providing his 'Shambles Disco' during the interval; to the barmen, bless 'em; and of course to COLIN PEARSON for getting it all together.

RON WYLD has been doing some calculating and reckons that the space gained by the Club in the national press during the last couple of months regarding the Boys 50 x 1 mile relay and the Club's donation towards the expenses of JOHN BICOURT's training trip across the Atlantic, would have cost any advertiser around £6,000. Apart from this there was coverage on Radio London and on radio and TV in Liverpool.

Looking forward to that great feast of athletics this summer, the Olympic Games, you may like to be reminded of the actual dates of the athletic competitions:- July 23rd to 26th and 28th to 31st.

RANJIT BHATIA who ran regularly for us some 15 years ago before returning to India, will be visiting the United Kingdom this summer. We look forward to renewing our acquaintance with him and in the meantime Ranjit sends his best wishes to all Belgravians.

Two former members of winning 'National' C.C. teams have been to our fixtures. On Boxing Day we had DICK TOBIN eyeing the field and FRANK ABBERTON was at the Surrey C.C. Championships.

It is learned that the Belgrave team of arena stewards has been asked to perform again this year at the Crystal Palace.

Our veteran veteran runner DUNCAN MacLEAN was recently seen on television on a programme devoted to health and fitness in old age. We saw this remarkable nonagenarian running, dancing, singing and talking. He attributed his vitality to having a very energetic mother, and told the interviewer that he hopes to run a lap at the Palace on his hundredth birthday!

FURTHER HONOURS FOR ED. LACEY

FURTHER HONOURS FOR ED. LACEY

An exhibition of sports photography, the work of our own ED. LACEY, was held recently in the Kodak building in High Holborn. Ed. is now acknowledged to be one of the world's leading sports photographers and his pictures are held in enormous esteem by sportsmen, sports fans - and his fellow photographers. It was just twelve years ago that Ed - then in his mid forties - gave up his "safe" job with Cable and Wireless Ltd to become a freelance photographer. From then on he doggedly pursued his chosen career - on many occasions bluffing his way into arenas and sporting events, including the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, to get the pictures he wanted. These days as an honoured member of his profession, such desperate tactics are no longer necessary, but the single-mindedness and determination are as much in evidence as when he first became an eager freelance. His work is particularly sought after for its dramatic composition and his ability to catch the climax of any event.

THE HAIG & HAIG CUP

Clive Shippen writes

Belgrave Harriers' annual dinner is always a very special occasion, but the 88th event held at the Worcester Hotel in November last was of extra-special significance. It was on that evening that Belgrave's first ever trophy, the HAIG & HAIG CHALLENGE CUP, reappeared in public and, in addition a renewed association was made with the whisky company itself, now John Haig & Company Limited.

It was back in 1897 that the first association began. The Club had been founded only 10 years earlier and had recently moved from its first headquarters at "The Kings Arms", Ranelagh Road, Pimlico, to the "William the Fourth" in Grosvenor Road. This was the start of a pub-crawl which ended 24 years later at "The Swan" near our present headquarters. But it was not unusual for sports clubs to be attached to public houses in those days; there were very few sports facilities in Victorian times and public houses were popular meeting places. The publican was no doubt very pleased to let out a room to an athletic club whose growing membership (70), plus many supporters, all made good custom - particularly after a run or walk! Boxing gloves and punch bags were kept in the club room and Belgrave Harriers' boxing section worked up a thirst on the premises while the runners and walkers were out on the road heading for Battersea Park and the sprinters were at Lillie Bridge athletic ground being coached by the international dash man, Harry Hutchings.

The boxing section did particularly well, eventually producing several leading amateurs, including Alex Lambert, A.B.A. Featherweight Champion in 1913.

It was in 1897 that George Ogilvie Haig, a vice-President of the Club, presented the HAIG & HAIG CHALLENGE CUP. This, our first ever trophy, was presented each year to the member scoring most points in club handicap events (nearly all races were run on the handicap system). This beautiful ornate solid silver cup was formerly a rowing trophy and portrayed a coxless four at full stretch. Apart from being manufactured in 1843 its history is unknown. Rowers, boxers and athletes were closely allied in those days and it was not unusual for trophies to be interchanged. The cup was additionally engraved for Belgrave and dated 1897.

The following year George Ogilvie Haig became our second president and had pleasure in presenting the trophy to the first winner, C.E. Lofts - especially pleasurable for Lofts as the award was accompanied by a case of the real stuff!

G.O. Haig held the Presidency for five years and was reckoned in his time to be a good sport. During his term of office the Club laid on a boxing show at the "William", but at the last minute the police got to hear of the unauthorised promotion and it was rapidly turned into a smoking concert. Fortunately the Bels then, as now, were well equipped with 'entertainers'!

In 1898, Sam Sherrington, the Club Captain, became the second winner of the trophy and by 1920 had run up two more victories, thus entitling him to retain the cup as his own. So, after only six years the HAIG & HAIG CHALLENGE CUP was lost to the club. It was another 8 years before the next trophy appeared - the Blackstaff Shield for the 7 miles cross-country championship of 1910.

Seventy-two years of Belgrave history passed by after Sam Sherrington's outright win of that long lost trophy. Seventy-two years which saw events that those early stalwarts could not have dreamed possible.....our own freehold headquarters; national champions at running and walking; the production of 10 international runners from just 12 cross-country 'Nationals'; Olympic, European and Commonwealth medals; and many many other achievements.

The HAIG hiatus ended in 1974 when Mr. Lionel Sherrington, Sam's son, contacted the Club to let us know that while those momentous years were rolling by the HAIG cup had been proudly displayed on the family sideboard. On being acquainted with the story behind the cup and its very special meaning to the Club, Mr. Sherrington most generously offered it back to us on the understanding that it would be well looked after. To ensure that this would be so our first ever trophy will be on display at each club dinner where it will be presented each year to the incoming president. A new plinth has been made which records the Cup's history, its return by Lionel Sherrington and its first new award to W. Couzens, our President.

The HAIG cup had found its way home again and the Club is most grateful to Mr. & Mrs. Sherrington for their generosity. One other thing needed to be done - the renewal of our association with the original donors, now incorporated in John Haig & Company Limited.

The arrival of the HAIG Cup in 1897 marked the beginning of an unrivalled half century of Club history. Let us hope that its re-appearance will signal the start of another era of achievement.

High jinks and high hopes at the 88th ANNUAL DINNER and DANCE

High jinks and high hopes at the 88th ANNUAL DINNER and DANCE

Last year, following ten years of stirling service Ted Stimpson finally stepped down from managing the Belgrave Annual Dinner and Dance and Bill Laws took over responsibility for organising this, Belgrave's major social event of the year. Your Committee believing that the format developed over preceeding years would be adhered to were startled to learn of Bill's plans for major changes including a disco and extending dancing past the witching hour to 2 a.m. Small groups could be seen huddled together in Belgrave Hall and it soon became clear that there was support both for the new and old formats. What to do when the tickets and menus were already printed? As usual the experience and wisdom of your management team prevailed and Bill's compromise proposal for dancing to the Florida Band until Midnight followed by a disco until 2 a.m. was widely acclaimed as combining the best of the new and old.

And so to the night. With tickets at £4 (the same price as 1974) a maximum number of 211 attended and what a night they had! A witty speech by our President ("the jumpers are jumping higher, the throwers throwing further, the walkers walking faster - and we're expecting the runners any minute now" - howls of derision from G. Biscoe and his cohorts on tables A B & C), insight into future plans and the possibility of a Ladies Section, ("the ladies would help Belgrave to lead out front" - who wrote your script Bill Couzens?) and generous undertakings from our guest. Frank Beckett, of the G.L.C. Parks Department, confided that Belgrave's home track at Battersea is next in line for the full treatment including tartan track, but naturally timing is doubtful in the present economic climate. Michael Henderson, Managing Director of the Haig Whisky Company presented Bill Couzens with the recently re-discovered Haig Cup which it's owner Lionel Sherrington has kindly re-donated to the club, some 90 years after its original presentation to Belgrave by the Haig Whisky Company. (In future the new Club President will be presented with this cup at each Annual Dinner & Dance). Michael Henderson was roundly applauded when he announced his Company's intention to finance the 1976 Belgrave Twelve Stage Relay which in the past has been used by the S.C.A.A.A. as a selection race for the National Championships. Other official guests welcomed to the Dinner & Dance were Mr. & Mrs. Frank Evans of the G.L.C. and Peter Barson of the Sports Council.

With the official ceremonies over the evening moved on to dancing to the Florida Band and then on to the raffle; and what a raffle! Twenty prizes totalling over £100 in value, the majority of which had been generously donated by members. Many thanks to all those who donated prizes but particular mention of Gerry North for the fantastic supply of sports goods, including footballs, shooting stick, various rackets and holdalls. Colin Pearson won one of Gerry's donations, a pewter drinking mug and many of the Sunday pack felt this was his only chance of winning one! A huge piece of rolled beef was eagerly snapped up by a certain city jobber who shall remain nameless but Maggie Thatcher's advice seems to be getting home. Things must be tough! The draw was undertaken by 'Emu' Lawton ably assisted by Charlie Manning in a large box - later seen also to include a lady who also shall remain nameless. Half an hour in that box and Charlies dancing seemed to go to pot!

At twelve o'clock the traditional circle, Auld Lang Syne and cheers for the President. The Florida Band disappeared, the bar stayed open and ten minutes later Peter Gurd sprang to life on the disco. And wonders to behold, no-one left. 211 people stayed on and had a whale of a time. There is one sight the writer, well lubricated by this time, will remember for a long time - Bill Merrimen doing his thing, a kind of combination of shimmy, palais glide, charleston with a splash of rock. The younger members were overawed; Bill revelling in the admiration proceeded to dish out his complete repertoire. His taxi had to wait for over an hour outside the Worcester Hotel until he was finally dragged away protesting, by a very patient wife, into the darkness.

And after it was all over we sought reactions!

An unqualified success .......... Chas. Manning Bloody splendid! .......... Tom Carter I only liked the first half, I can't remember the second.... Colin Pearson What dinner? .......... Brian Gorman

BELGRAVE MARATHON CHAMPIONSHIP

Gordon Biscoe, Cross Country and Road Running Secretary

ROAD AND COUNTRY

BELGRAVE MARATHON CHAMPIONSHIP

With the regrettable demise of the Poly. Marathon it has become increasingly awkward each year to designate a suitable and convenient marathon for the purpose of the Belgrave Championship.

For example this year the AAA Marathon is being held in Rotherham on May 8th whilst the RRC Marathon (our choice last year) falls only two weeks before our own 20 miles road race.

With a view to seeking a solution opinions were solicited from a selection of those most likely to be involved, with the expected result that you get as many opinions as people asked but no overall favourite. However, as an outcome from one suggestion made, the Committee have now agreed that this year as a trial case the Club Championship will be awarded to the Club Member achieving the fastest time in any marathon officially recognised by the Road Runners Club, within the U.K. and during the period 1st April to 31st October. Place medals will be awarded in a similar fashion.

So road runners, the choice of race is yours and the'spin off' from such a scheme is that you can have more than one crack at the championship, although it is not anticipated that many will be intending to run a marathon every other week - apart from Lionel Mann that is.

If you do have any other queries or comments (constructive please!) please let me know, but it is hoped that this will prove to be a practical solution to the problem.

Gordon Biscoe Cross Country and Road Running Secretary.

An unfortunate clash with the Mitcham 25km road race resulted in this year's Club '10' being a little short on 'star performers'. Bicourt's Bid

As many members will already know, John Bicourt has taken unpaid leave from his teaching post from the end of February through to the Olympics in a do or die effort to prepare in a "professional manner".

He will spend six weeks in Mexico City training at high altitude with the Polish steeplechasers, at the invitation of one of the favourites for the event in Montreal, Bronislaw Malinowski, and following this he will move on to the United States to stay with Olympic Marathon champion Frank Shorter. By that time John intends being racing fit and will compete in the 'chase at the Kansas Relays, Texas Relays and Drake Relays. He will then return to Britain with the aim of making the international team v. East Germany.

The British Olympic Trials will be held on the first two weekends in June and by the time they are over, if all has gone to plan, John will hold a best time for the season of well under 8:30. The Olympic Qualifying Standard is 8:32 but it is not John's intention to qualify with the minimum required and if fate is with him he will be around 8:20 by the time the Games come along.

John broke through to international level on a steady 70 miles per week and this has got him below 8:30 but he has found that he cannot handle any more mileage than this AND carry out his duties as a housemaster and head of two departments at school. 100 miles per week including sessions of 10 x 1,000 metres over barriers requires extra sleep and the avoidance of the tensions and frustrations of school life. John has also included "hill work" this winter for the first time since his junior days, in an effort to drag out that extra speed needed during the last two laps of a race - his weakest point. So many times in the past he has felt good as the race finish approaches but has found it impossible to accelerate.

Last season was intended to be low key with no steeplechases but urgent requests from the British Board had him turning out in spite of the fact that he had made his intentions clear beforehand. Nevertheless, without any preparation over the barriers he reached 8:33.2 and was rated 4th in the country at the end of the season.

Of course anything could happen and with rivals of the calibre of Davies, Staynings and Coates he will have to be in top form to make the team but he has plenty of determination. In the Munich Olympics he went out as 50th best performer in a field of 51 yet came away 22nd fastest.

John says: "At 30 years of age this is almost certainly my last chance at the Olympics and I intend to make it coincide with the realisation of the absolute maximum of my potential."

Bold words and bold plans by John Bicourt; but the Belgrave Committee too have taken a bold, controversial and exciting decision in backing up their top runner and donating £250 towards the £600 - £800 it is going to cost him in training expenses this summer.

LOCATION: RICHMOND PARK TIME: ONE BLEAK SUNDAY MORNING

WALKING

A new dawn on the horizon and a new challenge. London Pride

FEBRUARY 14th 1976 - BATTERSEA PARK - G.L.C. 10 MILES CHAMPIONSHIP.

The former London County Council encouraged sport to the extent of promoting championships which included a Ten Miles Road Walk. When the authority was enlarged to become the Greater London Council the championships were continued and anybody who lives or works within their boundaries can compete. The walk has attracted leading walkers and clubs and we have in the past provided both individual and team winners. This year's event was held in Battersea Park on the 14th February, a dry but cold day, the course being five laps of the park with the first two laps lengthened.

Right from the start Carl Lawton led the way from Paul Nihill (Croydon H.) with our other men well placed. We suffered an early mishap when Tim Thorogood came under the judge's ban. As the laps went by the leaders showed their class by pulling away from the rest but it was good to see our lads consolidating their positions. Over the last lap Carl powered his way to a fine win in 71 mins. 50 secs. - a personal best and a time few have beaten. His fine walking drew praise from all who were there.

With individual honours decided interest became focused on the team title. Soon to arrive was Richard Tanner who has established himself as a regular scorer in our teams. John Hall gave us another solid performance (it must be the Brighton air!). Knowing that Ray Middleton was somewhere around we didn't have to wait long before our long-distance champion obliged to make us the first club with four men in. The points tally proved us winners.

Of our others mention must be made of Ron Day who hasn't been walking a year but is developing a good sound style and improves every time out - proof of regular, dedicated and conscientious training. Our Newham A.C. member, Mike Scammell is another who is forcing his way to the front and will soon be a name to be reckoned with.

So, with individual and team wins in this event Belgravians came away from Battersea Park proud to have collected a double victory in this London Championship.

'LETTER FROM CANADA'

John 'Alistair Cook' Thresher

'LETTER FROM CANADA'

by John 'Alistair Cook' Thresher

As we roll forward into another year, it seems an appropriate time to record some details of our life in the first eight months in a new country and to give some early impressions of the different life-style.

We left London Airport on April 21st on a 747 Jumbo en route for Regina, in the heart of the Canadian Prairies. Our first stop was Montreal where we had planned to spend a day or two and collect our car, which we had despatched from London some five weeks earlier. However, Quebec and Montreal, as every Olympic fan will now know, have bad strike records. A longshoremens' strike ensured that our car could not be landed for an indeterminate period. It was necessary to replan the next 1,800 miles of our journey. Like the pioneers of less than 100 years ago, we decided to go by rail, on the Canadian Pacific Railway.

Montreal is an exciting city. The old French quarter intrigued us particularly, with its historic old churches and official buildings and superb back-street restaurants so reminiscent of Paris. The modern buildings and underground shopping complexes are also very impressive.

We travelled by rail to Toronto and stopped for two nights. It is a clean, bustling more modern city, dominated by the large bank buildings in the downtown area. We took a coach to Niagara Falls and were duly impressed by the incredible, awesome power of the water as we stood so close to the edge and clung on to the children for dear life! We met Derek Fernee briefly, who seems to have settled in well in Toronto and is enjoying life.

The rail journey across Canada was an experience not to be missed. We had not realised before that the Canadian Pacific Railway had linked British Columbia in the West and the older parts of the East less than 100 years ago. Between Winnipeg and Vancouver lay 1,000 miles of grass land with the impenetrable Rocky Mountains beyond. The journey took 36 hours, but we were well looked after by the Dining car attendant (an ex-Blackpool fellow), and we had very comfortable accommodation. There was an observation car which was the favourite spot as we skirted the northern shore of Lake Superior, still covered in the remnants of the winter ice.

We arrived in Regina at 5.30 a.m. on a wet Monday morning. I have since heard of a fellow who did the same and immediately re-boarded the train and didn't get out until Calgary, where he has lived ever since! Our furniture hadn't arrived and so we had to live in a hotel for a few days. Our house was ready, but empty, so like true pioneers we borrowed and hired necessities and decided to move in. Although it was then May, spring had scarcely begun and the temperature was slow to rise. I was soon out running through the park and round the lake which was flooded by the snow-melt. I was still being troubled by a sore tendon which had been aggravated by a ferocious bare-footed sprint down the Ridgway, S.W.20. against a certain Belgrave Olympian (did I say bare-footed?!!!).

The first of many new friends we have made were an English couple from Feltham who have been here since 1965. Joe Turnham was a very useful half-miler and is the same age as myself. He even dug up a picture of Mitcham Youth's road relay in 1958 with us both fighting out the first leg!

I ran in a 10 mile road race (my first ever) in 100°F in early July. I was desperately unfit and I really thought I was never going to make the finish for third and a silver pot (62 minutes, would you believe!). Since then I have run a few miles more and got reasonably fit by running most lunch times around the lake with scores of middle-aged joggers. The enthusiasm is incredible, the standards are abysmal - it suits me fine!

Regina hosted the Western Canada Summer Games on its new all-weather Chevron track in August and it was the first time the public had seen a big track and field meeting. There were no outstanding performances by English standards, except by Diane Jones, the pentathlete, who could be a medal contender at the Olympics. I met Derek Boosey there, now a Canadian National Jumps coach. He is resident in Quebec but travels a great deal.

I raced in September in Saskatoon in a 5 mile cross-country race and finished 21st out of 50 or so. This time the standard was much higher. Then, in October, I drove 500 miles to Prince Albert and back to run in the Saskatchewan cross-country championships. The temptation was a place in the first seven and a trip to the National in Vancouver. I finished a close-up 11th but was a little handicapped by not wearing spikes and as the race was run in five inches of snow and -2° C, this didn't help! Then the weather improved and I was running my 10 miles on Sunday morning in mid-November in shorts.

The weather has been generally very good by Saskatchewan standards and I have kept running outdoors despite temperatures of -25° C and - 30° C (yes, that's 50-60° of frost!). Many days, however, have been only just below freezing and the air here is so dry it almost feels warm.

My latest craze is cross-country skiing. I now make excuses not to run and go out on the ski-trails instead. It's very hard work if you attack it instead of cruising along, but as I become more proficient I can glide more and push less. I am hoping to be able to go on all-day excursions next winter, together with several other enthusiasts, with rucksacks on our backs.

Mavis, Hannah and Neil seem to have adapted to the way of life, which admittedly is more comfortable and relaxing. We have more time together as a family and, talking of families, we are expecting an increase in June!

My job has proved to be very interesting and rewarding. I have travelled extensively to Victoria, Vancouver, Calgary, Edmonton, Winnipeg, Ottawa, Toronto and Thunder Bay, sometimes by light 'plane or float 'plane.). The economy is very healthy out in the West, principally due to the abundant natural resources - wheat, oil, gas, potash and many other minerals and crops. Saskatchewan has tremendous potential yet to be realised and unemployment and inflation are very low by world standards. The population is under one million in a geographical area much larger than the U.K. The forests and lakes in the north are almost uninhabited, with no real roads. The fishing and hunting are unbelievable, as I found out on a thrilling fishing trip last year.

Our summer weekends we spend at the "beaches" - unspoilt lakes with swimming from sandy beaches and fishing and boating facilities, all within a short car journey. We barbecue steaks by the shore in amongst the trees and this year we are going to invest in a large tent and canoe.

Of course, we go through periods of nostalgia for family, friends, the English scenery, Chipstead, Wimbledon, Young's bitter, Belgrave Hall and Lauriston Cottage, The "Rawalpindi", Match of the Day, Fulham F.C. (I must stop before tears stain the page)! We hope to be back for a holiday in 1977 and to make regular visits in future. We would love to hear from anyone who has time to write and, better still, we would love to be visited. I shall be at the Olympics in Montreal this year and I hope to meet John Bicourt there. If there are any others who are going to make the trip, please let me know.

John Thresher and family.

Regina, Saskatchewan. January, 1976.

SPOTLIGHT on JOHN DAVIES

Alan Mead

SPOTLIGHT on JOHN DAVIES

The crowd at Dublin's Lansdowne Road rugby stadium buzzed with anticipation. It was July 24th, 1957 and in sunny conditions over 28,000 had gathered to watch some of the great milers of the day in action and who knows, maybe witness a four minute mile run in Ireland. The excitable Irish brogue of Billy Morton, the meeting's famous organiser, came over the public address system further increasing the tension: "Will you all be coming out now for Billy Morton's Mile."

Cheering shook the stadium as the competitors assembled for the start, amongst them Brian Hewson - Britain's future European 1,500M champion, Gordon Pirie - silver medallist in the previous year's Olympic 5,000M in Melbourne and world record holder over 3,000M, Ron Delaney of Eire - the Olympic 1,500M champion, and John Davies of Belgrave Harriers representing the A.A.A.

Unfortunately the race was to take place on a spongy grass track so it was really expecting too much for anyone to beat four minutes but nevertheless as the gun was fired the Belgravian and John Cameron, also representing the A.A.A. went to the front and taking turns in the lead, they hauled the field round for the half distance in two minutes flat. Inevitably the pace slowed as the soft surface took its toll but again the crowd roared mightily as the high stepping Delaney, wearing the emerald green singlet of Eire went to the front and held on to the tape in 4:09.7. Hewson placed 2nd, a shade behind, with Pirie 3rd. Our man was not placed but once again had made his presence felt in a top class field.

John was born on 25th July, 1931 and has spent the great majority of his life living in Pimlico, the heart of Belgrave country. He went to St. Peter's School in Lower Belgrave Street but unlike today's schoolboys, the pupils were not able to take part in any organised sport at that time due to the war and the constant threat of bombing raids. At 14 years of age he left school and went into the building trade to train as an estimator.

Football now began to play a very important part in John's life. In the next ten years he progressed to playing for Wimbledon and Kingstonian and also spent a year on Tottenham Hotspur's amateur books. At 23, whilst playing inside forward for Kingstonian in February 1955, he received a severe injury to his right thigh that was to change his life. The injury necessitated a six week spell with his leg heavily bandaged, and by April when the bandaging was removed, the muscles of his leg had begun to wither. To build them up again he began to run regularly at Battersea Park track and there, of course, he soon met members of Belgrave Harriers and in particular the renowned athletic coach Franz Stampfl. His natural talent was immediately spotted by Stampfl: "If you train you could run fast!" Before long he was hooked on running and became a Belgrave member in May, 1955. On reflecting now, John says that taking up this sport was the finest move he made in his life.

In the Club Championships of 1955 he ran in the 1 mile and clocked 4:27 for 3rd place behind Jack Brown's 4:23. Brian Hallowell, who was to become John's training partner and rival, placed 2nd. The club records for 880yds. and 1 mile at this time stood to R.T.(Bob) Taylor with 1:53.6 and Jack Brown 4:11.8.

John returned to Kingstonian Football Club to play a few more matches but finally decided to concentrate on training for running. In October 1955, together with Brian Hallowell, he embarked upon Stampfl's schedule for milers, training mostly at the track at the Duke of York's Barracks in Chelsea's Kings Road, during the lunch hour.

Stampfl, a strong advocate of interval training with the stopwatch, left for Australia at about this time but his ideas on training were all set out in his book "Stampfl on Running". John followed the schedules to the letter. Such training may seem slightly dated now after Cerutty, Lydiard and the like have shown other ways to train. Nevertheless, Stampfl's methods had produced 4 minute milers and there are still few athletes who would not be happy to attain such a standard.

The schedule was as follows:- Mondays: 10 x 440yds. in 70 secs. during October reducing to 60 secs. by May. Interval between each effort 2½ minutes. Tuesdays: 5 x 880yds. 2:20 (Oct.) down to 2:00 (May) 10 mins. interval. Wednesdays: as Monday. Thursdays: 4 x 1,320 yds. 3:40 (Oct.) down to 3:15 (May) 10 mins. interval. Fridays: 6 miles steady. Saturdays: Cross country. Sundays: Rest.

The intervals were regarded as the most important part of each session. A shorter rest than is necessary to bring recovery to a certain level would result in the work being too severe - a breaking down instead of building up and at worst non-completion of the session. On the other hand too much recovery and the session failed in its objective to gradually improve.

By December things were really going well and John remembers Tom Carter shouting through the railings at the "Dukes": "You'll be burnt up by Christmas".

The ¼ mile sessions came along a treat and the times floated down to reach an even 60 seconds by February but the ½ and ¾ mile sessions were found to be a lot tougher; the ¾'s still being stuck on 3:28 at the beginning of March. As those ¼ mile sessions were regarded as being the "rest days" between the days of really hard work, they were relaxed for a while to 65 seconds. Immediately the ¾ miles dropped to 3:21 and then 3:15 within two weeks.

In April 1956 the season opened for John with a couple of wins in inter-club races, first over 1 mile and then over 880 yards in a time of 1:57.8. By May the end of Stampfl's schedule was achieved and time trials began to take the place of the odd session. During one of these lunch time trials, tailing Brian Hewson, John returned a comfortable 1:56 for the "half" and a A.A.A. official who had been watching was sufficiently impressed to tell him that he would try to get him into the forthcoming Whitsun British Games as a reserve for the invitation 800 metres.

John subsequently received a stereotype letter addressed to "T.F. Davis" requesting him to attend the White City as reserve. He thought it would be a good idea to go along and stand in the dressing rooms alongside such great athletes as Iharos of Hungary and others of the international elite, and on the appointed day he arrived at the stadium and saw Jack Crump of the British Board.

"I'm Davies, reserve for the 800 metres." "No you're not", said Crump, "You're running!". "No you've got it wrong, I can't race. I'm only here as a reserve" stuttered a suddenly aghast John. "You're running" said Crump firmly.

And before long, far from rubbing shoulders with the stars in the dressing rooms, the Belgrave Harrier found himself down on the track lining up for his first proper race over 800 metres with the best two-lap exponents in Europe for company. There was the British Champion Hewson, Roger Moens the Belgian World Record holder for the distance, Sventgali of Hungary the European Champion, Mugosa of Hungary and three other fine runners.

The "Belgravian" later reported: "Our boy was not intimidated by this array of talent and ran most creditably, challenging the leaders and finishing 5th in 1:52.3 which must be the fastest time ever by a Belgrave man at that distance." Moens was a clear winner from Hewson and Sventgali. Just to prove that it was not a flash in the pan, John was back on the White City cinders again two days later to record another sub 1:53 in a relay.

Honours soon followed these remarkable runs. Representing the A.A.A. he raced to a 4:12.6 mile win in a match v Cambridge University at Fenner's grass track and on June 2nd, in windy conditions, he took the Middlesex mile championship at his first attempt. But Stampfl's training had not paid dividends for him alone. In the Cheshire Championships Hallowell won the 1 mile and also recorded 4:10.6 for a new club record.

Trophy meetings were the top inter-club competitions at this time. John won the Heathfield trophy 880 yards in 1:55.9, attacking from the bell in the final after getting boxed in earlier in the afternoon during the heats. In the Brockman Trophy 1 mile, facing Boyd of Herne Hill and Woodford Green's Derek Johnson who gained an Olympic silver medal later in the year, John clocked his best time to date - 4:11.8 for 3rd with Hallowell, unable to find his customary finishing burst, 7th.

In a whirlwind of competition throughout June and July he reached 5th place in the Southern Championships 1 mile (4:13,8 after a 4:14.8 heat the night before) and ran for the A.A.A. again v Kent. But the highlight was July 13th at the White City - The A.A.A. Championships for 1956. In the 880 yards he qualified for the final with 1:52.8, a Club Record which stood until David Cocks started chopping it down in the early 60's to its current 1:49.6. Some excessive buffeting in the final did not allow John to repeat the performance and hopes of a place were dashed. Brian Hallowell was back in good form in the 1 mile, not qualifying for the final but returning 4:11.2.

Then came the Club Championships, and just a year after John's debut he raced off with the 880 yards trophy in 1:56.8 and later duelled with Hallowell in the mile, taking the lead with half a lap to go only to be repassed as they neared the tape.

August saw Davies racing in Karlstadt, Sweden in an all-star 800 metres. A 52 second first lap pulled him through to 1:52.7 and 7th behind Audun Boysen of Norway (1:48.3), Dan Waern of Sweden (1:48.9) with another Norwegian and two more Swedes packed in between 1:50 and 1:52. Boysen, incidentally, still holds the Norwegian record at 1:45.9.

Finally, this incredible first full season in athletics wound up with a victory in the Vancouver Trophy 800 metres, 2nd in the L.C.C. mile, an international 1,500M and a 1:55.8 relay leg on a cold damp night at Tooting Track as late as October 24th.

It all seemed so simple. If he could do all this on one winter's training, all he had to do now was to increase the work and the 4 minute barrier was there to be beaten. So it was back to the schedule but make it tougher, double everything, 20 x 440 and halve the intervals. That was his first mistake. His second was to run on through a severe bout of flu' against a doctor's advice. Training became a struggle, the winter passed and in 1957 although racing in Dublin and Malmo, Sweden, he never really got near his previous year's form.

The lesson was obvious. He had run worse on twice the training so in October '57 it was back to the schedule, carrying it through again to May '58 - and of course it worked! He regained his Middlesex Mile title and represented the A.A.A. against Cambridge again to clock his best ever mile of 4:10.6 equalling Hallowell's club record. Various wins in trophy meetings came his way and he returned a best half mile for the year of 1:53.4. Three performances which particularly stand out in John's mind were his wins in the Club Championships. He took the 880 yards ahead of Bob Taylor and Malcolm Robinson, the mile from Pat Newall and Ranjit Bhatia and the three miles, in 14:25, after sitting in on Newall for nearly the full distance.

John Davies continued racing on the track for some years but never again really captured the sparkling form of 1956 and 1958. He began to race more on the road, turning out in Belgrave road relay teams, including the classic London - Brighton, well into the sixties.

Whilst training at Tooting track in 1959 he met a certain girl from Selsonia Ladies A.C. running in the opposite direction. Pam and John married in 1960 and for these two setting up home together was not going to cramp their athletic style. Although he was not racing so much John was often encouraged by Pam to get out for a run. Often they would run together, around the "bridges" and as far as 18 miles on occasions. More often than not Pam was the first one to climb the 62 steps to their flat at the end of a run. Pam was at the height of her own athletic career and her tremendous record in the National Cross Country Championships must rate her as one of the best women distance runners ever: 3rd in 1963, 2nd 1964 then four successive wins in 1965,1966,1967 and 1968 followed by 2nd in 1969 before the birth of their first son Maxwell. They now have another son, Edward. More than a few Belgravians will remember Pam from their schooldays as she has taught Physics at Battersea County for some time.

Looking for a challenge in another direction John began studying in earnest. Having left school at 14 he started tackling 'O' levels at the age of 30 and with that same concentration that was previously reserved for athletic training it was not long before he had gained 8 passes.

All this time John's regular haunt was still the "Dukes" and around 1970 he fell in with George Betts, now 60 and one of the regular "Dukes Joggers". George encouraged him to have a go at the Finchley '20'. So in 1973 with a winter of long steady running, including a weekly Saturday run of 20 miles plus, John took on his first long race, completing the course in 2:07:59. Now he felt the almighty urge to take on the premier road race - the marathon. The long training runs were continued in fact his average mileage for the entire year was 44 per week and in October at the age of 42 he lined up for the Harlow Marathon. That race will long be remembered as it was the day Ian Thompson also made his marathon debut. John placed 142nd in 2:55:36 and later said: "You can run all your life but it's not until you've tackled a marathon that you realise you know nothing about running". It was the start of yet another challenge and since then he has run three more. In the same race in '74 he was down to 2:53, at Milton Keynes in the Road Runners Marathon in July '75 he became a Club Champion again with 2:52 and at Harlow last October the improvement was continued with 2:47.35 - a series of runs of which he is extremely proud.

John admits to getting a lot more pleasure out of his running these days than he ever did on the track and recalls the terrible nerves that used to afflict him; the pain in the knee that would materialise on learning that Pirie would be running; having to turn away from Iffley Road track on hearing the self-assured voice of Derek Johnson. He now regards this as having been one of his shortcomings, something which took him some time to come to terms with; the belief that famous sportsmen were of a different breed and not just ordinary men. "Almost anyone can get to the top", John now says, "It's a matter of setting one's mind to it and really concentrating on it".

Now at 44, John particularly enjoys those days when everything clicks and his running comes fresh, free and lithe. Those who run with him on a Saturday judge their standard of fitness by him, for when they manage to get a word in edgeways during his constant, cheerful chatter, they know they are improving. And there are still further targets ahead: - to beat the World Women's Best time for the marathon and to trounce Brian Gorman.

Alan Mead

Lucas, a newcomer, ran well in his first important race in our colours.

THE ENGLISH CROSS COUNTRY CHAMPIONSHIP .....

Our team travelled to Alderley Edge, Cheshire on March 14th to defend our title of English Cross Country Champions but Birchfield carried too many guns for us on this occasion and regained the title ... Tom Carter had the distinction of leading our team home, and qualifying for the International team. Arthur Penny and Bert Footer were next home for us, one second separating them. (1. Birchfield 90 pts, 2. Belgrave H 112 pts - 12. C.T.Carter, 17. A.W.Penny, 18. H.E.Footer, 22. J.Ginty, 44. A.Allum, 50. S.Ring, 52. A.R.Shaw, 54. H.W.Shields, 68. A.Patience)

BRIGHTON RELAY APRIL 25th 1936.....

The Bels made more athletic history by winning this classic race for the third time in succession. Special mention might be made of Pat Parker, who fully justified our hopes of him; Tom Carter, who, although faced with the task of tackling an opponent who was out of sight when he commenced his stage, yet reeled off mile after mile in automatic fashion. He not only caught his rival but gave us a lead, which at this stage was the real turning point of the race.
