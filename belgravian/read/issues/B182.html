Editorial · page 3

COMMENT

The decision of the International Olympic Committee to drop Walking from the Olympic Programme is a shattering blow to those who are involved in this branch of athletics. In Belgrave Harriers we feel it particularly keenly, for over the years our members have proved themselves to be amongst the best walkers in the world and they have Olympic medals to show for it.

Yes, the 'Games' do need pruning but the events to be axed should be the team events which are nothing more than nation against nation, directly in contrast to the Olympic ideal.

Is it too late to do anything about it? The British Race Walkers' Club is not taking the decision lying down and is doing all it can to stir up some action and welcomes any helpful suggestions which should be sent to C. W. Fogg, Metropolitan Police Training School, Hendon, N. W. 9 .

The Notice of the Annual General Meeting, to be held on Thursday, 20th Sept., is to be found on page 4 of this gazette. Your Committee hopes that you will make the effort to attend if you can, for if you have any criticisms or observations to make, this is the time to do it.

C. W. Fogg

PRESIDENT'S PARS

Clive Shippen

With my year of office almost at a close I would like to thank all those officers, members of Committee and the many other Belgravians who have made the year so enjoyable and, indeed, so successful. Although I was present at most of our activities during the year there were several that I missed due to fixture clashes or outside pressures and I can assure you that it is most disappointing to miss any Belgrave turn-out when in the "Chair".

Unfortunately my year of office was irretrievably marred by the loss of a man I greatly admired - Alf Harley. Alf's death left a vacuum which will take a long time to fill, and your Committee has missed the wise counsel he so often gave. As President, I discovered just how much Alf did on behalf of the Club, for when he had gone the many things we had all taken for granted for so long were suddenly left undone, and on more than one occasion we fell down on our responsibilities.

The loss of our Past President, Syd Roberts, so close on that of Alf Harley, struck a double blow to the Club and filled me with disillusionment - my term of office being only 3 months old.

But 3 events did bring me a great deal of pleasure. The R.W.A. 20 kms. at Victoria Park where our team packed in for a great win. The National Road Relay at Derby where our twelve shocked the opposition by more than holding their own against star-studded teams to finish a magnificent 5th. And then there was the meeting we held at Wimbledon Park Track for 60 or more unattached colts and boys. Such enthusiasm. Such dedicated work too by so many Belgravians who gave their day to the event. There were many other occasions also where one witnessed the tremendous amount of work put in by our officials - work so necessary to keep our Club strong and thriving. It has been a joy to work with them all and Belgravians can rest assured, their Club is in good hands.

Just as the beginning of my term was a sad memory, so it was near the end too. Percy Wilson lost a long and brave fight against illness and died in July. He will be remembered, alongside brother Joe, for his many appearances at walking fixtures, his regular manning of sponging stations on the Brighton Road and particularly for his work with youngsters.

As another year approaches with its untold fortunes, and misfortunes, I am proud to have had the privilege of leading you through the eventful Club year of 1972/73. Thank you.

Coaches, helpers - anyone! That's what we need down at Wimbledon Park Track on Sunday mornings between 10.30 and 12.00. Our recent coaching day for unattached youngsters has stimulated a steady flow of new members local to Wimbledon. About 20 have been appearing regularly and Philip Gee, Bill Couzens and Alan Mead have been bearing the brunt of looking after these lads for 1½ hours. Measuring throws or jumps, rough timekeeping or just controlling and helping groups of lads is something many of our inactive members could do. Their help is needed and it is a job I am sure they will enjoy. By such small beginnings are coaches made. Can you help? If so, contact any of the abovenamed trio or contact me direct.

What a fine season JOHN BICOURT has been having. Now established as an international choice, John has only had Hollings and Davies to contend with amongst U.K. 'chasers. But the three of them have had some fascinating struggles to register a 1973 U.K. best. Each in turn held the mark and then John went to Norway, lost narrowly to Moravcik of Czechoslovakia but had the great satisfaction of hearing his time given, not only as a 1973 U.K. best, but as a U.K. record! Or so it seemed. A rapid revision of times left John just inches away from the National record A bitter disappointment, but he has regained his 1973 lead and is only the third Briton (after Andy Holden and Dave Bedford) to break 8:30. Well done John.

Club records are particularly sparse between the years 1887 and 1914, so any information relating to that period is particularly interesting. We should all be very aware of our history and traditions, and members would be doing the Club a service if they passed on any information they feel to be of value. If old cuttings or photo's have a special sentimental value then they will be returned after a sight by the writer. The photograph reproduced here is one of our few pre-World War I groups and features; R.W. Ricketts, E. Gordon, E. Adams, C. Cook, R.W. Best, J.B. Belchamber, H. Brown, H. Parker, O. Horwood and R. Savage. Can anyone identify any others?

Visitors to Belgrave Hall this last few weeks will have noticed the expensive looking facelift it has been given. Well it would have been expensive all right but for Arthur Bruce, our Chairman of the Building Sub-Committee. We live in an age of very high maintenance costs and Arthur decided that a bit of 'do-it-yourself' was called for. He organised a rota of working parties over a 7 week period. The Hall was washed down, made good, re-decorated and the floor sanded and sealed. Needless to say it was Arthur and just a handful of members who gave up a lot of their time, and our thanks is due to them and any others who helped out when they could. Not only was the work successful but the efforts of Arthur and his team saved the Club a great deal of money.

Belgrave Harriers went into the publicity business at Ravensbury Park on Saturday, June 23rd. An Open day for the Borough of Merton's sports clubs was held and our stand and display was the best on view - but then I'm biassed! Charlie Manning was behind the successful stand, despite many late changes of plan imposed upon him, and Bill Lucas made our demonstration go with a swing with his lively commentating. Your President was the first to be pulled out in a "devil take the hindmost" race which was eventually won by Gerry North. Trevor Hart and Alan Mead won the paarlauf and two teams of our colts and boys fought out a relay. To cap it all Carl Lawton demonstrated how easy it was to walk two laps of the grass "track" leaving a heap of struggling, moaning runners walking (?) in his wake. Our next show-piece (static only) will be at Morden Park on Merton Show Day, August Bank Holiday Monday (27th).

You can't keep a good man down! It was only 3 years ago that I reproduced a photo of Ernie Duffett (Dec.1970) showing him in some pretty good company - he was 3rd in the Surrey County C.C. of about 1928. Well here he is once more (see opposite page) - and 3rd again! Showing a novel form of drop finish, Ernie (111) fails to catch No. 1 and the winner (53) L.E.Hope of Shaftesbury who won this handicap mile in 4:25. The occasion was an S.L.H. meeting at the Oval in the late twenties - a venue where that small group of enthusiasts back in 1887 decided to form a club called Belgrave Harriers.

Clive Shippen

R.I.P. PERCY WILSON

Harry Shields

My mind is filled with many thoughts in an endeavour to do justice to the memory of Percy Wilson who died in his 61st year after a long and distressing illness. The 30 years service he gave to the Club is filled with acts of kindness and example.

He was never a Champion or an Officer but more in the tradition of a Sergeant, whom Wellington in his Peninsular Campaigns, leading to great victory at Waterloo, so well described as the back-bone of his army. His remarks were:-

" The ideal sergeant is not easily found and when found is invaluable, he can be relied upon never to connive at others faults, but to use tact to get his wishes carried out. He has to be a meritorious man whose personal example is the means of swaying actions. He demonstrates by example and his reward is the benefit of popularity by receiving loyal and intelligent help instead of mere obedience. No mercenary motive actuates his conduct and whose hands are never soiled with the gifts of an inferior."

My first experience of Percy and his inseparable brother Joe was when I forsook running to take up Walking. They took me under their wings and instilled in me the love and comradeship of the sport. Many others, including subsequent champions, have similar reason to be grateful for their early advice and encouragement.

In discussion after the Cremation, it was said he was a natural coach, of unselfish dedication and without thought of reward or thanks. This is indeed true.

Examples to indicate his sterling character readily come to mind. (a) The organisation of the Annual Boys Brigade Events at St. Magdelene's Church, Earlsfield, which attracted some hundred boys in graded walking races. It was apparent that most of the cost of the awards and other expenses came from Percy's pocket. (b) The London to Brighton Walk - during recent years he organised the Earlswood Common Feeding Station and accepted responsibility for many of the supplementary refreshment points on the road. (c) His ready help in all running and walking events either as a pointsman or to man a feeding station. On 100 mile walk and runs his cheery encouragement with a drink or a sponge during the long hours, no matter what the weather conditions, were acts of pure dedication.

His own career as a walker was not insignificant. He was a "Centurion" i.e. one who has walked 100 miles in under 24 hours. The number of London to Brighton and other long distance walks he has completed must bear comparison with any. The events in which he has taken part for Belgrave and his second claim club London Vidarians must be astronomical. His full capabilities as a walker were never seen because of his pre-occupation of help to others.

The loss of Percy is another of our great stalwarts gone. His service was different to that of Alf Harley and Harry Churcher but still one which brought lustre to the Belgrave Harriers and the whole of race walking.

It is astonishing that he was not put forward as a "Vice President" or "Life Member" of the Club. Probably I should take the blame for this omission.

His cremation was attended by representatives of the London Vidarians and the R.W.A. (Southern Area), while the affection in which he was held by his family was evident.

It upset me considerably in scanning the 47 floral tributes not to see one from the Club. In these days of Inter-Flora surely there can be no excuse.

To such a man the only fitting epitaph is, "It is well finished".

Our deepest condolences to Joe, and all his family and relations.

Harry Shields

SOUTHERN COUNTIES CHAMPS.

JUNE 22nd/23rd 1973 - CRYSTAL PALACE.

Eight Belgravians ventured into the Crystal Palace arena to compete in the Southern Counties A.A.A. Championships. Although lacking in numbers they achieved a fair measure of success.

Rodney Heward-Mills gained our solitary medal with a 15.54 triple jump but Steve White only just missed in an exciting long jump competition in which 2 centimetres covered 2nd to 5th places. Stevie was pushed down to 4th with an inferior second best leap, having already managed 6th place in a good 200m final the evening before.

Track Vice-Captain Charlie Gillett improved on his best times for 1973 in the 400m flat and hurdle races and looks as if he could go faster still.

After setting a target of around 3:52 for the season, John Stow is going to have to think again. On the Friday night he qualified in the 1500m with an excellent personal best of 3:51.1 and then improved again in the final to equal John Bicourt's best, thus continuing their keen rivalry. John Bicourt says that he can go a bit faster yet but on this occasion he understandably found the exertions of a 5000m race too much after his run in Warsaw the previous day and he was forced to retire.

SPOTLIGHT on CLIFFORD 'SNOWY' BROOKS

A.R.M.

Turn up at the start of any of our main track fixtures and you are certain to see the muscular figure of Olympic Decathlete Clifford "Snowy" Brooks, limbering up for the early events. Hours later as the meeting draws to a close he will still be there, holding together a relay team, having competed non-stop on the field and track throughout the whole match. A great deal of our success is owed to this prolific points scorer who is one of the most popular personalities in the Belgrave team and athletic circles in general throughout the Southern Counties. Everyone seems to know "Snowy" whether they be an international woman discus thrower or a lowly 800 metre runner. Talking to him in the restaurant after a session at the Crystal Palace is like being in the centre of a whirlwind as back-chat and banter flies back and forth between him and all and sundry.

He was born on 19th August 1944 at Camberwell and for most of his life he has been more or less based in the South London area. Athletics for him was a once a year affair at school - the school sports day. He was not particularly good, showing probably just as much ability in football and cricket but towards the end of his days at St. Luke's Secondary Modern, Kingston-upon-Thames, he did run in a few races for Kingston Athletic Club.

In 1960 he signed on with the Royal Navy and until 1971 he served as a steward on board the anti-submarine frigate HMS Plymouth and the aircraft carriers HMS Victorious and HMS Hermes. Life in the navy was enjoyable and his travels with the "grey funnel line" gave him the chance to see a good deal of the world, particularly the Far East and the Mediterranean.

During a spell at home, he decided to have another dabble at the sport of athletics - perhaps he would even do a bit of training. It was good fortune that during a visit to Battersea Park track he renewed his acquaintanceship with Ben Lulham who had been the mainstay of Kingston Athletic Club. At that time the club had recently wound up its affairs with some of its members joining Surrey AC and others, including Ben's remarkable squad of sprinters, coming to Belgrave. Cliff Brooks became a Belgravian.

Almost immediately he made his presence felt by winning three Club Championships and in the "Belgravian" of November 1964 it was reported... " A new face of great promise was that of C.T.Brooks whose performances of 5'2"/20'9¾"/38'6" in the three jumping events were on sheer natural ability. "

Being at sea much of the time meant that most of his training consisted of weight lifting which he had to do in a narrow space below deck in between the fuel tanks, with barely enough room to swing a "cat". His enthusiasm was not to be daunted and in 1965, with his all round ability, he made the natural progression to the ten-in-one-event, the Decathlon, competing as a guest in the R.A.F. Championships.

By 1968, influenced by his training companion Great Britain international and fellow navy athlete, Peter Gabbett, "Snowy" was beginning to make real progress. Sometimes this progress was to our dismay, for the following season we lost one of the premier trophy meetings - the "Ryder Trophy" at Southampton - to the Royal Navy by just eight points. Fifteen points were scored for the RN that day by an athlete wearing a tracksuit with the name "Snowy" emblazoned across the shoulders!

The Belgrave Club Record for the Decathlon was by now his own, and in his final year with the navy in 1971, he won the 400m hurdle championship in a RN record which still stands of 55.1 and in the same season was placed 3rd in the Inter-Services 400m hurdles and 2nd, behind Gabbett, in the RN Decathlon.

He now exchanged his sailor's uniform for another, that of the armoured division of Securicor. Life now took on a different routine, one that involved an almost daily trip, winter and summer, to the National Recreation Centre at Crystal Palace, training with four or five other decathletes. A considerable amount of his sessions were still with the weights and he is quite proud of his best lifts, although he asserts that a weight lifter of his body weight would be able to do far better.

Decathlon where he amassed 6389 points, his best score to date. This moved him up to 11th on the British Ranking list for the event and meant that he had improved our Club Record by 986 points since he first took it over. All this, of course, was crowned by his selection to represent Barbados, the birth-place of his father, at the Munich Olympic Games.

Munich was the experience of a life time. The facilities and stadium were superb and in the athletes' village language problems were soon overcome and a friendly atmosphere prevailed. "Snowy" particularly enjoyed meeting the U.S. Decathlon team and Bill Toomey, winner in the Mexico Olympics and World Record Holder.

The competition took place on 7th/8th of September and as he was not expected to excel in this company, he found himself quite relaxed and admits to having felt a lot more nervous prior to a Surrey Championship. His overnight score was on a par with that during the AAA Championship and there were hopes that he would again improve his best but trouble struck during the pole vault. He found himself unable to get in a clear vault - not surprising when you consider that the starting height was around his best ever. He decided at that point to retire from the competition, and watched as the mighty Nikolay Avilov of the Soviet Union crushed the best all rounders in the world to win with a World Record score of 8454 points.

What of the future? His progress since 1967 has been relentless but in an event where skill in so many different areas is necessary it takes a long while to reach ones best, so who knows what mark he will eventually attain. An obvious target for the time being is 7000 points but as long as he continues to improve, "Snowy" will keep up the daily grind, aiming higher and higher.

FROM THE BELGRAVIAN 25 years ago...

R.W.A. NATIONAL 20 MILES CHAMPIONSHIP, SUDBURY. May 8th 1948 ........... Our teams were supported by what must be the biggest crowd of Club members and friends ever seen at a road race. Our Road Captain, Charlie Churcher, surprised most people by not adopting his usual get-a-way method and the opening stages of the race were unusually slow. Later on, however, Charlie developed stomach trouble and was obliged to fall back. About the same time Colin Sutton had trouble with his shoes and lost a lot of ground. In the centre of the field, however, our Vice-Captain, Jack Bidgood, was doing some excellent work holding together about half a dozen of our walkers which no doubt had much to do with the ultimate result of the race. On recovering from his trouble, Colin Sutton battled bravely on recovering position after position and eventually caught up on Jack Bidgood and Co. Charlie, however, had continued to drop back and with our friends, Surrey W.C., holding two places in the first five, a win for us seemed out of the question. Eventually our lads finished in a procession with five consecutive placings from 18 to 22 and backed up by George Bentley, Joe Wilson and Joe Coleman, we succeeded in taking the Nijmegan Shield with our eight walkers in the first 48, an excellent performance on which all are to be congratulated.

2nd IN R.W.A. 20 MILES

Harry Shields

JUNE 16th 1973 - EWELL.

Apart from being the oldest and most cherished of the R.W.A. Championships, the 20 miles is that which requires a most careful balance of speed and stamina. No pity is given to those who mis-judge the course or weather conditions. To be with the leaders, an average speed of 7½ miles per hour must be maintained with turns of pace to match and break those around you.

This 54th annual competition was held in tropical conditions, surpassed in memory only by the grey-beards, with "Ah ! You should have been in the 50Km at Preston, or in the 'Brighton' of 1953 when the tar melted and the only encouragement was imaginary hallucinations of oases, like those described by desert explorers, to spur you on your way".

These were this championship's conditions and how soon the "sheep" and the "goats" were sorted out. On an approximately six lap course, indiscretions taken soon showed themselves. Our first grievous loss was that of Allan Callow, who after leading our team for some time, found the conditions too much, and dropped out. This eliminated us from the"Nijmegan Shield" for the Club with the first complete team, which until recent memory, was a trophy held by us for many years. It was a point of honour that outside the four scoring men, the others should encourage and prop up any in distress to make certain all finished, and many are the tales that could be told.

The tenacity of Dave McMullen, D.Hall and John Dunsford showed this spirit and the pity was that E.Stroud could not be included in the team and rewarded for his fine walk.

The championship was well fought, both individual and team. Although much the stronger team over the last 5 miles we lost to Sheffield United Harriers by a margin so small that it was only necessary for one of our first three men to catch and beat the man in front in order to win.

Harry Shields

MEET MARTYN BENNETT

Martyn is one of Belgrave's leading Juniors and the current holder of the'Olympic Cup' - our 5 mile Junior Championship.

Q. Why did you take up walking? A. I don't really know. I suppose it came as a natural progression from doing a few minor school walks to joining Belgrave and eventually concentrating on it as my premier sport.

Q. What training do you do? A. Winter: Rugby training Monday to Friday and a match on Saturday morning. Race Walk on Saturday afternoon. I did no real walking training during last winter save for races on Saturdays. Summer: Sunday- 14 miles slow in Richmond Park. Monday- 2 miles slow and 5 miles fast. Tuesday- 5 miles including sprints. Wednesday- 5 miles slow. Thursday- 8 (increasing by 2 each week) x 400 metres in 1:45-1:50 with 1:10 rests. Friday- 2 miles slow. Saturday- race or rest. My summer training has proved less effective than I hoped and at the moment I'm doing a lot of circuits and weights in preparation for the oncoming Rugby season.

Q. Any other interests? A. All sports interest me and I'll have a go at anything. Rugby and Cross-Country occupy me during the winter but I'm not much good at either, though I enjoy both. Music, Pubs (though I don't drink much) and books seem to occupy most of my spare time but for the past year I have managed to put training before these in terms of importance.

Q. Aims and ambitions? A. Unfortunately I am one of those people who have tremendous ambitions but who rarely realise them, mainly through lack of effort on my behalf. Athletically, I had hoped to walk in an Olympics but now a group of sensationalist and twisted showmen, hypocritical to the Olympic spirit, have deprived me and every other Junior race walker of one of the major reasons for remaining in the sport. Outside of sport, I hope to go to university next year and get a degree, probably in history.

Q. Best performances? A. In terms of time my best performances have been at Crystal Palace in the spring of this year where I did a 14:16 3km and a 24:28 5km. I suppose my three sub 58:30 seven mile races during the winter were my most satisfactory, as they all came as a bit of a surprise to me (and to everyone else I think).

know the rules - HIGH JUMP

(a) The uprights or posts shall not be moved during the competition unless the Judges consider the take-off or landing ground has become unsuitable. Such a change shall be made only after a round is completed. (b) Unless such details are specified on the programme, the Judges shall decide the height at which the competition shall start, and the different heights to which the bar will be raised at the end of each round. The competitors shall be informed of the details before the competition begins. (c) A competitor may commence jumping at any height above the minimum height and may jump at his own discretion at any subsequent height. Three consecutive failures, regardless of the height at which any of such failures occurs, disqualify from further jumping. n.b. The effect of this rule is that a competitor may forego his second and third jumps at a particular height (after failing once or twice) and still jump at a subsequent height. (d) Even after all the other competitors have failed, a competitor is entitled to continue jumping until he has forfeited his right to compete further and his best jump shall be recorded as the winning height. (e) The employment of weights or grips of any kind is forbidden. (f) A competitor may place marks to assist him in his run up and take-off and a handkerchief or similar object on the cross-bar for sighting purposes. The distance of the run is unlimited. (g) All measurements shall be made perpendicularly from the ground to the upper side of the cross-bar where it is lowest. A steel or fibre-glass measure should be used. Any measurements of a new height should be made before competitors attempt that height. In the case of records the officials must check the measurement after the height has been cleared. The height shall be recorded to the nearest ¼ inch (1 cm.) below the height measured, i.e. fractions less than ¼ inch (1 cm.) must be ignored. n.b. Judges should ensure, before commencing the competition, that the underside and front of the cross-bar are distinguishable and that the bar is always replaced in a similar manner. (h) A competitor fails if he - (i) In the course of a jump dislodges the bar so that it falls from the pegs; or (ii) Touches the ground or landing area beyond the plane of the uprights without first clearing the bar; or (iii) Takes off from both feet.

PROFESSIONAL ATHLETICS

Denis MacManus

Hon. Editor,

It seems to me that your "Comment" in the June issue of"The Belgravian"is somewhat ambiguous.

Surely, amateur athletics - which is our concern - has nothing whatsoever to do with the professional showbiz industry whether it be Mike O'Hara's professional athletic circus; Kramer's professional tennis circus; all-in-wrestling on the telly; or professional football at Wembley.

To emphasise the very real difference between our amateur sport and their showbiz sport, I enclose a news-cutting which states: "Mike O'Hara's advice to the athletes he pays to run in his professional circus - and he's said to be wooing Dave Bedford - includes the following advice 'you are athletes first, but remember you are entertainers also and a bit of showbiz will be appreciated by your fans' ". This really implies that showbiz athletes, in the commercial interests of the entertainment industry will be told precisely how to act their performances, which would then be about as genuine as all-in wrestling on telly.

On the other hand, the vast majority of amateur sportsmen whom I've known over the past 50 years have enjoyed their sport for its own sake and also for the honour of their club. Let us keep it that way, and let us ignore the money-grubbing professionals with the contempt (or should it be pity? ) which they deserve.

Denis makes a valid point - we are involved with amateur athletics. However, we must be honest with ourselves. True amateur athletics disappears above "county" level with the first gift of free sports-shoes, whilst the ordinary club competitor must fork out £10 for a pair. Is professional sport such an evil thing anyway? Do cricketers or golfers enjoy their sport any the less for the money they make at it and does the week-end sportsman on the village cricket oval begrudge the international his earnings?
