CLUB NOTES

Honours fall thick upon us. To have won the "Berman" Cup and the South of the Thames Junior Championship, when we can be no more than getting into our stride, must satisfy the most ambitious of our enthusiasts. Yet, while we are justifiably puffed with pride, we must look ahead to the big races of the new year and conserve our energies for still greater triumphs.

Already our Committee are at work on the programme for next summer season. Two projects which have received favourable comment in the Committee-room are outlined here. The first is that we should take part in a four-cornered relay and team match of 4 events (4 x 100yds , 4 x 440yds., 1 mile team race and 2 miles walk, team race) with Highgate H., Sambridge H. and Herne Hill H at Finsbury Park. The match would be decided by points, on the same lines as the "Doris" and "Inter-Club Relay" Shields competitions, which have always been so popular with us.

The second suggestion, from the S L.H , concerns Achilles Club, Herne Hill H., London A.C., Surrey A C. and the Polytechnic H., besides themselves and our own Club. It is for the formation of an athletic league in which each club will meet the others in turn, once during the season, in a team match. The events would be as in the former suggestion, but without the walk, and in addition 4 x 220yds., 4 x 880yds. and 4 x 120yds. hurdles (shuttle), relays. Field events (high jump, long jump, putting the shot and throwing the javelin) would be included, too. What a galaxy of athletic opportunity !

At the Committee meeting on November 1st, the following 11 new members were elected : J. Lawrence, J. C. Capp, F. R. Webb, C W. G Law, H Morgan, A. H Bulley, J A Blair, S Garland, E. W. Warren, F. Tame and J J. Hegarty

Three resignations were accepted with regret : R. S. Barnard, E A. Barber, H. Dowsett.

INTER-CLUB C.C. RACE WITH ASHCOMBE A.C AND THAMES VALLEY A.C. (5 MILES).

L.E F.

Apparently a cross-country race is an exciting affair to watch. At least, that is what I and a small band of supporters thought on Saturday, October 20th, when more than thirty Belgravians went down to Feltham to compete in their first race over the country this season. As usual, we were much too strong for the other two clubs, who had a keen race, however, for second place in the team contest; Ashcombe beating the Valley by only one point.

Khlopin, as we expected was leading round the rather dangerous bend some hundred yards from the start, but he was soon passed by Cohen and Pyer. We were surprised to see how the field had spread out after half a mile. Cohen had set up a very fast pace, but he was soon caught and passed by Bicknell. Pyer was third, some distance behind, but well ahead of the rest of the field. This order for the leaders remained the same to the end.

At half-way it was very strange to see Footer as far back as the thirties—he was taking a friend along—but it was not strange, but merely wonderful, to see him tearing in at the finish—ninth ! Many remarks were made in regard to Footer's method of dealing with obstacles. Ordinary people either climb over or crawl underneath, but he seems to dive through without breaking his stride and without so much as a "Pardon" to the slighted obstacle

Another spectacular feature was the easy running of Duffett and W. G. Webb, who appeared to be jogging round having a yarn and yet finished fifth and sixth respectively. The average runner has to "run his eyeballs out" to get into the twenties, yet our two running captains can get up to the leaders by means of a little gentle trotting.

It has already been mentioned that Bicknell was first man home. He finished unchallenged at quite a fast pace and in perfect style, looking as though he had enjoyed running over his own course. Mickey Pyer was first man to score for the Belgrave and was still well ahead of the bunch which included those two captains who scorn racing. The latter were looking round to count up the fact that we had ten in thirteen, and it is understood that they were very relieved to discover that there was no need for them to start hurrying after all.

A figure in white was seen to dart from the above-mentioned bunch and run into fourth place and become second man to score for us. This was A. Penny, who had run remarkably well. Penny is a useful miler on the track and was third in our opening race on the road at Wimbledon. It is safe to prophesy that he will be another strong addition to our team. Robertshaw, perhaps our most successful runner last winter, was only nineteenth. He is not yet fit, which is all to the good, for he is not likely to become stale at the time of our more important races next February and March.

Of the others who finished well up, mention should be made of S R Chorley, who is "streets" better than he was last year, and our former walking secretary, in his first cross-country race for two years, did well to finish thirteenth with plenty in hand. Throughout he had been running comfortably and he makes running look a very easy sort of business Most of the others, apart from the leaders, looked the worse for wear

In conclusion (and by way of a digression) I would like to give a tip to officials. It is their duty to be dignified and to earn respect from all I will therefore tell them that the man with an umbrella who witnessed this contest was addressed as "Sir" by one of the competitors

L.E F.

INTER-CLUB RACE.

W.A.R.

At Wimbledon, on November 10th. Club 5 miles Sealed Handicap in conjunction.

Our Club's numerical strength was again demonstrated in the second Inter-Club Race with T V H and A.A.C., as although both the opposing clubs had more starters than at the first meeting, we again mustered more than they could field together. In fact there were just 100 starters and I think 60 were Belgravians

The large field made for more to score, and 18 a side was decided upon. Of course, this was very much in our favour, and our 18 were actually in the first 26 places. Probably the Club Sealed Handicap, incorporated in the race, was an incentive to our chaps, and produced a very close field. Even closer were the placings in the Handicap The short space of 26 seconds separated the first 10 men.

The race itself was very interesting, as there was the prospect of a fine tussle for individual honours. After a fast start, Allum (Belgrave) led at the first turn, with Cohen (Ashcombe) close behind Getting on to the golf links, Cohen took over the lead, but hereabouts Footer came through with a rush and, using Sandy Hill to the best advantage, went to the front. The order through the Brook was Footer, Cohen, Flower, Junr., Duffett, Pyer, Rice and Allum

Then came a minor tragedy as Footer suddenly decided to go round the rough field instead of across it, thereby losing the lead and about 70 or 80 yards of ground, which, of course, he could not make up on Cohen, although he passed the rest of us again.

Going up the Horse-Ride, Bicknell (T.V.H.) and Penny (Belgrave) put in some good running and got into the first 6 places. Except for a new T.V.H. member in Porter, who made a good effort across the common home, the other positions remained unaltered

For the rest, A. G. Jones ran very well, as we thought he was short of training, and S Chorley again got home in the first 25. In fact, the whole of the South of Thames Junior A Team showed up well.

S. Drake has certainly improved in winning this very close handicap The "Footer" school had a good day, as R. Freestone took the Novice's Prize, and H. E. himself took 2 awards. Coleman, on his running, merits watching in the Surrey County Junior Race.

W.A.R.

SOUTH OF THE THAMES JUNIOR.

The South of the Thames Junior Race, Surrey and Berks, took place in splendid weather at Epsom Downs, on November 17th.

Our Club had 4 teams entered, a record, I believe, for this race, and although some of the selected men failed to turn up, we managed to start the 4 teams. What is more, they all closed-in, a very satisfactory display

There was a large field of runners, and at the fall of the flag our boys were well away. Wesson, of Epsom, forced the pace at the start, with Allum of our own Club lying 2nd The rest of our "A" team were close behind. At a mile W. S. Mitchell, Belgrave H , had taken the lead, followed by L. J. Cohen, of Ashcombe. Allum, Rice and Penny were close on their heels. In fact, our 4 were in the first 6. Hopes beat high in our breasts, and we were not disappointed. For when the field came in sight once more, Mitchell, although deposed from the lead by Cohen, was running finely, and our other men, Penny and Allum, were having a battle royal for 3rd place Three in four ! It was all over, for Rice, although dropping several places, came along in the 13th position and we had won an easy and handsome victory

It was a case of " well run all !" a very fine performance and one that justified our high hopes of those three most promising members, namely, W. S. Mitchell, A. W. Penney and A. Allum. I only hope that Mrs. Allum, who was one of the many supporters present at the race, was as pleased as we were.

The remaining 4 of our "A" team ran well together, and fully justified their selection. Although it hardly seems fair to mention individuals, everyone having run so well, a word of praise is due to F. G. Ford who ran, although feeling far from well, and also to C. R. Strange, H W. Swetman, R. Hickey and R. Freestone, who all ran extremely well, and should, if they persevere, improve on their performances.

RUNNING

E. A. DUFFETT, Hon Running Sec.

We have some attractive fixtures during the next month and perhaps a brief review of them might be helpful to a few members.

On December 1st the following team will compete in the Surrey Youths' Race, to be held from the headquarters of the Ranelagh H., viz., J. G. Coleman, A. C. Canard, S. Garland, T. C. Jones, D. Mann, L. C. Clapham, A. L Sanders and A. A. G. Thorne. The response of our junior members in support of this race is disappointing, but we hope for better support next year.

On December 8th we have a big day. Social Run with our old friends, Croydon H., Epsom H. and Mitcham A.C., from the headquarters of the latter at Rose Hill Farm, Sutton, followed by a social evening at the White Hart Hotel, Mitcham All runners and friends are asked to come along and swell the merry throng. On the same day a team will be representing our Club in a race versus St. George's Services from their abode at Mitcham; team will be selected and notified as usual These runners will rejoin the main body at the White Hart in the evening.

The third inter-club race between Ashcombe, T.V.H. and ourselves will be held on December 15th, from the former's headquarters at Roehampton. It is hoped that our boys will support this fixture as well as they have done the previous ones, and so keep up our record attendances.

December 22nd is the date of the Club's 7 Miles Championship for the Blackstaffe Shield (Holder, A. Clark) A new course of 2 laps has been selected, and in conjunction with the race will be a Sealed Handicap, which I trust all members will support, as although some consider themselves out of it, so far as Championship placings are concerned, I should like to remind them of the Handicap. I am sure that, given a fine entry, our Handicapper will be in a most lenient mood.

" Boxing Day," our own Powderhall meeting, the one the sharp brigade wait for Here it is : handicaps to suit all, for sprinters a 120yds race, for the distance men a 2 miles run. Now then, boys, here is your chance to take home, not a turkey, but one of the many handsome prizes that are to be given. Bring your friends along, they won't go to bed the previous night, so get them to make a 24 hour shift of it by coming to Battersea Park to see you run.

E. A. DUFFETT, Hon Running Sec.

R W.A. 7 MILES WALK.

A record field of 231 individuals and 28 teams started at Bowes Park on October 27th. We were successful in securing the "Berman" Cup, presented to the winning team in the level race, from the holders, Surrey A C.

J. G. Scamell was again very speedy and led our team home F. H. Elson, who was next, was only a few yards in front of R. D McMullen, with whom he had walked all the way, and J. E. Field provided our fourth. As F. Rickards followed Field in, we were pretty safe for a fourth man.

Such a capture as the "Berman" Cup is a worthy sequel to the fine record of last season. Both E. Saunders and W. G. Dodkin walked better than ever before, and the former gained third place in the handicap J. J. Cricton, in his first open road walk, showed considerable promise.

We were the only Club to enter three teams for the race, and the walking of our "juniors" (in the road-walking sense) could hardly have been better. Altogether it was an auspicious start.

WALKING.

B J T KNIFTON, Hon Walking Sec.

December will be a busy month for walkers. On the 1st we shall be engaged in the annual Croydon—Banstead and back race with the Surrey W.C. (11¾ miles). Last year we were the losers through a weak tail, although we had the first two men to finish. This time we hope to remedy the defect. McMullen, Dodkin, Cricton, Carne and Rathbone have already promised to be there, and they, with Green Rickards, and Manning, will make a redoubtable team.

On the following Saturday we meet the Metropolitan Police. All our resources must be summoned to beat this "touring side," but again I am sure we can rely on our boys. The first Belgrave man will, as usual, receive a small prize.

The date for the "Horwood Cup" (Junior) race is December 15th. The event is eagerly awaited and promises to be one of the most exciting "tear-ups" of the year. It will be worth watching. A handicap is held in conjunction, so that the splendid pack of novices which we have coming-on, may share in the spoils.

Quite a number of our men have "Godstone" ambitions, and are using the "Banstead" as training to this end. The race, of course, takes place on Boxing Day ("weather permitting" we should add, smilingly, with memories of last Boxing Day) and the distance is about 18¾ miles. The course is a difficult one. Walkers of tender years will find the Three Miles Handicap at Battersea Park more suited to their capabilities.

B J T KNIFTON, Hon Walking Sec.

Report · page 11

SOCIAL HELD AT WIMBLEDON, NOV. 10th, 1928.

A very hard run with T.V.H. and A.A.C., in which the "Bels" had a run-away victory, and the wide open spaces of Wimbledon Common, had given everybody an enormous appetite. Afterwards we were joined by members of the London Olympiades and Poly. Ladies—a hundred of us sat down to tea. Judging by the consumption, I feel convinced that all had a good "bob's worth;" surely the tea that is provided by the Bels. cannot be "touched" for quality, quantity and service, anywhere

After the tea we were treated to some good entertainment. C. Boud created a fine success with two songs Then that dapper little chap. W. H. Williams, also captured the applause. The talent of these Club members was highly appreciated. Miss Fish, daughter of "Smiling Bill," upheld the ladies' side and gave us a good song. Of course, W. L. Bayliss accompanied the items, as usual—no easy task—and we are indebted to him. On arrival of the dance band, we all had a chance to show our paces. Why the devil do you all sit round the room like wallflowers? For goodness' sake, you great big mothers' darlings, get up and make the girls dance with you! (My sympathy is with the girls). What is the use of a dance band when you all sit and stare? It is entirely up to everyone to make himself sociable.

A new member of ours, P. J. E. Cook, did us a great favour by persuading his sister to entertain us (some baby, eh!). After singing us a "love" song, she followed with some fine dancing, Some of our boys, I noticed, bucked-up considerably; they seemed ready for another five miles! W. H. Williams again obliged, and further items from Miss Cook brought the "turns" to a close.

I take this opportunity of thanking all who helped in arranging this very successful evening, especially the artists who so willingly came forward. We want additional artists for future occasions. Don't hide your light under a bushel!

SOCIAL RUN WITH HIGHATE AND POLY H AT EDGWARE, NOV 3rd, 1928

W.A R.

The prospects of a fine afternoon, high tea and a social afterwards, and perhaps the recollection of the good time certain members in particular had last year, attracted a large number of our runners to Edgware. Poly. H. had been invited also, so we turned out over a hundred strong. Our walkers were at Wimbledon trying-out the new course for our "open" walk, and strangely enough, the Highgate walkers gave the fixture little support.

Eventually slow, medium and fast packs were sent out. Most of the Bels were in the fast pack, which covered about 7 miles The country was not of the best, owing to awkward hedges, fences and unpleasant odours The hold-ups at the obstacles enabled the stragglers to come up, and so the writer was present when the pack ran across the course of the Mary Ward A.C during the latter club's 5 miles handicap. There followed a "tear-up" for a mile or so, in which the back-markers were unofficially paced.

There was a notable absence of the usual "tear-in" at the end of the run, no doubt due to the liberal quantity of hot water which was awaiting us.

Tea supplies were rather short, but since our Paul was satisfied, "nuff sed." In spit of our expectations, there was no social after tea, so we exchanged good wishes with our hosts and departed.

Before the train had left the platform a card-party was in full swing, and Bert Footer's facial expressions when holding four aces and a king, were a tonic for anyone. Another large party listened while H J. Bignall, Highgate's Olympic representative, related his Amsterdam experiences, and it was a jolly gathering which at length broke up.

SOCIAL SECTION.

H. L. ROLLINS, Social Hon. Sec.

The expression "Social" may not impress some members of our prospering Club It is true that socials, dances, etc, may not actually improve an athlete's ability, but by meeting fellow-members at these functions friendships are formed, which would not have come about in the ordinary way. We wish to encourage a friendly and sociable atmosphere among our members; this will be achieved, I am sure, by everybody's support of our Socials at Wimbledon, and Dances to be held in the course of the season. Get to know one another, and you will find training a still greater pleasure, that constrained feeling of new members in the dressing room will soon vanish.

I have arranged a rather full programme for this season; several Socials are included, but don't forget THE DANCE at Wandsworth Town Hall on the 30th of this month. Dances are not usually a financial success, but with the membership we have, they should pay for themselves

As the new Hon. Sec. of this section, I am anxious to make a big success of the Club's social side, and look for your hearty support. Suggestions for new social activities likely to find favour in the Club are asked for especially.

Don't forget the Dance—November 30th, 1928, at Wandsworth Town Hall.

H. L. ROLLINS, Social Hon. Sec.

Column · page 13

SPORTS JOTTINGS

By " Observer."

In the Club's recent Three Mile Road Race, it was noticed that the system of starting the men off their marks was not satisfactory. There was the inevitable encroaching on to higher marks, with the result that some gained, while the men further back in the handicap had to suffer. I think that next year we should use some patent starting trap, say in the form of a pen into which the men file as their names are called. Talking of traps, here is surely a fine opportunity for some budding inventor to invent a patent starting scheme for sprinters. Picture a trap on similar lines to which the greyhounds use; men on their marks, into the trap, set, then up goes the flap and they are away to a guaranteed level start. Anyone attempting a flier would get a nasty crack on the head, so I don't think you would find many of the flying school in them. Well, here it is, and if anybody wishes to make use of this suggestion to the A.A.A., they are at perfect liberty to do so.

The question of collecting money, due from members, for the Gazette is, I understand, causing our worthy Editor no little inconvenience. It is the practice of a very well known walking club to issue a copy of their Gazette free to all its members, a practice which I would like to see operating in our own Club, but then their subscription is 10/6 per annum, whereas ours in only 6/-. So we arrive at this point: would it not be better to increase our subscription and so have a free Gazette? After all, the latter is a necessary part of any athletic club and I think our members will all agree that our own wants some beating in this respect. One point is clear · it costs money to produce this journal, and while members are so slack about paying for their issues, the whole thing is becoming a financial burden to the Club. Therefore my alternative is to raise the annual subscription by 4/- which is the cost of a year's Gazettes to each member. Under the new subscription they would receive their bulletins gratis.

The Inter-Club Race at Feltham saw our leading men finishing consecutively from 3rd position down to 10th. Very nice to see, but with one disappointing feature: some of them were not wearing Club vests and so, instead of an unbroken line of claret and gold, one saw the line broken by blue and black, and white vests. I should like to see our members wearing Club vests in all races, whether Inter-Club or Opens, and so bring the good old claret and gold well before the public eye. Vests can be had on application to our genial Treasurer, Albert Macher, and I trust that members will accept this hint in the spirit in which it is given.

The question of my identity appears to be causing a few people some sleepless nights. I feel very flattered, I assure you, but really think they could make better use of their writing abilities, for even if my identity is discovered, what useful purpose would it serve? However, if my readers really want a clue, one of Edgar Wallace's own particular brand, here it is :Columbia Record No 4942, and you will know my guilty secret.

With the Road Walking season now commenced in earnest, I shall from time to time include some of our heel and toe exponents in my series of short biographies. My next three include, foremost, one of the old brigade of the running world and one who has fought many stirring battles with the stars of the past, including the ex-mile record holder, Joe Binks —

G. Still, now in the veteran stage, but still as keen as ever, and moreover still respected by our Handicapper in Club races over the country. George, who is a life member of the Highgate Harriers, had a distinguished career while with the Gatebearers; he was in their winning National teams of 1904 and 1905, finishing 16th and 19th respectively. He has, however, been with us for many years now, and is always willing to help the younger members along, and I am sure that if George could be persuaded to write his reminiscences, they would make very interesting reading.

W. G. Dodkin, used to be well-known as a runner, being in the winning team of the Sussex Junior race while a member of Brighton and County H. He has not experienced the best of luck while with our Club, being troubled by illness, and he has now forsaken the running game and has joined the walkers. Shews great promise, and has gained a few awards in his new sphere, he should be successful in Club and Open Handicaps before the season ends.

L. Storey, a young member of our walking section, and one who has shewn wonderful improvement under, so I am told, the guidance of that old diehard, Bill Fish. Storey, after finishing second in the Club Novices' Walk, caught the Handicapper napping and credited himself with 2 wins and a 3rd in successive races. He should do well during the winter, although, being rather young, he might with profit to himself, take things steady until next summer; a remark which applies to several of our younger members, both runners and walkers.

LIFE! (A True Story). (Continued)

P.N K

Just before Christmas of 1918 (which I remember very well, on account of the number of empty rum jars scattered in the snow throughout the village) the foreman in charge of sand-bag filling gave us the so-called "piece-job" This meant we had a certain amount of work to do in allotted time; the sooner we finished it, the sooner we went on our holiday. So far, so good; but certain bright spirits devised an original labour-saving plan As you know, the earth for the bags was frozen, but here was snow just as hard and much easier to get. So into our "sand" bags went about 50 per cent of snow, and we finished our 6 days' job in 3½ days—then holidays! In the spring I went to look at these sand walls—just as well the Bolsheviks never came!

Christmas over, I optimistically turned out again, only to be told that the blockhouse work was finished and that I must unload sledges. This meant carrying endless loads of provisions and ammunition all day, supervised this time, not by an easy-going Russian foreman, but by armed soldiers.

Here, again, I got under the influence of certain labour economists. After turning up in the morning and doing about an hour's work, we used to slip away, get into an empty barge by the river and play cards till about 5 p.m., when we got back and reported attendance! In those days I could teach our train excursionists something about gambling

However, this was too good to last, and, sure enough, soon we were told that in future only local labour would be employed. This was the beginning of a very lean time for me, as no work meant no cigarettes, and no cigarettes meant no food. Having nothing to barter, I set out, like Alexander, in search of fresh worlds to explore, if not to conquer

About 3 miles away from the village, along the river bank, lay 6 ice-bound barges, on which were quartered men and officers of the R F C. (now the R A.F.) and there I directed my footsteps one morning in January. On the deck of the last barge a thick-set man, with a red face adorned with bristling moustaches, was splitting firewood. There was nothing remarkable in his occupation, but what amazed me was the flow of profanity which accompanied his work, to which obviously he was not used. Taking my courage in both hands, up I went by the steps

"What do you want? Be off," he shouted, demonstrating his meaning by an eloquent motion of the boot.

And, really, I must have looked particularly Bolshevistic in my ill-gotten fur coat and Russian felt boots. But I was not so easily frightened and, pointing to the pile of wood on the deck, I said in my best English, "Chop 'em. Come on?"

"Come on" was a nick-name given by Russian workmen to English soldiers for their endless exhortations.

With evident relief, he handed me the pole-axe, and in quite a short time I had finished his wood for him. Pleased, he took me to the kitchen and dished me a huge plateful of oatmeal. Now, at the risk of offending our Social Secretary (who provides our Club dinners) I must vote that porridge as my most enjoyed meal After this, as it was getting dark, I went home, but not before my red-moustached friend had secured a promise from me to come again next day to help him with his work.

After about a week of wood-splitting, we went together to the Commandant's base, where I achieved my ambition of "joining up" in the so-called Labour Battalion, consisting for the most part of men and boys too young or otherwise unsuitable for active service.

The very first night, walking on air, I went to my diggings, where my landlady had nearly given me up for lost. So, in my new uniform, shortened in most parts, I sat at the table, a guest of honour; all the simple souls asking me if I had a rifle.

"No," I said carelessly, "but I can have one for the asking, as my friend is an officer; for hasn't he two stripes on his coat-sleeve?"

But I must confess that my work was anything but easy. Here is the programme · up at 5 a m, in pitch darkness, light the kitchen fire, and put some stone-hard white bread to thaw; men's breakfast at 6-30 consisting of bacon, tea and biscuits. A privileged few, including Corporal Preston, the cook and myself had white bread—for with true philosophy the corporal said that as there wasn't enough to go round, we might as well have it all!

After breakfast we had general parade in the snow, then most of the men went off to the aerodrome, and I got on with my wood-splitting, interrupted only by fierce guerilla with the night sentries, who, strangely enough wanted to sleep. In these arguments I was outclassed, but Corporal Preston always came to my rescue. I might mention that Corporal Preston had seen many years of service at sea He also kept a public-house at Bristol; so the enemy used to retire hastily before his eloquence.

After this I had to fill up many water urns. For water I had to go some 100 yards out in the river, as nearer than that to the shore it was frozen to the bottom I invariably filled the buckets too full, with the result that each evening I had to be thawed out of my trousers

In the evening I had my daily lesson in English as, sneaking to the men's quarters (I was none too welcome here, owing to my wood chopping operations) I listened to their talk and songs

CORRESPONDENCE.

H. AND I. CAPPER.

Tatt's Ring, Newmarket.

To the Editor of The Belgravian 17th October, 1928.

Dear Sir,

Although speculation as to the identity of " Observer " has been rife amongst your readers for some while past, we must confess that whilst, as Turf Commission Agents, we found his paragraphs interesting and pertinent, we were but mildly bitten with curiosity concerning him until yesterday, when your October issue reached us. Then, when anxiously pondering over the newly-arisen query, "Whoever can 'Tipster' be?" we remembered the suggestion made by one of our most respected clients in a previous issue of The Belgravian respecting "Observer," and immediately the thought flashed into our minds: "Why! 'Tipster' is surely 'Observer,' but the poor dear old lady must have either lost his glasses or taken several more than are good for him!"

We sincerely hope that this suspicion is correct, since there is one alternative that is by no means pleasing. For is it not possible that in "Tipster" you have amongst you one who contemplates " making a book" when the Ten and Twenty Miles Walking Championships are decided; and who hopes, by dwelling in your Magazine upon the very real brilliance of a great many of your stars, that those of you who like to indulge in an occasional flutter may be induced to overlook other stars, that threaten to out-shine the ones whose dazzling has been commented upon?

Before now herrings have been drawn across the trail by astute old foxes who were desirous of throwing dust into the eyes of their pursuers, leading them up the garden, and fleecing them to a cinder. (Our meaning, we believe, is quite clear, Mr. Editor; so please pardon us if, in our agitation, we mix our metaphors).

Should this prove to be the case, and should the identity of "Tipster" be established, all who do business with him would do well to first make a point of inquiring what odds he is prepared to lay against each of those who, he states, "Should fight out the Championships between them." If in the "Murphy Shield" race he quotes very short prices regarding Tommy Green, Harold King, R. D. McMullen and J. G. Scamell, he will be left with practically no choice but to give tens at the very least against a mere sorry cab-ranker, such as A. L. Fletcher.

Then, when the distination of the "Ricketts Shield" is to be settled upon, if "Tipster" did not welsh during the earlier race, he should be given the opportunity of quoting short prices concerning Green, McMullen, Cooper and King. When he has done so ,the would-be better should be able to procure very favourable terms if his idle fancy prompts him to back a horse which is, presumably, but a negligible creature when judged according to its achievements (does not "Tipster" wholly disregard it?) but which nevertheless appeals to him strongly, simply because in "Fred Rickards" it has a name which strikes him as being extraordinarily "winsome." He will, at least, have good reason to expect longer odds against these two horses from "Tipster" than he is at all likely to obtain from

Yours faithfully, The Reliable "Old Firm" of H. AND I. CAPPER.

A REPLY TO " H."

H. PARKER.

3, Artillery Row Buildings, S.W.1.

Dear H,

I am rather surprised that you, whilst complaining of the anonymity of "Observer," should yourself hide behind a nom de plume. Why not come out into the open?

I myself, with many others, am intrigued as to the identity of "Observer." I am not "Observer." (And perhaps our old friend Nobby Clark put the reason into a nutshell when he told me I had not the brains. Perhaps he is "Observer?" Who knows?) Neither do I know who "Observer" is (as such)

As you suggest, he—or she—certainly seems to know quite a lot about running, and in going through "Observer's" notes, it has struck me that he knew more about Middlesex or North of Thames items of interest, than Surrey or South of Thames; therefore I have been looking amongst our recently-acquired members for this elusive individual. For I think he is a new-comer to the Club, and any old stager who knows the people about whom he has written in his potted biographies, would understand.

I at one time thought he would be found as a frequent visitor to the track at the Duke of York's Then again, in "Observer's" notes in the September Magazine he gives us quite a lot of detail about a small meeting at Rayleigh on August Bank Holiday. This meeting, as far as I am aware, was not reported through the usual sporting channels. So how did he get his details of the happenings? Did he compete? Was he a visitor? Did he hear it in gossip. It certainly looks as if "Observer" almost unmasks himself. Perhaps this will help you to unravel the mystery.

At one time I was looking for "Observer" amongst the flowers, but I have since altered my opinion, and will take a walk along the road to the cherry orchard, and see what luck I have!

Yours sincerely, H. PARKER.
