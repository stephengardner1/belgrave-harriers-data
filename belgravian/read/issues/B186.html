Club Honours Board

Clive Shippen (Past President)

Dear Belgravian,

Following A.A.Harley's untimely death last year your Committee considered various proposals for commemmorating the unique service rendered to the Club by this fine Belgravian. It was eventually decided that a Club Honours Board featuring Senior Championship successes was not only something that a club of our stature merited anyway, but it would be particularly appropriate for carrying a plaque in memory of Alf. who took such an interest in the history of our club and its many team successes.

During Alf Harley's 46 years of membership he was twice President, Honorary Secretary for 26 years, Editor for 9 years and Walking Secretary for 10 years, and thousands of Belgravians enjoyed the benefits of his selfless devotion to the Club. There are many, I am sure, who would wish to be associated with this project by contributing to the cost of the board (approximately £270).

The fund has already been launched by the Past Presidents, Vice-Presidents and Life Members of the Club but its success depends also on General membership. Subscribers will be notified of the unveiling date and acknowledged in the December "Belgravian".

Your donation, however small, will be gratefully received by:-

Clive Shippen (Past President) 24, Fryston Avenue, Coulsdon, Surrey.

Editorial · page 3

COMMENT

At last Battersea Park athletic track can boast some all-weather surfacing. Well, on the jumping areas at least. The long jump run-up was put down by Dunlop and the high jump area has been laid with a substance developed in the United States and completely new to this country. In the few weeks that these areas have been in use there has already been a noticeable increase in the standard of jumping and in the number of those who wish to jump at Battersea track.

There appear to be no plans afoot to resurface the entire track or to enhance the pole vault and throwing areas. Apparently there is also a problem in safely housing the high jump landing bed, for at the present the only vandal proof store is on the other side of the Park, and this means that it is not always available for use. All this is a great pity because the whole south-western sector of London is very much a barren wasteland as far as the athlete is concerned. He or she must travel to West London Stadium or the Crystal Palace to use the most modern athletic facilities. On the credit side, the groundsman at Battersea has intimated that during the winter months when he is less busy he will cut down the remains of the old pole vault 'mound'(at present inhabited by a nest of rats), so that at least that site will be ready should any developments arise.

On a slightly different tack, a TV programme some weeks ago highlighted the problems faced by youngsters in south-west London. The main point made was that there is nothing for them to do and nowhere for them to go, not even for a kick-about with a football. The fact that a large park exists in Battersea with football pitches, tennis courts and an athletic track, not to mention a fun-fair, riverside embankment and gardens, seemed to be totally ignored by the programme. However, it must be admitted that these amenities are not available on winter evenings, and if a scheme could be got off the ground to develop a sports centre that would be open all year round in all weathers, a great deal would have been done to alleviate the problem. It would certainly also give athletics the chance to exploit an area of London that is virtually untapped.

Our sport has a great advantage over others in that it can be practised by almost anyone who has the determination to get up and have a go. Bear in mind the incredible performance of the man at our league meeting at Exeter who, with only one leg, won the high jump with a leap of 6'0"! Whatever one's sex or shape or size, there is an athletic event to be tackled and even if one is never likely to become a world-beater there is the pleasure of measuring improvements, inch by inch and second by second.

Given the facilities athletics can be a real glamour sport, attracting many more than the comparative few who now take part.

• TED STIMPSON recently underwent an operation in Wimbledon Hospital and is now out and about again. He was pleased to see a number of Belgravians who visited him during his short stay in hospital. Ted, a Past-President, is one of those who do a great deal for us, lending a hand at track, country and road fixtures. He also plays a large part on our social side and for many years has organised the Club's Annual Dinner - an event which few, if any, clubs can equal. In 1939 he led our cross-country team home in the "National" at Manchester to score our second team win (the first was in 1935), and as fourth man home gained an international vest. After war service he gained another E.C.C.U. winning team medal at Sheffield in 1948. Over recent years he has enjoyed a weekly spin over the Common, and is a regular attender at Committee meetings; he also represents us on the Middlesex County A.A.A. Committee.

• RICHARD MOODY, now a Surgeon Lieutenant in the Royal Navy, is off on an enviable tour of duty this winter. He sails from Plymouth on September 17th on board HMS Achilles, a Leander Class frigate, as Medical Officer for a group of ships. Ports of call will be Gibralta - where he hopes to take part in the race to the top of the rock, Cape Town, Mombasa, Mauritius - for Christmas, and Singapore. Richard will be back in the UK next summer.

CHAS. MANNING, President.

.... is probably the word I'm looking for - it's this that makes administration so much easier. It's when someone goes beyond the line of duty, often without being asked, to put themselves out to help our Club. I'm not referring to the Carters, Biscoes, Bakers and Couzens of our Club who frequently receive the accolades they deserve, but to the man often overshadowed. Someone like Pete Sutton who sweeps out the canteen and committee room, someone like Joe McDonald who at the hint of a suggestion is so eager to please, or Fred Paget washing up and running a bar. And talking of bars - Dennis MacManus, Jack Carter and Stuart Barrett - we couldn't have done without them. These are not just words, without them we could have put up the shutters as far as socials were concerned. Brian 'T' Gorman and his efforts have given heart to our training from the Hall. Colin Pearson, an active athlete, has rolled up his sleeves to the most foul but necessary cleaning work. Arthur Penstone, always busy - Bert Stroud with his wife and kids, whether it's in the canteen or standing on corners directing runners for four hours at a stretch and still smiling. To mention one lady makes others immediately spring to mind. Pat Mead, never an observer, always involved, the track people know how much and all who read this magazine owe her thanks. Evelyn Stimpson, mainspring in so many ventures, grafting away with needle and thread or paintbrushes or pottery, forever helpful and understanding. Margaret McMillan and her great efforts at the Bazaar - Sue Coy - Mary Crookes and Stephanie Langheim - have all pitched in with their positive contributions. Gwen Couzens and Bill's Mum at the Jumble Sales. My own Mum, not Yidisha but a continuous source of lucrative ideas and help. And Linda, my bossy daughter, who as I write is making papier mache piggy banks for the Merton Show - her involvement with tea making, distributing leaflets, selling programmes, running raffles, etc., is total.

I thank you all - missed some? Of course. The bloke what never made a mistake never made anything. To those, please accept my apologies. And to you all, cited or not, thank you - thank you very much.

SPOTLIGHT on .......... STEPHEN WHITE

Steve White became a member of Belgrave Harriers as a 15 year old in 1965, introduced by Tony Brooks. Immediately, those connected with the track and field side could not help being impressed by the slim youngster who trained with purpose from the very beginning, who seemed to know exactly what he was about, and who exuded the calm confidence of a future champion.

Nearly a decade later, Steve could be said to be the man at the heart of our team. It's not just that he nearly always wins for us, it's the way he does it with assurance and flowing style, perhaps three or four times during an afternoon. He lives at Oxford and in spite of the travelling involved, always turns out for the Club in league and cup matches. On one occasion this year he had gone down to Brighton for the weekend yet still visited Mayesbrook Park in East London to spearhead our team. Another time, representative honours had been gained by him but there was no easing up for the event as he staunchly carried the claret and gold for us just the day before.

Representative honours include the A.A.A., Southern Counties A.A.A., Surrey A.A.A., Berks/Bucks/Northants/Oxon., and he gained a junior international vest in matches against France and West Germany in 1969. Steve was 3rd in the English Schools senior 200 metres in 1968, 2nd in the junior A.A.A. 200 metres championship in 1969 and a finalist in the senior A.A.A. 200 metres in 1973. He has sprinted faster than any other Belgravian and only John Mitchell has leapt further. Honours are not confined to the sports field, however. At Tulse Hill School he was Deputy Head Boy and has been President of Oxford University Athletic Club.

The following questions were put to Steve recently by Alan Mead and give some insight to the motivation of this quiet, great Belgravian.

Alan: What made you choose athletics as your main sport? Steve: I suppose I enjoyed the personal success of athletics. It was good to win at football of course but there it's a team success, athletics gave me a lot of personal confidence. Also, I preferred the loneliness of the training, it seemed more purposeful and there was a greater sense of progress than in the team games. This is not to say I spurned company, not that I didn't enjoy team games. It was just that if I was going to take something seriously then I had to devote all my self-centred attention to it; football etc., was an enjoyment, athletics became more fundamental. There was, moreover, the sheer aesthetic pleasure of running well and being fit.

A: Are any other members of your family involved in sport? S: I think my father used to play football in the Army, otherwise not.

A: How often did you train when you started running and jumping? S: I started at primary school when I was 9 but that didn't involve much training, I just ran when the district sports came along. I can remember going to Sunday morning training at Tooting Bec prior to the local cub pack's sports - we really were very keen. I didn't start training seriously until I was 14 - about 5 days a week.

A: What other sports did you take part in at Tulse Hill School? S: Soccer, basketball, cricket for my first two years until athletics began to intrude.

A: A basketball player I once encountered said you were known as "wonderboy" as you were a good all-rounder. S: It makes me sound like some "All American Boy" cartoon character. I hope nobody called me this, but I certainly was actively involved in three major sports, captain in two of them (athletics and basketball) and particularly successful in athletics, and my successes were well paraded by the school authorities. I suppose it did make me popular and it meant that I got to know a great many people whom I would never normally have spoken to, and they me. For a great many boys at Tulse Hill prowess at sport was more admirable than any other ability.

A: Did you receive any coaching at school? S: I did receive the regular coaching from school masters until I had some success and showed some promise. I was then singled out for more privileged attention and given more detailed schedules and so on. Then, when I joined Belgrave, there was Dave Ansell who was one of the finest long jump coaches in the country. (I think Derek Cole and Alan Lerwill have missed him since he went North - we all owe a lot to Dave). Then in my fourth year at school, Dick Wyer arrived to teach P.E. and he has been friend and 'coach' ever since.

A: Where did you train at that time? S: At school, where gymnasium and weights facilities were excellent, plenty of encouragement too; Brockwell Park and Herne Hill track.

A: How much of your early success was due to sheer ability? S: I honestly don't know because I was training relatively seriously relatively early and therefore never permitted myself to rely for long on natural ability alone. However, I did win races at primary school, at district level and at holiday camps. I suppose actually that natural ability was important and also a contributory factor in my choice of athletics as my main sport. I've tended to be a bit philosophical in my answer to your first question - sheer natural ability was important.

A: What ambitions did you have in those days? S: In the short run to win the English Schools (I'm still upset that I never did); to win a junior international vest; to pass my exams ('O' and 'A' levels) - this above all else. In the long run to win the Olympics, of course! and to go to University.

A: What were your achievements in sport in your Tulse Hill days? S: Early on in soccer; in my second year we won the Ewell Trophy and London Grammar School Cup (we were the best team in London that year). I was playing for a Sunday club side which perennially won league and cup. Until I started to play in the 1st eleven, I honestly think the team I was playing in lost no more than 10 matches. That probably had little to do with me but it was marvellous to play in such successful teams. In basketball I played for London Schools. In athletics I won the London Schools Intermediate long jump in 1967, 3rd in the English Schools senior 200 metres 1968, 2nd A.A.A. junior 200 metres, junior internationals against France and West Germany 1969.

A: When did you go up to Oxford and what were you reading? S: October 1970; Politics, Philosophy and Economics.

A: How did you make your mark in the athletics team? S: I actually went up to Oxford assuming I would have no trouble getting into the athletics team and quite honestly I didn't; the standards were not particularly high and anybody who had success at school was eagerly welcomed.

A: When did you become President of Oxford University Athletic Club? S: In 1973 for academic year 1973-74. My term ended in May this year after the Varsity match. Julian Goater is the present President. I was Hon. Sec. in 1971-72 and have been on the committee in one way or another ever since.

A: You graduated in 1973, how is your post graduate course going? S: Fairly laboriously at present. I spent much too much time being President of O.U.A.C. last year and now spend hours in the library catching up and trying to find a thread for a thesis. I must admit I'm looking forward to finishing and leaving the academic environment.

A: What do you hope to do after this? S: "Something in the City". Actually I really don't know specifically, but probably something allied to my subject (Economics). Perhaps Government Economic Service or Bank of England. I do fancy working for the U.N. or something like that but they usually require experience in business or government first. I really don't relish the prospect of becoming an urban commuter with mortgages and gas bills. Perhaps I'll work for 'Shelter' or 'Child Poverty Action" - the irony of that would be that I probably couldn't afford to find accommodation myself. This might all sound a little flippant but I'm not too concerned about my future (I think my wife's more concerned!) and I'm confident I'll do something worthwhile and enjoyable.

A: When did you meet your wife? S: I met Jean in 1968. We were married in 1972.

A: How does Jean view your athletic career? S: With benign tolerance.

A: What moments stand out in your athletics? S: My worst moment is being knocked out of the English Schools 200 metres in 1969 when I had the fastest time in the country to date and was favourite - I really wanted to win that.

I was really pleased on beating Cambridge by a record margin this year, not because the Varsity Match is a prestigious match nowadays, not primarily because of anything I did personally, but because it was such a magnificent team performance and there was a real sense of club spirit. That was a wonderful reward for all the tribulations I experienced as President throughout the year. Other than this I felt totally elated to break 22 seconds for the first time and 24 feet and also 50 seconds for 400 metres. I've run abroad for Lambeth, Great Britain and O.U.A.C. and I've enjoyed every trip.

A: Have you met many athletic "personalities"? S: David Hemery once in the showers at Crystal Palace. Alan Pascoe is a "good bloke". Both he and Hemery, I've noticed, are prepared to give encouragement to youngsters, to interrupt their own sessions to say a few words. I really admire such humility in such greatness. But I have met many people in athletics and quite honestly, whether they've been "personalities" or not hasn't really been significant.

A: Do you prefer jumping or sprinting? S: It depends whether I am jumping or sprinting well. Though I do find it more difficult to train for jumping because of lack of company and coaching at Oxford; it's easier to get out and run.

A: Have you realised your athletic ambitions? S: I do worry about improving and I do still want to obtain an international vest. But I'm not obsessed with this particular ambition. I will do all I can to achieve it but if I fail I will find satisfaction in the knowledge that I have tried rather than disappointment in failure. More important is to enjoy athletics here and now, and I do, so I have no reason to complain. Doing the O.U.A.C's President's job made me approach the sport from a new direction, and as I have said, creating a club spirit and helping the rest of the team gave me a great deal of enjoyment and satisfaction. I'm sure it is the most worthwhile thing I have done at Oxford.

A: How long will you continue to compete? S: For as long as I enjoy it. I suspect that when I start work, it might be a case of for as long as I can find the time.

A: Would you be satisfied with competing at a lower level? S: I often wonder whether my enjoyment of athletics derives from my success. I believe it might. In which case if I cease to be successful I shall cease to enjoy athletics and therefore pack it up. However, my criteria for success might change, I might regard myself as successful if I am fit at 30 and so continue to do athletics even though I am no longer winning. I hope I shall do some sport when I am older. I'm certainly not doing it for the glory and nothing else.

It's 1969 and Steve White, fastest junior in Britain with 21.[illegible], wins the Southern Counties A.A.A. junior 200 m. in 21.8. Photo: Ron Linstead.

SPOTLIGHT ON STEVE WHITE Continued..........

A: How often do you train and what type of training do you do? S: Six days per week, winter and summer. Weights three times per week in winter, twice per week in spring and once in summer. I get coaching/advice from Dick Wyer, otherwise I just work things out for myself. I train mainly at Iffley Road track and sports hall or on college playing fields. My sessions are very flexible and varied to avoid boredom. I usually try to concentrate on one particular aspect of technique per session, leg drive, arms or whatever. I do repetition work over 60, 70, 120, 150, 200, 300 metres and always try to keep the quality high, even in snow. This isn't easy but I prefer This approach to doing, say 10 x 300 metres with 30 seconds recovery in about 45 seconds. Anything above 38 seconds for a 300 metres is poor for me. I do a lot of supplementary work, stretching, hopping, bounding, weighted belts, etc. In fact, I spend one whole session a week doing this sort of thing in the winter. Weights: I mix pyramid work, in which I go for maximums (215lbs. clean, 210lbs. bench press, 600lbs squat), with light, fast repetitions in order to facilitate speed of contraction and avoid feeling cumbersome. In general I try to fulfil set tasks but never by strain and unnecessary pain. Relaxation is fundamental, enjoyment is absolutely essential. I couldn't become an athletic metronome. I like to think that what I am doing requires some thought and some grace.

A: Do you play any other sport now? S: This year no. Last year I played football but at present athletics is my sole sport.

A: What other interests do you have? S: Theatre, Literature, the papers, dinners with friends, weekends with friends, a few pints, my wife (not in that order'.).

A: Do you have any regrets about being so involved in athletics? S: Believe it or not I've always taken pains to avoid being involved in athletics to the exclusion of all else. So athletics is just one, albeit important, of the things I do. I don't regret any part of my athletics career. It has enabled me to meet many people, it's given me self-confidence, self-control and so on, and of course I've enjoyed the glory and respect of others.

A: What are your views on Belgrave Harriers? S: I think Belgrave's first priority must be to win the Southern League and gain promotion to the National League. This has become an increasingly difficult task and will continue to become more difficult. We shall, if we are not careful, become a club firmly entrenched in the Southern League with little hope of improvement. This will arise because good athletes, and promising athletes, will be attracted to the better competition they will find in the National League. The situation is therefore cumulative, the existing strong clubs will grow stronger and the provincial league clubs, competing with these National League teams in recruitment, will grow weaker until some equilibrium position is reached. The final result might be that clubs relegated from the National League will be too strong for provincial league clubs and therefore will be promoted back to the National League the following year; the population of National League clubs therefore remains fairly stable over a time. I wouldn't like to generalise too much in this respect. There are and will be provincial league clubs who will rise to National League standard and prosper. As examples of these we have Borough of Enfield, Essex Beagles, Metropolitan Police, Oxford City A.C., in the future perhaps Windsor, Slough and Eton and Haringey. But these clubs all have special characteristics not possessed by Belgrave: (i) a single readily identifiable area from which to recruit, very often with no competing clubs in the same area (Oxford City, Metropolitan Police, Windsor, Slough and Eton), (ii) a tartan track which has provided a focal point to the club, which is why I rate the prospects of Haringey and Windsor rather highly. Belgrave, on the other hand, has a catchment area which stretches over the whole of South London and competes for recruitment with many other South London clubs. Although Belgrave Hall provides some sort of focal point it benefits mainly cross country and road runners and anyway its attraction must be weakened by having club members so diversified geographically. This makes it difficult for a large percentage of the club to get to and train at the Hall at the same time. I would therefore include Belgrave (potentially, if not yet) with clubs like Herne Hill Harriers, South London Harriers, Surrey A.C., Queens Park Harriers, Hercules-Wimbledon. All these clubs hardly inspire hope for the future and appear, if anything, to be on the wane as far as competitive results are concerned. I am informed that Belgrave has the athletes - if only they would turn out'. - I have been told this since the Southern League was formed. Belgrave, I was assured, were still a great club, just wait till next year, and if only so many athletes weren't on holiday or in the army and posted elsewhere, or else just couldn't be bothered, we would win the Southern League easily. I think we are still deluding ourselves. In the last Belgravian we were congratulating ourselves for being the best club in Surrey. How can we honestly regard this as a success when we still haven't won the Southern League. And if we do regard this as a success then I think our standards are ashamedly low; how can we, in all conscience, encourage a promising youngster to join us when all we can offer is success in Surrey, surely if we are interested in his development we would rather prefer to divert him to Thames Valley Harriers or Croydon Harriers or Enfield where he would find better competition. I think that if we are still potentially National League calibre, all those holidaymakers and couldn't be bothered athletes should be pressed to turn out now, because if we wait we will not even have the expectation of good results, the current athlete will have retired and the good youngster will have gone elsewhere. But if the malaise is more fundamental, as it probably is, then we must look for more fundamental solutions. I would favour amalgamations. There are too many clubs in South London. Talent is diluted too thinly and consequently good athletes are condemned to Southern League competition indefinitely. I think that serious consideration should be given to a more rational athletics set-up in South London so that we can eliminate petty squabbles between clubs about who uses which track and who has prior claim on a local youngster. I appreciate that there will be serious opposition to such changes. Many of the South London clubs are the oldest in the country and have long and admired traditions. Also Belgrave Harriers has one of the finest social set ups of any athletic club with its dances and socials and club magazine. I recognise all this and would hate to see it jeopardised. Moreover, the Southern League isn't such a bad league. In some ways, it is preferable to the National League as you compete against different clubs each time out and the atmosphere is more relaxed. Again I accept this and have personally enjoyed the Southern League a great deal. However, unless there is some attempt to win the league and to get into the National League one of the most fundamental drives of athletics is denied - the will to win. If this is missing in Belgrave, and we don't want to get into the National League, then we might as well become a successful social club and forget about athletics. Finally, if we did have bigger clubs and a Belgrave team in the National League, it would still be possible by some adjustment to the existing rules, to run 'B' sides in the provincial leagues. Thus you have a club catering for all levels of ability and providing a means for the athlete who ceases to be good enough for the National League to continue with his athletics with the same club at a lower level. I am sorry for the length of this answer, but it really has been frustrating getting nowhere in the Southern League over the years; I'm afraid nothing less than winning or coming second will give us access to the National League. The alternative is not encouraging.

ROAD AND COUNTRY

GORDON BISCOE Road Running and Cross Country Secretary.

As the records now show, our cross country and road performances last season were not very exciting to say the least. Second place in the Surrey C.C.Champs. and the performance in our own 12 stage road relay being probably our proudest boast. The prize for this competition will be to the value of £10 and of the winners own choosing. Other prizes may be awarded as is felt necessary. It should be noted that this competition in no way affects handicap prizes or medals etc awarded for any individual Club championship.

May I request that all cross country and road runners help to make this scheme a success but more than this, that in the coming season they will endeavour to make Belgrave a success, especially when selected for representative teams. (Special dispensation will be arranged for those gaining international representation!!)

ROAD RUNNING AND CROSS COUNTRY POINTS COMPETITION.

Points will be scored as follows:

1. In any designated inter-club match open to all members, a) the first 12 members to finish - 6 points. b) the next 12 members i.e. 13 - 24 - 5 points. c) the next 12 members i.e. 25 - 36 - 4 points. d) the remainder who complete the course - 3 points.

2. In any Club championship, a) members whose positions in the championship are within the first third of the total number of starters. - 5 points. b) members within the second third - 4 points. c) the remainder who complete the course - 3 points.

3. All members actually coming under starters orders in the races designated, irrespective of whether finishing or not, will score 1 point.

4. For acting as a recognised official, course marker or pointsman - 2 points.

The competition is open to all junior and senior members, both first and second claim. In the case of road relays, points will be awarded as in (2) above except that times will be considered instead of positions. In the event of any dispute the decision of the Belgrave Committee will be final.

HEROIC RUN BY JOHN BAKER

ARM

John Baker, our 50 year old Hon. Treasurer, has been the butt of many a jest regarding his un-athletic appearance. Looking like an emaciated version of West German international Harald Norpoth, he lurks near the back of our road and country races, eagerly waiting to pounce like death upon any runner who should get within his range.

The writer will remember to his last days the terror that struck him one murky Saturday in mid-winter. In the gathering dusk on the last lap of the Club '10' he entered the woods with all strength rapidly failing and John Baker behind in full cry. The wail of the banshees could be heard, the harsh panting of tortured lungs getting closer and closer ... until ... he could struggle no more, and he too fell prey to the grey ghost of Belgrave.

We in Belgrave know that beneath John's fragile frame burns a determination that is matchless but one can well imagine the disbelief expressed by his fellow employees at Esso - "What, you a runner!" .

Well, they are regarding John with awesome respect now. You see, a sponsored run/walk was organised by the Esso folk, to raise money to buy a kidney machine for Charing Cross Hospital. John had no problem in gaining sponsors, for after all, surely a bloke like him wouldn't last a mile!

The event took place around Richmond Park, a favourite haunt of our hero. After 30 magnificent miles he wanted to go on and on but Mrs. Veronica Charles, one of the organizers, insisted that John called it a day. He was tired but was in no trouble with his health and doubtless he could have gone on all week. No, it was his poor sponsors that needed protection. They had already been skinned to the tune of £138 by John's efforts and if someone hadn't stepped in they would be working off their debt for another year!

One sour footnote was that one of his sponsors refused to pay up on the grounds that he just did not believe it.

Great work John.

SEPTEMBER 10th 1949

In the London to Brighton walk on September 10th, eleven of our walkers started off on the stroke of Big Ben at seven o'clock, to be joined later by a number of supporters. In the early stages, Jack Bidgood Bert Clayton, Jack Baker, Jack Wakeley and George Oliver, walked in a group with Don Heath and Joe Wilson not far away. Gordon Doubleday, F.Herring, Sid Spencer and Bill Urry followed at an easier pace.

Jack Bidgood eventually went away from the others and at Chequers Horley was in 9th position. A check at Crawley (29¾ miles) showed that our men had held their positions with the exception of Jack Wakeley who had stopped for a massage. By the time Bolney was reached, Jack Bidgood had improved his position to 7th, Clayton was 16th, Baker 21st and Wilson 27th. With the retirement of Bert Martineau from the Surrey team there seemed a chance that our team would be placed and although Jack Bidgood dropped a couple of places to go back to 9th, Joe Wilson moved up to 23rd and at the finish our positions were: Jack Bidgood 9th; Bert Clayton 16th; Joe Wilson 21st; Jack Baker 23rd making a score of 62 points and just losing us the third position. So Jack Bidgood became our long-distance champion as well as holder of the 20 miles Championship. He's a champion to be proud of. His judgement of pace and unflagging stamina throughout the race set a fine example. Bert Clayton was a worthy second and showed his grit in overcoming tiredness in the last few miles. Joe Wilson walked his best Brighton to date and came along in fine style in the latter half to finish remarkably fresh. Jack Baker pluckily stuck it out over the last half to complete the team.

YOUNG ATHLETES

The Youths Medal Competition was neatly sewn up by Peter Crosbie who warily steered clear of the long track races but scored heavily in the field and sprint events to pick up the maximum 100 points for five events. Paul Warren and Danny Richards provided the other medallists with Adrian Cook and David Didino not competing in the necessary five events but nevertheless putting up some good performances.

This year's Boys Competition for the Mrs. L. Ryan Cup turned out to be the most exciting on record. With no less than 45 boys taking part it was a major achievement to score in the necessary five events.

When the day for the last three events arrived everything was set for a close finish. Mark Shippen, the leader after five events, was unable to improve any more as he was away on holiday; Martin Munro who scored max. points on the first two events and was then unable to attend for the next three was back in contention; Simon Shanks had scored well in three events but now desperately needed two more good scores; Ivan Beaumont, older than most of the others and therefore suffering with a lot less handicap allowance; all were pushing for the lead.

The 1,500 metres was Simon's strong point and he scored well, finishing just behind Tony Pilcher and new member J.Heath. Ivan Beaumont leapt a tremendous 15'10" to win the long jump outright and also gained 2nd in the high jump but was it enough? All the time Martin Munro was creeping up with really solid all-round performances.

Working out all the scores for such a close competition was a real headache and after checking and re-checking, Simon Shanks was declared the winner, only 1½ points ahead of Martin Munro who was in turn 5½ up on Ivan Beaumont. The medal for the best score outside the first three in all eight events was won by Stephen Fletcher with a tough all-round performance and not so far behind him came Andrew Byrne who won a special medal. Andrew has improved dramatically in the last twelve months, proving that no matter how far behind you are now, determination plus the strength you gain each year can make all the difference. The losers today can be tomorrow's winners.

A final word of congratulation must go to those mighty "micro-colts" who competed so doggedly against boys considerably older than themselves - David Hicks, David Worley, Andrew Vallance and not forgetting Philip Headley who at eight years old and with only the same allowance as twelve year olds finished a magnificent 4th in the 1,500 metres with an actual time of 5 minutes 48 seconds!

MEET ANTHONY PILCHER

A youngster who has started his athletics with a bang is 12 year old Tony Pilcher. Tony has only been running for a couple of months, having joined the Club after a visit to one of our jumble sales, but already he has set new figures for the junior boys 800 metres and has equalled the 1,500 metre record.

His best 800 metre run came in the Brighton young athletes meeting where he burst from the pack with well over half a lap to go and then kept it up to the finish to win in 2:31.4. This scorching finish has become his trademark and it is a surprise to hear him say, "I don't like 800 metres, it's too far. I really like the 100 metres."

Tony goes to Bushey School at Raynes Park. He gets plenty of sporting encouragement at home from his father, Reg., a keen veteran cyclist who competes for the 29th Wheelers based in Tooting. Reg. has become a most welcome helper when it comes to measuring long jumps etc.

At the moment Tony trains once a week on Sundays at Wimbledon Park. This coming winter he hopes to attend the Friday night sessions at Belgrave Hall as well as continuing his other sporting interest - football. He plays little league football for the Barclay Savers and Oakway teams.

CLUB CHAMPIONSHIPS

The return of the Belgrave Club Championships to their historical home of Battersea Park coincided with the all-weather jumping surfaces at that venue becoming available for the first time. A fair number of senior athletes took part, but the most pleasing thing was to see the ranks of youngsters who keenly contested the boys and colts events with heats necessary in both age groups for the 100 metres. So often boys events are held separately from senior competitions, but mixing with the old'ns and simply competing at the same track on the same afternoon seemed to raise their enjoyment of the proceedings. The organisation ran as sweetly as ever and we must thank our visiting officials - Harry Hathway, 'Tommy' Thomas, Mrs Nightingale and Mr and Mrs Long - who did great work in the timekeeping department.

The jumping events produced some excellent performances. Outstanding was the triple jumping of 14 year old Ivan Beaumont who gained 2nd in the senior competition with a distance that beat his own boys record. (Here it must be noted that the following week at Battersea he pulverised the long jump record as well. It had stood since 1961!) Reg. Hopkins, having spent a fortune by entering practically every event, had an exciting duel in the long jump with Snowy Brooks, with the former just getting the verdict with a personal best leap. He also won the high jump, a mere centimetre below the championship best. The junior high jump gave David Didino the chance to launch himself to a new height of 1.67m and Richard Danpure, a new signing, set a championship best in winning the boys event. Philip Clay smashed the previous junior boys club record by 7 centimetres in this event. Richard completed an unusual double by taking his 800 metre race as well. Yes, those jumping areas are going to be a real boon.

Colin Pearson seems to be the eternal runner-up. He has run in Club Championships from 800 metres to 20 miles, always giving his all, but he has never quite managed to make off with the winners "pot". This year's 800 metres was reckoned to be wide open, although Peter Carton, simply on past performances, was favourite. Could Colin do it? Well, he certainly tried, and maybe he was a shade ahead as he attacked Pete down the final straight but as they crossed the line with Colin wildly lunging forward it was Peter who got the decision. Colin turned the tables on him in the 1,500 metres by producing a do or die burst with a full lap to go. Peter had to give way but Laurie O'Hara was clearly untroubled by such tactics.

Alan Black was in a class of his own in the steeplechase although Leo Coy, reducing his week old best by another 30 seconds, is improving fast these days and looks certain to produce a sub - 10 minute run in the not so distant future.

Mark Shippen won a close boys javelin competition with 3rd placer Stephen W. Crosbie setting a new junior boys record. A titanic struggle took place in the senior javelin between John Martin, the holder, and Snowy Brooks. John must have been fairly sure of keeping his hold on the trophy because he hadn't bothered to clean it up. Snowy managed to wrest it away but John had the last laugh when he won the discus after Snowy produced six no throws!

Snowy admitted later that his start was "a bit naughty" in the 100 metres. He held Charlie Gillett off by a tenth with young Reg. breaking new ground with an 11.6 for third. Charlie later stamped his authority on the 200 and 400 metre fields with a couple of good times considering the dusty condition of the track. Philip Gee added a worthy 2nd in the one lap sprint to his earlier 800 metre third place, and Paul Williamson reaped some of the benefits of consistant training with his effort in the 400 metres. At the start of this season, his first, he clocked 56.6 for the event. He is now down to 52.6.

As shown on this issue's cover, David Bailey led home the field in the senior boys' sprint with some hot opposition breathing down his neck. New member James Heath took the colts' race.

TRACK AND FIELD

What a let down! After finishing 3rd and 4th in the Southern League in 1972 and 1973 respectively, our final place this year was a mediocre 11th (out of 25 clubs). We won our opening fixture but the results of the next three meetings looked like we were aiming for arithmetic progression (or perhaps I mean regression!) - we finished 2nd, 3rd and 4th. Our decline was partly arrested by gaining third place in the penultimate fixture but we finished the season at Wimbledon Park with a 4th place.

In common with many other clubs we do not have an unlimited depth of talent, especially in middle distance events and, therefore, injuries to one or two key men (which has happened this year) are really felt. Life is not made easier by a number of members whose variety of excuses never cease to amaze and depress me.

I only hope that 1974 was a bad year and that everyone will make an effort to support whoever takes on the mantle of Track Secretary in 1975. Division 1 of the Southern League is extremely competitive and it would not take much more of a decline to make Division 2 a real possibility in 1976.

After all that I would like to thank those who have made my job easier by their reliability and willingness to compete. Unfortunately space does not permit me to list them all but Steve White (who travels from Oxford to compete for the club) and Charlie Gillett deserve a mention for their efforts; neither ever seems to have a bad run.

New names have, of course, been seen in the Belgrave team this year; Martin Scott, who after competing very successfully at the beginning of the season unfortunately pulled a muscle (see you next season), Paul Williamson, who has steadily improved his 400 metres time and is now almost a regular in the 4 x 400 metres relay team, Leo Coy, who has found his niche in the team at 3,000 metres Steeplechase. He has only run this event three times but has improved from 10m.44secs. to 10m.5.8secs., and finally, Michael Manning, who although still a Junior has turned out in a couple of 5,000 metres league races and has run very well.

PHILIP GEE Track Secretary

The proposed 'Alf. Harley' club honours board John Walker writes:-

JOHN WALKER

Dear Editor,

Belgrave Harriers. Quo vadis. Is Belgrave Harriers dying? No. Then why the proposed memorial board? I have seen the cap in hand circular and I believe the sum mentioned, 350 nicker (minimum!) should be spent in a much wiser fashion.

Now, contrary to what some wildly deranged Belgravian may think I aint no lecturer! Either in Big Hall or Belgrave Hall! I feel that something should be done to shove some plasma back into the Bels and that can be done only one way. Not with a board, but with flesh and blood. Youngsters. Here I must pay tribute to men who since the Bels formation have done great work administratively. If it is felt by some that one such person should be especially remembered what better than that his name and memory be given to an event - close to his heart - the tribute being no less sincere than as I understand is being contemplated, by a memorial board.

There is another way to remember the history of Belgrave. How about printing, say, 500 copies of the Bels history, with photos, and selling them? In this way a permanent reference could always be to hand in many homes. I realise a lot of work would be involved but it surely is practical and when the books are sold the club would benefit.

Another venture which I'd support would be to encourage youngsters - early teenagers for example by taking parties of lads on regular training weekends by coach somewhere. Also, I believe, as I understand Bill Laws does, that Belgrave should advertise more than at present. Space should be bought in various newspapers and other media.

Getting back to saving money. Why have medals? Incidentally this aint sour grapes! I know I aint won many. I am serious in the medals suggestion. Today engraving and medals are very expensive.

One other thing. I don't believe our Treasurer is that old that he can't take out several novices on a training run. Naturally I mean male novice runners.

As for the Club Honours Board itself, the cost is now expected to be £80 less than the estimated sum and it is my hope that Club funds will not have to find any of the anticipated £270. The response from Past Presidents, Vice-Presidents and Life Members to my request for them to launch this fund was marvellous and well over half sent in letters applauding the decision.

In the course of a year several hundreds of visitors from all spheres come to our Headquarters and on many occasions I have noticed individuals studying with interest the Ken O'Kell boards listing Past Presidents and Internationals. We cannot expect all outsiders to be fully aware of our history but there is a good chance that many future visitors will leave Belgrave Hall with a better knowledge of our great record.

And it is of that great record that Alf Harley was terribly proud. So much so that he spent a great deal of time collecting together all the information he could find, with a view to writing the book that John Walker would like to see. It was never started, but we discussed it and pooled our researches. I hope to make that book reality, but if it took Blackheath six years to write theirs, ours can hardly be done quicker! As for selling 500 copies and making some money for the Club, my own experience of "The Belgravian" and "Veteris" leads me to doubt whether this could be regarded as a money spinning venture.

Now to the youngsters. John suggests that we shove some plasma back into the Bels. Surely he must have seen the Young Athletes section of the June "Belgravian"? There seemed to be plenty of plasma amongst the 50-odd colts, boys and youths photographed or mentioned in that issue - and as our coach trip to Brighton showed, they can produce some useful results in open competition too. Reg Hopkins and Bill Couzens are principally responsible for the healthy state of our young membership, and although they get some help they could do with a lot more. I am sure that they will both be delighted to hear that John supports this type of venture. Perhaps they will hear from you John. Oh! And by the way - see you at the A.G.M.!

Bel's king of the mountains

John Blair-Fish is still running well over the fells up North. Early season efforts included the Camethy Hill Race where he finished 19th out of 98 competitors over the 9 mile/1,600 feet course in 57 mins. 6 secs., and the Manx Mountain Marathon - which sounds a real killer as it traverses 30 miles/9,000feet - in which he was 12th from 51 in 6hrs. 15mins. in spite of losing three-quarters of an hour and climbing an extra 1,000 feet through an error in navigation.

During the summer he tackled eight more of these 'superman events' in Wales and Scotland, gradually improving his average position, over distances varying from 3 to 17 miles, with climbs being as much as 7,500 feet. (Makes the 'kiss of death' on our 5¾ mile race look a bit weak doesn't it.) A particularly good performance was his 4th in the Ben Lomond 9miles/3,000feet where he crossed the line 4th in 1hr. 15mins.

John has cut back in training in the last few weeks to concentrate on the Ben Nevis race on September 7th. He was 40th last year and looks forward to improving on that.

Good luck to you John. We hope to see you running down South again some day.

John Blair-Fish
